In short

Stasis offers a way to locate the issue a discussion must address. Ask about facts, classification, seriousness or justification, and action or procedure. The categories help expose disagreement hidden inside a broad policy question. They do not force inquiry into a rigid order or guarantee consensus. A useful application identifies where evidence or reasons could change the assessment.

Layer 1Re-learn

Four questions about one proposal

Imagine a neighbourhood discussing a wall painted without permission. Some residents call it vandalism; others call it a community mural. Before deciding its fate, distinguish what happened from how it should be classified, what merits or harms it has, and who should decide what happens next.

The positions

Locating the dispute

Issue Question in the fictional case What would help?
Fact Who painted the wall, and what permission existed? Records and testimony about the event.
Definition Does the relevant rule classify this as unauthorised alteration? The rule, its scope, and its application.
Quality Is removal justified despite the mural’s benefits? Reasons about damage, consent, value and alternatives.
Action Who can decide, and should it be removed or retained? Authority, procedure and feasible options.

Purdue OWL presents this four-question approach as a writing and inquiry tool, using fact, definition, quality and policy. Its historical introduction connects the tradition with Greek and Roman rhetoric. The table adapts that modern teaching account; it is not a translation of an ancient legal classification.

Find where the positions meet

Suppose everyone agrees that no permission was obtained, but one participant thinks that settles removal while another believes later approval is possible. Repeating evidence about the absence of permission will not address the remaining dispute. The bridge concerns the rule’s consequences and the available remedies.

Conversely, agreement that consent matters can leave a factual disagreement about whether it was given. Here, finding the owner’s actual decision may do more work than extending an abstract debate about public art.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Use a sequence without making it a barrier

Factual clarification often helps later assessment, but evaluative concerns can guide which facts deserve investigation. The seriousness of irreversible damage might justify examining removal options before every historical detail is known. Likewise, the policy question can reveal that a previously disputed detail makes no difference to any feasible action.

Hitchcock’s account of critical thinking allows a spiral process in which a question is redefined and components recur. Applied here, that flexibility prevents a useful organising device from becoming an instruction to suspend all practical deliberation until complete factual agreement exists.1

Classification needs a standard

The term vandalism may describe a legal category, a moral accusation or an expression of dislike. Ask which is being used. A statutory definition needs interpretation of the relevant rule; a moral assessment needs reasons about rights and harms. Agreement on a word can conceal disagreement on those standards.

Purdue’s account includes questions about what influences a definition, how seriousness is judged, and whose interests are affected. Those questions extend the method beyond merely assigning names to stages.2

The fourth category in the modern OWL presentation is policy. The chapter also includes jurisdiction and procedure, which should be distinguished from the merits of the chosen action. A fair process can produce a poor substantive decision, and a promising proposal can come from someone lacking authority to implement it. Keeping both visible makes the application clearer.

See classifying disagreement for a finer division, and burden of proof for who needs to support a challenged claim.
