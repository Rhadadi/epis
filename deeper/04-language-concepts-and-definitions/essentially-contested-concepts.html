In short

Some concepts are argued over not because people are confused but because the concepts are evaluative, complex and open, so that reasonable people weight their parts differently: democracy, justice, art. The most useful tool for such debates is the distinction between a concept and its conceptions. People can share a concept while holding rival conceptions of it, which tells them what they actually disagree about.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Concept and conceptions

A clear example is critical thinking itself. “Its definition is contested, but the competing definitions can be understood as differing conceptions of the same basic concept: careful thinking directed to a goal.” The conceptions differ in “the scope of such thinking, the type of goal, the criteria and norms for thinking carefully, and the thinking components on which they focus”.

2. Contested concepts are usually thick

Calling something democratic or just praises it. Such concepts combine description and evaluation, like the thick concepts studied in ethics. And thick concepts often carry “culturally specific information”: “Different societies might have sufficiently different understandings of courage to have distinct concepts of it”.

3. Many classifications are possible

Locke already denied that there is “a single classification of things in nature”: there are many, “each of which might be particularly useful depending on one’s purposes”.

Common confusions

  • If a concept is contested, it is empty. The critical-thinking case shows otherwise: the shared concept anchors the debate, and the rival conceptions can be compared, for example by asking what goals they serve.

Sources for this part: Hitchcock 2024, preamble; Väyrynen 2026, preamble; Väyrynen 2026, § 1; Uzgalis 2024, § 2.3.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Contested epistemic concepts. If different cultures have different concepts of justification, “On what basis, then, are we to compare and evaluate these differing practices?” Stich asks whether preferring our own concept would be “just ... a form of epistemic prejudice”1. Justification itself may be a contested concept. Compare Internalism and externalism.
  • From contest to engineering. One response to permanent contest is to ask which conception we should adopt, given the purposes the concept serves2. See Conceptual engineering.
