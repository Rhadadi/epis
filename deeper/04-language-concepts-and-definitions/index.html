Deeper study · Chapter 4

Language, Concepts, and Definitions

For when a section didn't click, or left you wanting more: each page explains the idea again, tells the full story, fills in what the chapter leaves out, and gives the sources.

Pieter Bruegel the Elder, The Tower of Babel, 1563

← Back to Chapter 4

  • Short study · 3 minWhy language is an epistemic issueWe think, argue and learn in words, so the faults of words become faults of thought. Bacon called these faults the idols of the market, the most troublesome of his four idols, because “words react upon the understanding”.
  • Short study · 2 minUse and mentionYou use a word to talk about the world; you mention it to talk about the word. Quotation marks are the usual signal.
  • Full study · 9 minSense and reference“Mark Twain is Mark Twain” tells you nothing; “Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens” can be news. Yet both are about one man.
  • Short study · 3 minKinds of definition“Definition” covers very different activities: a lexicographer reporting usage, a chemist discovering what gold is, a mathematician laying down a new term, a philosopher trying to say what knowledge is. They answer to different standards.
  • Short study · 3 minRules for good definitionsThe textbook rules for definitions (not too broad, not too narrow, not circular, and so on) go back to Aristotle’s requirement that a definition apply to exactly the things defined and capture what they essentially are. Modern logic adds two sharper requirements for stipulated definitions: they must not let you prove anything new, and they must settle how the new term is used.
  • Short study · 3 minNecessary and sufficient conditions and their limitsThe classical theory says a concept is a definition in disguise: BACHELOR is UNMARRIED plus MAN, and anything with both falls under it. That picture explains a great deal, and it underwrites philosophy’s oldest method, testing definitions against counterexamples.
  • Short study · 2 minFamily resemblance and prototypesIf concepts are not definitions, what are they? Wittgenstein’s answer was a network of overlapping similarities, like resemblances in a family.
  • Short study · 3 minAmbiguityA word or sentence is ambiguous when it has more than one meaning, and an argument that slides between the meanings can look valid when it is not. Ambiguity is easy to confuse with three neighbours: vagueness (blurry boundaries), generality (one meaning covering several kinds of case) and context-sensitivity.
  • Full study · 9 minVagueness and the sorites paradoxOne grain of sand is not a heap, and adding one grain never seems to make the difference; yet a million grains are a heap. The sorites paradox has been argued over since the fourth century BCE, and every solution costs something.
  • Short study · 2 minVerbal disputesA dispute is merely verbal when the parties agree on the facts and differ only in how they use a word. The diagnosis is old: Bacon complained that the “great and solemn disputes of learned men often terminate in controversies about words and names”, and James dissolved an argument about a squirrel by separating two senses of “go round”.
  • Short study · 2 minLoaded language, euphemism, and framingThe same facts can be described so as to push a listener toward different conclusions. Experiments show how strong the effect is: in Tversky and Kahneman’s disease problem, describing identical outcomes as lives saved or lives lost reverses most people’s choice.
  • Short study · 2 minEssentially contested conceptsSome concepts are argued over not because people are confused but because the concepts are evaluative, complex and open, so that reasonable people weight their parts differently: democracy, justice, art. The most useful tool for such debates is the distinction between a concept and its conceptions.
  • Short study · 2 minThick and thin conceptsThin concepts like good and wrong evaluate without saying much about what a thing is like. Thick concepts like cruel, generous and gullible do both at once.
  • Short study · 2 minFacts, values, and the is-ought gapHume noticed moralists sliding from “is” to “ought” without explanation. On the standard reading this became “Hume’s law”: no evaluative conclusion follows validly from purely factual premises.
  • Short study · 2 minThought experiments and intuitionsA thought experiment sets up a scenario in imagination, lets it run, and draws a conclusion, from Lucretius’s spear thrown at the edge of the universe to Gettier’s cases. Thought experiments can be powerful and are also fallible: the spear argument fails once we see that space can be finite without an edge.
  • Short study · 2 minReflective equilibriumReflective equilibrium is the method of adjusting principles and judgments about cases against each other until they fit. Rawls named it; Goodman described the same procedure for rules of inference.
  • Short study · 2 minConceptual engineeringConceptual engineering asks not what our concepts are but what they should be. The idea is older than the name: Carnap called it explication, and saw philosophy itself as “a kind of conceptual engineering” for the language of science.
