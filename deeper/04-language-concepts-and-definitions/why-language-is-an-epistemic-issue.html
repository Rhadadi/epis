Why language is an epistemic issue

Going deeper on a section of “Language, Concepts, and Definitions”.

In short

We think, argue and learn in words, so the faults of words become faults of thought. Bacon called these faults the idols of the market, the most troublesome of his four idols, because “words react upon the understanding”. Locke gave a whole book of his Essay to language because classification, and so knowledge, runs through general words. Twentieth-century philosophers went further and recast many philosophical claims as claims about language.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Bacon: words push back

“Men imagine that their reason governs words, while, in fact, words react upon the understanding”. Words “are generally formed in a popular sense”, and when “a more acute understanding” tries to draw finer lines, “words oppose it”. That is why Bacon thought the idols of the market “the most troublesome of all”.

2. Two ways words mislead

Bacon distinguished two kinds. Some words name “things which have no existence”, such as Fortune or “the element of fire”; these are easily removed by refuting the theories that spawned them. Others name real things but are “confused, badly defined, and hastily and irregularly abstracted”. His example is “moist”, which means at least eight different things, so that “in one sense flame is moist, in another air is not moist, in another fine powder is moist”.

3. Locke: knowledge runs through general words

“Locke devotes Book III of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding to language”, a sign that he thought “issues about language were of considerable importance in attaining knowledge”. “Without general terms and classes we would be faced with the impossible task of trying to know a vast world of particulars”.

Common confusions

  • Precise definitions solve the problem. Bacon recommended definitions, “imitating the caution of mathematicians”, but warned that they “cannot remedy the evil in natural and material objects, because they consist themselves of words, and these words produce others”. His remedy was to go back to “particular instances”.

Sources for this part: Bacon 1902, I.59; Bacon 1902, I.60; Uzgalis 2024, § 2.3.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Many good classifications. Locke rejected the idea that “there is a single classification of things in nature that the natural philosopher should seek to discover”. There are many ways to classify the world, “each of which might be particularly useful depending on one’s purposes”, and “There are always borderline cases”1. This is the root of the chapter’s later sections on vagueness and verbal disputes.
  • Words and ideas. Locke’s “main semantic thesis” was that words “signify nothing but the ideas in the mind of him that uses them”. Mill objected that saying “the sun is the cause of the day” is not a claim about ideas; Kretzmann argues that Locke meant ideas give words their meaning, not their reference2. Compare Sense and reference.
  • The linguistic turn. The logical empiricists recast “what had been seen as philosophical claims” as “proposals for structuring the language of science”, so that the choice among them became “a practical decision about usefulness for certain purposes rather than a theoretical question”3.

What the chapter simplifies

  • The Bacon quotation. The chapter quotes a different translation. Devey’s 1902 version says that “words are formed at the will of the generality, and there arises from a bad and unapt formation of words a wonderful obstruction to the mind”4.
