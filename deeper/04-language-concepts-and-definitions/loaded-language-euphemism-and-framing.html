Loaded language, euphemism, and framing

In short

The same facts can be described so as to push a listener toward different conclusions. Experiments show how strong the effect is: in Tversky and Kahneman’s disease problem, describing identical outcomes as lives saved or lives lost reverses most people’s choice. Philosophers have long catalogued the techniques, from Bentham’s tactics for turning attention “from the measure to the man” to the evaluative charge carried by thick words. The defence is to restate a claim in neutral terms and see whether it still persuades.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Framing changes choices

In the “Asian disease” problem, program A will save 200 people; program B has a one-third chance of saving 600 and a two-thirds chance of saving no one. “A majority of respondents (72 percent) chose option (a), whereas a majority of respondents (78 percent) shown an equivalent reformulation of the problem in terms of the number of people who would die rather than survive chose (b)”. A meta-analysis found that “the framing condition accounts for most of the variance”.

2. Evaluation hides in description

Words like selfish or reckless both describe and evaluate. Some philosophers think the evaluation is attached only by use, so that such words are “loaded words” rather than special concepts; Stevenson’s 1938 work on persuasive definitions is the classic treatment.

3. Bentham’s catalogue

Jeremy Bentham’s Handbook of Political Fallacies (1824) described about thirty tactics used against reform. His “vituperative fallacies” share “the endeavour to draw aside attention from the measure to the man, in such a way as to cause the latter’s badness to be imputed to the measure he supports”.

Common confusions

  • Framing effects prove people are irrational. Wheeler cautions that “It is rash to conclude that people, rather than the peculiarities of the task or the theoretical tools available to us at the moment, are in error”. Even how people read the conditional “If program A is adopted, 200 people will be saved” is not fully understood.

Sources for this part: Wheeler 2024, § 5.1; Väyrynen 2026, preamble; Väyrynen 2026, § 2.1; Hansen 2024, § 2.6.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Words that name nothing. Bacon’s first kind of verbal idol is names “of things which have no existence”, such as Fortune1. Some political labels work the same way: they name a threat or a group that does not exist as described.
  • Neutral uses. One can use a loaded term “in some non-evaluative sense”, marked by “scare quotes or a certain tone of voice”2. Quoting the other side’s vocabulary is not the same as adopting it.
