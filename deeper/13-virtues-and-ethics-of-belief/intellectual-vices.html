In short

An intellectual vice obstructs good inquiry or the handling of knowledge. It can involve a character trait, an attitude, or a way of thinking. A mistaken answer alone does not identify a vice. Diagnosis needs an account of the mechanism and its setting. Improving that mechanism can matter more than deciding whom to blame.

Layer 1Re-learn

From a mistake to a pattern

Imagine a fictional editor who rejects corrections because acknowledging them would damage his image. The immediate error is a false date. The deeper obstacle is his way of handling correction. Another editor might print the same false date after carefully checking an unreliable archive. Identical errors need not reveal identical character.

Vice epistemology investigates epistemic defects in traits, attitudes, and thinking. Cassam’s approach, as surveyed by Turri and colleagues, includes distinctions among character vices, bad attitudes, and defective styles of thinking. It does not require every vice to be a stable trait of a wholly vicious person.

This matters for familiar labels. Arrogance can concern one’s standing relative to other people; closed-mindedness can concern how one handles alternatives; insouciance can concern a lack of care about inquiry. Before applying any label, say what the person did and how it obstructed the epistemic task. A disagreement with you does not itself provide that explanation.

Why success can conceal a defect

Suppose the fictional editor happens to be correct in another dispute despite refusing to read the correction. The correct outcome does not vindicate that refusal. Conversely, a conscientious investigation can fail. Evaluating an intellectual practice involves its relation to inquiry and correction over relevant circumstances, not simply the last answer it delivered.

Clifford makes a related historical point: the shipowner’s failure to investigate remains culpable even in a version where the ship completes its voyage safely. His moral argument concerns how the conviction was acquired. That is an illustration of evaluating belief formation, rather than a complete modern theory of vice.

Sources for this part: Turri et al. 2021, § 10.2; Clifford 1879, pp. 177–180.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Responsibility is a further question

A disposition can obstruct inquiry without having been freely chosen. Cassam’s examples distinguish voluntary malevolence from vices such as insouciance that need not be voluntary. We should therefore separate whether something is a vice, whether someone is blameworthy for acquiring it, and whether they can now address it.1

The same separation helps with a fictional workplace where questions are punished. Silence could reflect arrogance, indifference, realistic fear, or lack of access to information. The visible behaviour underdetermines the diagnosis. A person’s role and available alternatives belong in the explanation.

The surrounding practice

Situationist challenges raise questions about how broadly stable intellectual traits explain behaviour. Replies appeal to narrower dispositions, aspirational virtues, and environments that support good cognition. Those debates warn against treating one observed performance as a complete personality profile.2

For the editor, a correction process that records objections, assigns a second reviewer, and preserves a visible amendment history could interrupt the obstruction. That proposal is an illustration, not an empirical claim that any particular procedure reliably reforms character. Its rationale is transparent: it gives a correction a route into the publication that the editor’s pride cannot easily block.

These issues connect to epistemic injustice. Prejudiced credibility judgments can harm other knowers and inquiry together, but individual virtue is not a complete substitute for structural repair. The virtue literature also records criticisms of placing the burden of correction on a marginalised speaker.3

Where to go next

Start with the virtues-and-vices discussion in Turri, Alfano, and Greco. It supplies the secondary account of Cassam and the qualifications about responsibility used here. Read Clifford’s original shipowner passage alongside it to distinguish his moral argument from contemporary vice epistemology.4
