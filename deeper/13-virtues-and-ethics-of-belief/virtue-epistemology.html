In short

Virtue epistemology begins with the capacities and character of knowers. Some theories explain knowledge through reliable competences; others study traits that make inquiry go well. A correct answer can be lucky rather than a competent achievement. Good character also needs an environment in which criticism, learning, and correction are possible. The field contains competing projects rather than one formula for knowledge.

Layer 1Re-learn

A correct answer and a good performance

Imagine two fictional surveyors finding the same boundary. One checks the plans, uses the instrument competently, and notices an inconsistency. The other points at random and happens to be right. Both answers are correct. The difference concerns how correctness came about, and whether it reflects an ability exercised in the relevant conditions.

Virtue epistemologists use that difference to investigate knowledge and epistemic value. Reliable perception, memory, and reasoning can count as intellectual virtues in one strand of the field. Another strand emphasises character traits such as conscientiousness, courage, humility, and openness. The contrast is useful but not an exhaustive division: some theories connect capacities and character.

An attitude and an achievement

The first surveyor could be conscientious yet misled by a defective instrument. The second could possess good instruments yet refuse to check an obvious anomaly. Character, competence, conditions, and outcome are distinguishable. Calling someone virtuous cannot replace asking how those elements contributed to the particular judgment.

Some virtue theories analyse knowledge; others aim to regulate inquiry, explain epistemic character, or improve intellectual life. These projects need not offer the same definition of a virtue or the same solution to lucky true belief.

Sources for this part: Turri et al. 2021, § 3, 5; Turri et al. 2021, § 4.

Layer 2The full story

An older vocabulary and a modern family of theories

Thinking about intellectual excellence predates contemporary virtue epistemology. Turri and colleagues trace relevant themes through classical and early modern philosophers and other traditions, including al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and Avicenna. These connections identify resources concerning cognition and the social transmission of knowledge; they do not establish that each predecessor held the same modern theory. A historical lineage can contain different aims and different conceptions of intellectual excellence. It is more informative to identify the shared question than to project an entire current taxonomy into every earlier text.1

The modern field developed around Sosa’s work on intellectual virtue and contributions by thinkers including Code, Montmarquet, and Zagzebski. The resulting approaches put agents, their powers, and their intellectual lives nearer the centre of epistemology. Some retained the traditional task of defining knowledge and justification. Others challenged a narrow focus on isolated propositional states and asked how people conduct inquiry, become responsible knowers, or participate in a community. That plurality explains why a debate over one analysis of knowledge cannot settle the prospects of every virtue approach.2

An older illustration of balanced inquiry appears in James. His observation concerns investigative interest and susceptibility to error, rather than Sosa’s later analysis of knowledge. It is useful precisely because it identifies a tension that a character approach must handle: motivation can sustain inquiry and also distort it. Being disinterested in the sense of having no care for a question is not obviously the ideal. Caring while remaining answerable to failure is a more demanding possibility.

Read the original

Interest with a check on deception

The most useful investigator, because the most sensitive observer, is always he whose eager interest in one side of the question is balanced by an equally keen nervousness lest he become deceived.

3. This public-domain passage is a historical illustration of investigative balance, not evidence that James anticipated every contemporary virtue theory.

Faculties and character

The conventional distinction is between virtue reliabilism and virtue responsibilism. Reliabilist approaches emphasise abilities that reliably lead to truth, including perceptual and cognitive faculties. Responsibilist approaches emphasise intellectual character: motivations and dispositions involved in responsible inquiry. Both descriptions need qualification. Some reliable competences are acquired rather than innate, while a character virtue may itself be understood partly in terms of successful epistemic functioning. The literature also records objections to drawing too sharp a boundary between the approaches.4

Return to the surveyor. Accurate visual perception of a mark might exhibit one competence. A willingness to ask whether the old map uses a different coordinate system might exhibit conscientiousness or openness. Neither makes the other unnecessary. Someone can faithfully apply a faulty procedure; someone with excellent perception can carelessly skip the relevant observation. The example explains why a complete account of good inquiry may involve more than one kind of excellence, even if a particular analysis selects one kind for its formal role.

Nor must intellectual and moral virtue coincide. An excellent ability to discover facts can serve a wrongful purpose. A morally well-intentioned person can investigate badly. The distinction does not mean the domains never interact; testimonial justice and other-regarding intellectual virtues make interaction explicit. It means that success in one assessment cannot be silently substituted for success in another. The virtue literature includes debates over how narrowly truth-directed intellectual virtues should be conceived and what other people’s epistemic interests contribute.5

Sosa’s performance model

Sosa’s account uses a performance analogy. A performance can be accurate, adroit, and apt. Accuracy concerns success; adroitness concerns competence; aptness concerns success because of competence. The archer who hits a target through skill differs from an unskilled shooter whose arrow is redirected onto it by luck. Applied to belief, truth provides the relevant success, while aptness connects that success with the believer’s competence. This relation is stronger than merely placing a true belief beside a generally reliable faculty.6

The argument, step by step

Why mere success is insufficient

  1. An answer can be correct through coincidence rather than competence.
  2. Knowledge should exclude at least relevant forms of such lucky correctness.
  3. Requiring correctness because of competence excludes successes for which the competence does not explain the result.
  4. Apt belief therefore offers a way to distinguish knowledge from merely lucky truth.
7. This is a reconstruction of the explanatory motivation, not a proof that every proposed account of aptness solves every luck case.

The phrase because of matters. Suppose the fictional surveyor reads the correct boundary from a display that has malfunctioned and happens to show it at this moment. Her ordinary competence at reading displays may still be excellent. Yet that competence did not secure the display’s current connection with the boundary. To decide whether her belief is apt, we must identify the relevant competence and its conditions of exercise. Calling reading generally reliable leaves the central explanatory question unanswered.

Conditions of competence and environmental luck

Virtue theories do not have to treat competence as a property that works equally well everywhere. The relevant conditions can involve the person’s constitution, the situation, and the environment. This allows a good account to distinguish a competent exercise from a superficially similar performance in conditions that disable the ability. But specifying these conditions creates a difficult balance: make them too broad, and luck slips through; make them too narrow, and every successful answer can acquire an artificially tailored competence.8

Environmental luck is particularly challenging. A person might identify the one genuine object in a setting filled with convincing imitations. The successful encounter can involve real perceptual ability, yet the surrounding alternatives raise doubts about knowledge. The virtue literature discusses fake-barn cases, different responses, and disagreements over their implications. It would be misleading to announce that the performance analogy alone has settled them. The analogy supplies a structure for a theory; the theory still owes an account of when the environment supports the competence.9

Compare the surveyor’s task with reading an old coordinate marker in a district where many markers were moved. If she happens upon the unchanged one, the question is not simply whether she can read numbers. It is whether the full route from the mark to the boundary constitutes a competent way of locating that boundary under these circumstances. The invented case illustrates the need to specify the task and conditions. It does not decide which formal version of safety or virtue is correct.

Why knowledgeable success might have special value

Virtue approaches also address the value of knowledge. A central challenge asks why knowledge is better than an equally useful true belief. If a reliable process adds value only because it normally produces truth, that value may appear redundant once truth is already present. The entry discusses this swamping problem and proposals that treat successful competence as an achievement with value beyond the outcome alone. Getting the answer through ability can matter in a way that merely landing on the answer does not.10

For the fictional surveyors, the correctly guessed boundary might settle today’s practical question. Still, one surveyor’s performance exhibits an achievement the guess lacks. We may value understanding how she reached it, the skill exercised, and the capacity to repeat appropriate work. These considerations are distinguishable: a present achievement and an ability useful in future tasks need not supply the same kind of value. A defender of achievement value must say why the performance matters even when its immediate product is matched.

There are complications. An achievement can be trivial or directed to an unworthy task. A difficult false belief does not become knowledge because it required effort. Conversely, easy competent perception can yield knowledge without dramatic struggle. Virtue theories must therefore distinguish epistemic achievement from exertion, drama, or moral merit. The literature’s debate over credit and achievement reflects these pressures; it is not an invitation to celebrate any laborious conviction as an intellectual accomplishment.11

Credit and the contribution of other people

Credit theories face an especially revealing testimony problem. Lackey’s Chicago visitor example concerns a person learning directions from a knowledgeable informant. The hearer can acquire knowledge without making the most significant intellectual contribution to the answer. If knowledge requires the believer to deserve primary credit for truth, ordinary testimonial knowledge seems threatened. The problem is not that the hearer contributes nothing. It is that the account may demand a kind or amount of credit that testimony does not normally provide.12

Replies distinguish kinds of credit, allow partial credit, or understand testimonial achievement through cooperation. Sosa and Greco offer different resources for handling dependence on others. These proposals raise questions about whether credit concerns praiseworthiness, attribution of success, or the explanatory contribution of an ability. A person may deserve little special praise for trusting a sensible answer while still exercising the competence relevant to knowing. Conflating these senses can make the theory look stronger or weaker than it is.13

Suppose our surveyor obtains a missing conversion rule from a specialist. She identifies the need, asks a relevant question, and uses the answer appropriately. The specialist supplies information she could not independently derive. A theory that treats learning from others as inherently inferior would miss the point of a cooperative inquiry. But an account that credits the hearer merely because the result is true risks making credulous uptake look virtuous. The challenge is to describe responsible dependence without requiring epistemic self-sufficiency.

Character as a guide to inquiry

Responsibilist and regulative approaches broaden the question beyond the definition of knowledge. They ask what intellectual life should be like: which motivations, habits, sensitivities, and relations help inquiry go well. Someone can possess many true beliefs while being incurious, evasive, or unable to learn from criticism. Conversely, an admirable inquiry can reach a false result because evidence was incomplete. Character assessment is therefore not reducible to a tally of correct answers, even though epistemic success remains relevant to the virtues’ point.14

Particular virtues require particular explanations. Intellectual courage involves worthwhile activity in the face of risk or difficulty; rashness is not simply more courage. Humility can concern recognising and owning limitations, freedom from concern with status, or appropriate uncertainty about one’s judgments. These accounts differ. Open-mindedness cannot be identified just with changing an answer, since change might reflect pressure instead of reasons. A catalogue becomes useful when it says which obstruction a virtue addresses and how appropriate judgment distinguishes it from nearby excesses or deficiencies.15

For the surveyor, perseverance could mean checking a stubborn discrepancy carefully. After the instruments prove defective, the same persistence with that method could become counterproductive. Courage could mean raising a justified objection before a senior colleague; taking a dangerous measurement without the needed training could be rash. These are proposed explanations of a fictional practice. They show why behaviour needs to be interpreted with its aim and circumstances, rather than suggesting that one external action uniquely reveals a person’s character.

Situationism and the stability of traits

Situationist criticism questions whether broad, stable character traits explain cognition as well as virtue theorists sometimes assume. Empirical findings concerning context, heuristics, and inconsistent behaviour motivate challenges; the precise implications are disputed. It is too strong to conclude that nobody possesses any intellectual virtue. It is equally too quick to ignore the challenge because virtues are desirable. The relevant questions include how broad a disposition is supposed to be, how often it should operate, and what evidence would demonstrate its presence.16

Responses take several forms. A theory can describe virtues as developmental ideals rather than common possessions. It can narrow the traits to appropriate domains. It can emphasise the environments and social supports through which good cognitive performance becomes possible. Other replies challenge the interpretation of the empirical results or the demand for general reliability. These are different strategies, with different costs. Narrowing a trait may improve its credibility while reducing its explanatory reach; treating virtue as an ideal may preserve a norm without supplying a description of ordinary behaviour.17

Consider a fictional survey office that protects correction through independent checking and written uncertainty notes. Such support may reduce the burden on a junior worker who would otherwise need unusual courage to report an anomaly. This does not make courage irrelevant. It changes what good conduct requires and how it can be sustained. A theory that describes only the worker’s personal disposition leaves out part of the mechanism through which the inquiry succeeds. Whether the proposed supports actually work in a particular office would require evidence beyond this illustration.

Testimonial justice and structural limits

Other-regarding virtues make the social dimension explicit. Testimonial justice aims to counter prejudice in credibility assessment. Yet the virtue literature also records objections to relying too heavily on individual hearers, and to placing corrective demands on marginalised speakers. A virtuous listener operates within practices that distribute speaking opportunities, credibility, and access to information. Improving the individual response can be valuable without repairing the whole structure that made the response necessary.18

The survey office might listen respectfully to a worker while excluding her from the records needed to establish the anomaly. In that case, respectful reception and access to inquiry are separate matters. Or it might invite objections but reward staff for finishing reports without visible disagreement. Such a setting helps explain why character alone is an incomplete institutional analysis. These fictional cases connect virtue epistemology with epistemic injustice and the distribution of opportunities to know, without treating every institutional defect as a personal vice.

What a virtue explanation owes

An adequate explanation should specify the epistemic task, the relevant capacity or trait, its conditions, and its contribution to success or inquiry. For a knowledge theory, this includes how competence excludes the relevant luck. For a character theory, it includes why the disposition is a virtue rather than an attractive personality description. For an institutional proposal, it includes the interaction between individual agency and environmental support. These are standards for assessing a proposal, not a single definition accepted by every virtue epistemologist.

The field’s plurality is a strength only when the projects remain explicit. A failure of one credit account does not refute every account of intellectual courage. A useful character ideal does not automatically solve Gettier cases. An environmentally supported practice does not show that the individual has acquired a stable trait. Keeping these claims apart allows the different approaches to contribute without borrowing conclusions that their own arguments have not earned. The main lesson of the surveyor example is explanatory: a correct result begins the evaluation, but it does not complete it.

For that reason, a practical assessment should preserve information about the route to the result. In the fictional office, the report can record the instruments used, the uncertainty that remains, the specialist consulted, and the objection that changed the conclusion. That record would make both competence and dependence visible. It would also prevent a later reader from attributing the whole success to one confident individual while losing the contributions of the people who supplied the missing information. This is a proposed way of explaining the achievement, not a claim that a written record alone guarantees intellectual virtue.
