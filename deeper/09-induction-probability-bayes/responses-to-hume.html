In short

Nearly three centuries of replies to Hume can be sorted by what they attack. Some look for an a priori argument after all: Kant’s synthetic a priori, inference to the best explanation, Laplace’s probability calculus. Some argue that the circle Hume found is harmless, or that there is no single principle for it to circle round. Others change the question, from “is induction justified?” to “is it the best policy we have?” Each reply teaches something, and most shift the problem rather than remove it. None persuades a sceptic who refuses to reason inductively in the first place, and several of their authors say that was never the aim.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Two horns, many exits

Hume’s argument is a dilemma: no a priori argument can show that the future will resemble the past, and any argument from experience assumes it. “Since Hume’s argument is a dilemma, there are two main ways to resist it”: tackle the first horn and find an a priori argument, or tackle the second and find an empirical argument that is not viciously circular. A third family accepts the dilemma but denies that justification needs what Hume asked for.

2. A circle that may not be vicious

“Induction has worked, so it will work” uses induction to support induction. Some philosophers reply that this is only “rule-circular”: it relies on a rule of inference “in order to reach the conclusion that that very rule is reliable”, without assuming its conclusion as a premise. The trouble is that “less sane inference rules such as counterinduction can support themselves in a similar fashion”.

3. A bet rather than a proof

Reichenbach did not try to show that induction will succeed. He argued that if any method can find the regularities of nature, induction will. His analogy is a fisherman in unknown waters: “Shall he cast his net? Well, if he wants to fish in that place, I should advise him to cast the net, to take the chance at least”.

4. No single principle

Hume assumed one Uniformity Principle behind all inductions. Critics point out that the future “only resembles the past in some respects, but not others”, and that each inference relies on its own specific assumption instead.

Common confusions

  • A good answer must convince the sceptic. Defenders of the inductive justification of induction concede that it cannot. As Papineau puts it, the argument is “not supposed to do very much”; what it offers is “a kind of important consistency check on our existing beliefs”.
  • Bayesian convergence answers Hume. Convergence theorems are real, but “in each instantiation, this convergence is relative to a particular specific prior”. The prior is where the assumption about uniformity goes in.
  • Popper did without induction. Popper’s deductive picture leaves open which unrefuted hypothesis to rely on. “We seem to need more than just deductive reasoning to support practical decision-making”.

Layer 2The full story

How the debate developed

Timeline

Replies to Hume

  • 1781 · Kant: synthetic a priori knowledge is possible1.
  • 1814 · Laplace’s “rule of succession”2.
  • 1878 · Peirce attacks Laplace’s rule and argues that induction is self-correcting3.
  • 1938 · Reichenbach’s pragmatic vindication4.
  • 1947 · Williams’s combinatorial argument, later developed by Stove (1986)5.
  • 1952 · Strawson’s ordinary-language dissolution6.
  • 1983 · Armstrong’s nomological-explanatory solution7.
  • 2003 · Norton’s material theory of induction8.
  • 2008 · Schurz’s meta-induction9.

First horn: an a priori argument after all

Kant denied the premise that a priori reasoning yields only relations of ideas. He “famously argued in response to Hume that such synthetic a priori knowledge is possible”, reversing the empiricist programme: experience itself “only comes about through the concepts or ‘categories’ of the understanding”10.

Inference to the best explanation. Armstrong, BonJour and Foster argue that a persistent regularity calls for explanation by a law, and that a law which holds everywhere and always is the best explanation. Armstrong: “To infer to the best explanation is part of what it is to be rational. If that is not rational, what is?”11. Two objections stand out. If induction is itself a kind of explanatory inference, the argument is circular after all. And why should a law that holds only for the observed region be a worse explanation? Foster’s reply is that such a law “is more mysterious, inherently more puzzling, than one which is temporally universal”12.

The probability calculus. Bayes and Laplace offered the first mathematical reply. Draw balls from an urn of unknown composition, put a uniform prior over the possible proportions, and the calculus delivers a prediction: having seen every draw come up white, the next will very probably be white too. This is Laplace’s “rule of succession”13.

Read the original

Laplace, A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities (1814), chapter III14

Thus we find that an event having occurred successively any number of times, the probability that it will happen again the next time is equal to this number increased by unity divided by the same number, increased by two units. Placing the most ancient epoch of history at five thousand years ago, or at 182623 days, and the sun having risen constantly in the interval at each revolution of twenty-four hours, it is a bet of 1826214 to one that it will rise again to-morrow. But this number is incomparably greater for him who, recognizing in the totality of phenomena the principal regulator of days and seasons, sees that nothing at the present moment can arrest the course of it.

The rule gives (n + 1)/(n + 2) after n successes. Laplace’s last sentence matters as much as the number: someone who knows the astronomy behind sunrise has far better grounds than the bare count. The contrast anticipates a concern of later material accounts: background facts can support an inference more strongly than a bare count. It does not make Laplace an advocate of Norton’s later theory. (The 1902 translation prints the number of days as 182623; five thousand years is about 1,826,213 days, which gives the odds Laplace states.)

The calculation looks like an a priori answer to Hume. It rests on three assumptions: the rules of probability, a model of the data as independent draws, and a prior. “There has been a persistent worry” that the urn model will not fit other cases of inductive inference, and the uniform prior relies on the principle of indifference, which can “give rise to inconsistent probability assignments” depending on how the possibilities are carved up15. “The quest for an a priori argument for the assignment of the prior has been largely abandoned”16.

Peirce made the objection sixty years after Laplace. If the rule is applied to someone who “had never heard of tides” and has seen the tide rise m times, it gives m + 1 over m + 2. Put m = 0 and it says there is an even chance of a “totally unknown event”. The urn arrangement, Peirce argues, can be likened to Nature only on “the principle that alternatives of which we know nothing must be considered as equally probable. But this principle, is absurd”17. Probabilities of different arrangements of Nature would make sense “if universes were as plenty as blackberries”, so that we could sample them18.

The later Bayesian tradition made the assumption explicit rather than removing it. De Finetti showed that if observations are “exchangeable” (their order does not matter), they behave as independent draws from an unknown proportion. Exchangeability “may be seen as a natural formalization of Hume’s assumption that the past resembles the future”19. The probabilistic framework, on this view, “has rather allowed us to clarify what could be meant by Hume’s claim that inductive inferences rely on the Uniformity Principle”20. Howson calls the result a “logical solution”: “Inductive reasoning is justified to the extent that it is sound, given appropriate premises”21.

The combinatorial argument. Williams and Stove argued that most large samples match their populations, so any given sample probably does. Hacking’s reply is that this trades on two senses of “high probability”. The theorem shows that the inference is “usually right”, not that it is “credible on each occasion of use”22. Getting from a sample to its population needs Bayes’s method, and so priors again.

Second horn: a circle that is acceptable, or no circle

Rule-circularity. Van Cleve and Papineau argue that “although premise-circularity is vicious, rule-circularity is not”23. Externalists add that a reliable rule can yield justified beliefs without the reasoner first showing it reliable. Lange puts the other defence: “all that we can reasonably demand of a fundamental form of reasoning is that it endorse itself”, and deduction is in the same position, as Lewis Carroll’s Achilles found when the Tortoise would not use modus ponens24. The counterinductivist is the standing objection. Counterinduction can argue from the premise that “Most CI arguments have been unsuccessful” to the conclusion that many of them succeed, so a mad rule vouches for itself just as well25.

No rules. Okasha and Norton deny that inductions share a common principle. Each rests on a specific empirical assumption, justified by another induction with a different assumption. “Hume’s argument rests on a quantifier shift fallacy”: for each inductive inference there is some presupposition, but there is no one presupposition shared by all26. The circle becomes a regress. Norton thinks the regress “benignly terminates”, and Okasha suggests “Perhaps infinite regresses are less bad than vicious circles after all”27. It is not clear, though, that even Norton’s material theory can avoid “any role for general rules”28.

Changing what justification requires

Hinges and entitlement. Following Wittgenstein, Crispin Wright argues that we are entitled to some principles, the Uniformity Principle among them, without arguing for them29. Salmon’s objection: “admission of unjustified and unjustifiable postulates to deal with the problem is tantamount to making scientific method a matter of faith”30.

Strawson’s dissolution. Asking whether induction in general is justified is like asking whether the law of the land is legal: “For to what legal standards are we appealing?” Being reasonable about matters of fact just is proportioning belief to inductive evidence31. The objection is that we can still ask whether following these standards makes our conclusions “likely to be true”, and Strawson himself allowed that whether “induction will continue to be successful” is a “contingent, factual matter”32.

Reichenbach’s vindication. If the world has limiting frequencies, the straight rule will find them, though it may go badly wrong early on; if it has none, no method succeeds33. Two problems. Infinitely many other rules also converge in the limit, so the argument does not single out induction. And the argument “presumes that there is no cost to trying”, while “it is in the short run that inductive practice actually occurs and where it really needs justification”34.

Read the original

Peirce, "The Probability of Induction" (1878)35

We may express this by saying that in the case of analytic inference we know the probability of our conclusion (if the premises are true), but in the case of synthetic inferences we only know the degree of trustworthiness of our proceeding. As all knowledge comes from synthetic inference, we must equally infer that all human certainty consists merely in our knowing that the processes by which our knowledge has been derived are such as must generally have led to true conclusions.

Peirce gives up on assigning probabilities to inductive conclusions and asks instead how often the method of sampling leads to the truth. This emphasis on a method’s trustworthiness invites comparison with later pragmatic vindication and externalist justification, without identifying Peirce’s theory with either. Peirce adds that induction will hold good “in the long run”, which he grounds in his account of reality as “the object of the final opinion to which sufficient investigation would lead”.

Learning theory and meta-induction. Formal learning theory extends Reichenbach’s idea. It justifies methods by their efficiency in reaching the truth, and argues that a preference for simpler hypotheses is the unique method that gets there “as efficiently as possible, with a minimum number of retractions”36. Schurz’s meta-induction applies induction to the track records of prediction methods. Its weighting strategy is “long-run optimal”, and since object-level induction has so far beaten its rivals, “it is meta-inductively justified to favor object-inductivistic strategies in the future”37. Critics reply that this justifies following meta-induction, not induction itself38.

Living with the problem

Some accept the sceptical conclusion and contain it. Hume himself confined the doubt to philosophical reflection. Popper “regarded the problem of induction as insurmountable” but held that science proceeds by conjecture and deductive refutation, not induction39. The difficulty is that many hypotheses survive testing, and scientists still judge one better supported than another, so “it appears doubtful then that pure deductivism can give an adequate account of scientific method”40.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Peirce. The chapter’s list starts with the twentieth century, but Peirce’s 1878 criticism of Laplace and his “trustworthiness of our proceeding” answer came first42.
  • Machine learning. Learning theory offers “model-relative” guarantees: no universal justification for learning algorithms, but general theorems about how a given model and prior will perform43.
  • Meta-induction and formal learning theory, above44.

What the chapter simplifies

  • Bayesian convergence. The chapter rightly says convergence needs priors that already favour uniformity. The Stanford survey goes further: the Bayesian framework clarifies Hume’s assumption rather than justifying it45.
  • The material theory. Norton presents his view as a dissolution of Hume’s problem, but whether the regress of material justifications ends benignly, and whether the theory avoids general rules, is disputed46.
  • Externalism. The chapter presents it as a separate response. In the literature it mainly appears as a defence of rule-circular arguments: if rule R “is in fact reliable”, one can form justified beliefs using it47.

Connections

  • Hume’s problem of induction, for the argument itself.
  • Internalism and externalism and Reliabilism about justification.
  • Popper and falsificationism and Inference to the best explanation (Chapter 10).
