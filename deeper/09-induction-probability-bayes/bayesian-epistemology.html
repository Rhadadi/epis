Bayesian epistemology

In short

Bayesian epistemology studies rational degrees of confidence and their revision. Its familiar core asks for probabilistic coherence at a time and conditionalization when certain new evidence is learned. Neither requirement alone identifies the right starting probabilities. Arguments from betting and accuracy offer different defenses of the norms. Uncertain evidence, idealization, and disputed priors make the philosophical program broader than using one equation.

Layer 1Re-learn

Coherence and change are separate

An invented believer assigns 0.7 confidence to rain tomorrow and 0.5 to no rain tomorrow. Those assessments violate the complement rule: the exhaustive alternatives should sum to one. Probabilism constrains how credences fit together. It does not imply that any particular forecast is accurate merely because the two numbers add correctly. Lin calls this a synchronic norm, applying at a time.

Conditionalization concerns revision across time. In the standard case, an agent learns E with certainty, and E exhausts the new evidence. The rule makes the new confidence in H equal to the old conditional confidence in H given E. In a finite possibility space, incompatible possibilities lose their weights and the compatible possibilities are rescaled while preserving their relative weights.

Imagine, for illustration, three equally probable parcels containing red, blue, or green tiles. A trustworthy inspection establishes that a selected parcel is not green. Conditionalization leaves equal probabilities for red and blue. It preserves their ratio because the new information distinguishes neither. If the inspection also reveals that red parcels receive special handling, that additional evidence must be included; updating on not-green alone would omit part of what was learned.

Why should we follow those norms?

Dutch book arguments connect credences to acceptable bets. In Lin’s example, an agent assigns 0.75 to A and 0.30 to not-A. Paying $75 for a $100 payoff on A and $30 for a $100 payoff on not-A guarantees a $5 loss. The argument generalizes from such incoherence to probabilistic constraints, assuming suitable principles connecting beliefs and bets. Disliking gambling or facing nonmonetary consequences complicates that connection.

Accuracy arguments seek an epistemic rationale. They treat a credence as an estimate of a proposition’s truth value, one if true and zero if false. Under specified measures of accuracy, a nonprobabilistic assignment can be dominated by another assignment that is more accurate in every possible state. The choice and justification of the measure matter; the result is not an assumption-free demonstration that any probability estimate is good.

Sources for this part: Lin 2024, § 1.2, 1.4; Lin 2024, § 1.2; Lin 2024, § 1.6; Lin 2024, § 1.7.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

The problem of priors remains

Subjective Bayesians allow coherent priors; objective Bayesians propose further constraints intended to express freedom from bias. Lin shows why the difference is substantive: coherence and conditionalization alone permit priors that respond inductively and priors that respond counter-inductively. A model can therefore obey the core rules without making the familiar projection from repeated observations.1

Appeals to eventual convergence also need conditions. Merging-of-opinions theorems use assumptions about priors and learning, and concern an indefinitely accumulating body of evidence. Lin discusses worries about whether the convergence actually occurs and whether the long run helps us soon enough. They should not be paraphrased as every disagreement disappears after enough facts.2

Learning need not produce certainty

A cloudy sky may raise confidence that a racetrack will be muddy without settling the matter. Jeffrey conditionalization accommodates uncertain learning by changing weights on a partition while holding specified conditional credences fixed. This additional assumption can itself be challenged when an experience also changes those conditionals. Lin explains both the extension and its limitation.3

Read Bayes’ theorem for the mathematical relation, full belief for categorical attitudes, and scoring rules for assessing predictions.

Where to go next
