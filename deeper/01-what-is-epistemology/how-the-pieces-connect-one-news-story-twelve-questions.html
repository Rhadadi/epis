In short

The chapter ends with a headline, “New study: People who drink coffee live longer”, and twelve questions a trained reader would ask about it. Each question belongs to a part of epistemology: testimony, causation, statistics, replication, motivated reasoning, the ethics of belief. This page shows that the questions are not just good advice: researchers have measured how much a chain of press release and news story distorts a finding, and why one study should move you only a little.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Every headline is the end of a chain of testimony

A researcher writes a paper; a press office summarises it; a journalist summarises the press release; you read the headline. Each link is testimony in the philosopher’s sense: Steup and Neta count “articles by journalists” and broadcast information as testimony. Each link can distort.

2. Then come the questions about the study itself

Was it observational or an experiment? Could something else explain the pattern? How big is the effect? Has it been replicated? These belong to the philosophy of science and statistics.

3. And the questions about you

Do I want it to be true? How confident should I be, and what would change my mind? These belong to the psychology and ethics of belief.

Common confusions

  • The twelve questions are a test the story must pass. They are a checklist, not a calculation: they come with no weights. Combining their answers into one degree of confidence is the job of the probabilistic tools of Chapter 9.
  • If the study is peer-reviewed, the headline is reliable. Much of the distortion happens after peer review, in the press release (see below).
  • One good study settles it. Even a well-run study should move you only a little if the field tests many unlikely hypotheses (see below).

Sources for this part: Steup and Neta 2025, § 5.5.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • The chain of testimony, measured. Sumner and colleagues studied 462 press releases on health research from twenty leading UK universities in 2011, with the papers and 668 news stories based on them1. Between a third and two-fifths of the press releases already exaggerated: 40% gave exaggerated advice, 33% exaggerated causal claims from correlational results, and 36% inferred to humans from animal research. When a press release exaggerated, most of the news did too (58%, 81% and 86%); when it did not, the news exaggerated at 17%, 18% and 10%. And there was “little evidence that exaggeration in press releases increased the uptake of news”2.
  • Good press releases help. Schwartz and colleagues found that when a medical journal’s press release gave the main result in absolute risks, 53% of newspaper stories did so; when it did not, only 9% did3.
  • Why one study should move you little. John Ioannidis showed that the probability a claimed finding is true depends on the field’s ratio R of true to false hypotheses tested, the study’s power 1 − β, and the significance level α. Without bias it is (1 − β)R / (R − βR + α), and a finding is more likely true than false only if (1 − β)R > α4. Findings are less likely to be true when studies are small, analyses flexible, fields “hot”, and interests strong5. “P-hacking” (dropping outliers, reporting selectively, restricting the analysis to a subgroup) pushes the real false-positive rate well above its nominal 5%6.
  • Evidence about evidence. Learning that an expert or a peer disagrees with you is higher-order evidence about your own belief; how it should combine with the first-order evidence is an open question7.
  • Can testimony create knowledge? One philosophical question is whether testimony can only pass knowledge on, or can sometimes produce knowledge the speaker lacked8.

What the chapter simplifies

  • The reader alone. The twelve questions address one reader and one story. The research suggests that some of the most effective fixes are institutional: Sumner and colleagues conclude that “improving the accuracy of academic press releases could represent a key opportunity for reducing misleading health related news”9. That is social epistemology’s subject10.
  • Studies of studies are studies too. Both press-release studies are observational, and their authors word their causal conclusions carefully: the chapter’s question 4 applies to the evidence about question 2.
  • “It can be proven that most claimed research findings are false.” That is Ioannidis’s own provocative wording11. What his model proves is conditional: given the assumed values of R, power and bias, the rest follows. Whether most findings in a given field are false depends on whether those values hold there.
