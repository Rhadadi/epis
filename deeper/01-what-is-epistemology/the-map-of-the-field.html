In short

Contemporary epistemology has many branches: traditional, formal, social, virtue, naturalized, feminist, and the epistemology of science, morality and religion. The chapter’s diagram draws them as a tree. They are better seen as overlapping angles on the same questions, and the map is drawn from one tradition: classical Indian and Chinese philosophy have long, developed theories of knowledge of their own.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Branches differ by method and focus, not by subject

All the branches study knowledge, belief and evidence. They differ in how (with mathematics, with psychology, with case analysis) and in what they look at (the individual knower, communities, character).

2. A quick guide

  • Formal epistemology uses “tools from math and logic”, for instance probability theory to explain scientific reasoning.
  • Social epistemology studies “the epistemic effects of social interactions, practices, norms, and systems”, correcting an older focus on the isolated individual.
  • Virtue epistemology asks what makes an excellent thinker. Its two camps disagree about whether the virtues are faculties such as perception and memory (virtue reliabilists) or cultivated character traits such as open-mindedness (virtue responsibilists).
  • Feminist epistemology’s central idea is situated knowledge: knowledge that reflects the particular perspective of the knower.

Common confusions

  • Formal epistemology is about credences and traditional epistemology about belief. No. Scott Sturgeon’s two-by-two grid shows all four combinations: there is informal work on degrees of confidence and formal work on all-or-nothing belief.
  • Social epistemology is about groups, not individuals. It is about how social practices affect what individuals and groups know, from testimony to the organisation of science.
  • Naturalized epistemology means psychology. Quine first proposed psychology, but in later writings counted other sciences as relevant too.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Classical Indian epistemology. Pramāṇa-śāstra, the theory of the means of knowledge, is a genre of Sanskrit literature spanning almost twenty centuries, and every major school takes a position in it1. Its debates parallel modern ones. Nāgārjuna argued that if every source of knowledge had to be certified by another, there would be an infinite regress; Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta philosophers replied that knowledge is self-certifying (svataḥ prāmāṇya)2. On testimony, Nyāya treats it as a distinct source, while Vaiśeṣika and Yogācāra reduce it to inference from the speaker’s trustworthiness3.
  • Chinese epistemology. Knowledge has traditionally been understood more broadly than in European philosophy, as stemming also, or primarily, from moral training, and the study of knowing has been bound up with the study of being4.
  • Epistemic injustice. Miranda Fricker distinguished testimonial injustice, when prejudice deflates a speaker’s credibility, from hermeneutical injustice, when a group lacks the concepts to make sense of its own experience; her example is sexual harassment before the term existed5.
  • Knowledge as skilled performance. On Ernest Sosa’s account, knowledge is “apt” belief: true because it manifests competence, as an archer’s shot hits because it is skilful6.

What the chapter simplifies

  • The tree. The branches overlap. Feminist epistemology “is thus a branch of social epistemology”7; formal methods are used on questions from every branch8.
  • The founding of social epistemology. Alvin Goldman’s Knowledge in a Social World (1999) set the agenda of its “veritistic” wing, which assesses social practices by how reliably they produce true beliefs9.
  • Moral and religious epistemology in one line. Moral epistemology must explain how moral knowledge is possible at all, given deep moral disagreement10; in religion, “reformed epistemology” widens what counts as evidence, and Alston argued some religious experience can be treated like perception11.
