In short

A reason to believe something can be of two kinds. An epistemic reason bears on whether the belief is true: evidence, argument, reliable testimony. A practical reason bears on whether having the belief would be good for you: comfort, reward, belonging, motivation. The chapter’s billionaire offers a huge practical reason to believe the moon is made of cheese, and it does not help: you cannot simply decide to believe it. The deep questions are why practical reasons seem powerless over belief, whether they should ever count, and what to make of the famous arguments, from Pascal and al-Ghazālī to William James, that they sometimes do.

Layer 1Re-learn

Why this section is hard

In everyday talk “a reason to believe” covers both kinds at once. “You should believe in yourself” and “You should believe the forecast” sound alike, yet the first is about what believing will do for you and the second about what is true. And the two kinds really do mix in life: what we want to be true quietly shapes what we come to believe. Keep one test in mind: would this consideration make the belief more likely to be true? If yes, it is an epistemic reason; if it only makes the belief more pleasant or useful to have, it is a practical one.

Step by step

1. Two kinds of reasons

  • “The forecast says rain; the clouds are dark.” These make rain more likely: epistemic reasons.
  • “If I believe it will be sunny, I’ll enjoy the morning.” This makes the belief nicer to have, not rain less likely: a practical reason.

Practical reasons are perfectly good reasons for actions. The question is whether they can be reasons for belief.

2. You cannot believe at will

Try to believe, right now, that the moon is made of cheese. William Alston rested his case on exactly this experiment: “Can you, at this moment, start to believe that the U.S. is still a colony of Great Britain, just by deciding to do so?”. Bernard Williams went further and argued that believing at will is not just hard but impossible: to believe something at will, regardless of whether it is true, and know you had done so, would be to hold a belief you yourself regard as unconnected to the truth, and that hardly counts as believing.

3. When you deliberate, only truth counts

When you ask yourself “Shall I believe that p?”, the question immediately turns into “Is p true?”. Considerations about the benefits of believing simply drop out of the deliberation. In the literature on the aim of belief, it is widely held that beliefs formed by deliberation have a special connection to truth. Nishi Shah and David Velleman argue that to conceive of an attitude as a belief that p is to conceive of it as governed by a norm of truth, as correct if and only if p is true. Desire is different: if you desire that p and p is false, you have not “erred or made a mistake”.

4. Where practical reasons do come in

They do not vanish; they work at one remove.

  • What to look into, and how hard. Whether to check the brakes, read the contract or get a second opinion depends on what is at stake. Clifford’s ship-owner was blameworthy not for a lack of luck but for “stifling his doubts” instead of investigating.
  • What to act on. Clifford himself granted that “there are many cases in which it is our duty to act upon probabilities, although the evidence is not such as to justify present belief”. Acting on a working assumption is not believing it; see Belief, credence, and acceptance.
  • How to get a belief indirectly. Pascal knew you cannot just decide to believe, so he advised acting as believers do: “taking the holy water, having masses said”.
  • By the back door. Desires and fears shape belief without our noticing: motivated reasoning (Chapter 14). Peirce described the deliberate version, the “method of tenacity”: a friend once begged him not to read a certain newspaper “lest it might change my opinion upon free-trade”.

Common confusions

  • Pascal argued that God exists. No. The Wager argues that you ought to believe in God, or take steps to, because it is the best bet; its reasons are prudential, not evidential.
  • James said you may believe whatever you like. No. He allowed it only for a “genuine” option, one that is living, forced and momentous, and that “cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds”. Chignell stresses that James “is by no means writing a blank doxastic check”.
  • Clifford demanded certainty. No. He demanded sufficient evidence, allowed belief on trustworthy testimony, and in the later part of his essay gave a more moderate account of what “sufficient” means.
  • If belief is involuntary, no one can be blamed for a belief. Not so fast. Clifford’s ship-owner is blamed for how he formed his belief: by avoiding inquiry. And Matthew Chrisman, borrowing an idea from Wilfrid Sellars, argues that epistemic “oughts” are not rules for action at all but rules of criticism, like “The clock’s chimes ought to strike at the quarter hour” or “A child ought to be able to tie his/her shoes by age four”. They say how a believer ought to be, and, like the clock rule, they do not require that their subject can follow them voluntarily. That lets us keep both the “no rewards principle” (no reward, however large, can make you simply decide to believe) and genuine norms for belief.
  • “Positive thinking” is a practical reason that works. Believing a pill will help can change how you feel (a placebo), but that is a reason to take the pill, not evidence that it has an active ingredient. Keep “believing helps” apart from “it is true”.

Layer 2The full story

How the debate developed

Timeline

Practical reasons for belief, from Pascal to now

  • c. 1105 · Al-Ghazālī, in his Persian Alchemy of Happiness, argues that a doubter should act as if there is a next life1.
  • 1670 · Pascal’s Pensées published, with the Wager2.
  • 1781–87 · Kant: moral grounds justify practical faith (Glaube), which is not knowledge3.
  • 1877 · Clifford, “The Ethics of Belief”4.
  • 1896 · James, “The Will to Believe”5.
  • 1973 · Williams, “Deciding to Believe”6.
  • 1988 · Alston argues belief is not under voluntary control7.
  • 2003–05 · Shah, and Shah and Velleman: belief is governed by a norm of truth8.
  • 2000s–now · Defenders of practical reasons for belief, such as Andrew Reisner and Susanna Rinard, and their critics9.

The debate has two strands that are often run together. One is about permission: may we believe on practical grounds? Pascal, James and Kant say yes, in different ways; Clifford says no. The other is about possibility: can practical considerations move belief at all? Its most influential modern statements are Williams’s (1973) and Alston’s (1988)10, and it changes the first: if belief cannot respond to practical reasons, “ought” seems to have nothing to grip. Pascal saw this himself, which is why he recommended holy water and masses rather than an act of will11. Some authors now recast pragmatic arguments as reasons for religious commitment or acceptance rather than belief, a move Pascal arguably made himself12.

Read the original

Read the original

Pascal, Pensées, fragment 233 (published 1670)

“Let us weigh the gain and the loss in wagering that God is. Let us estimate these two chances. If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then, without hesitation that He is.”13

Pascal is answering someone who has accepted that reason cannot settle whether God exists. His point is that you are “under the necessity of playing”: not believing is also a bet. And since “there is an eternity of life and happiness” at stake, even a small chance of winning outweighs any finite loss14. Hájek notes that this is really the third of four arguments in the same section, and that it uses probability theory and decision theory “for almost the first time in history”; Ian Hacking called it “the first well-understood contribution to decision theory”15.

Read the original

Al-Ghazālī, The Alchemy of Happiness (Kīmiyā-yi saʿādat), chapter IV (c. 1105)

“But, should he say that a future life is possible but that the doctrine is so involved in doubt and mystery that it is impossible to decide whether it be true or not, then one may say to him: ‘Then you had better give it the benefit of the doubt! Suppose you are about to eat food and some one tells you a serpent has spat venom on it, you would probably refrain and rather endure the pangs of hunger than eat it, though your informant may be in jest or lying.’”16

“The Lord Ali once, in arguing with an unbeliever, said, ‘If you are right, then neither of us will be any the worse in the future; but if we are right, then we shall escape, and you will suffer.’”17

Five and a half centuries before Pascal, al-Ghazālī gives the same structure in his Persian book on ethics, the Kīmiyā-yi saʿādat18: when a question cannot be settled, weigh what you stand to lose. His examples are everyday prudence: you would skip a meal on a doubtful warning about poison, pay a charm-writer “on the chance of deriving benefit”, and take “perilous voyages in ships for the sake of merely probable profit”19. Notice his conclusion: “Even if he is doubtful about a future existence, reason suggests that he should act as if there were one, considering the tremendous issues at stake”20. That is a practical reason for acting, close to what this guide calls acceptance, not a reason to believe. (Claud Field’s 1910 English version was made from a Hindustani version of the work, not from the Persian original21.)

Read the original

Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" (1877)

“He had acquired his belief not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts. ... Let us alter the case a little, and suppose that the ship was not unsound after all; that she made her voyage safely, and many others after it. Will that diminish the guilt of her owner? Not one jot. ... The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him.”22

The ship-owner story is often remembered as a story about a disaster. Clifford’s own point is the opposite: the outcome does not matter. What makes the belief wrong is how it was formed. Two further passages are less known. To the busy person who has no time to study a question, Clifford replies: “Then he should have no time to believe”23. And he asks whether his principle would leave us “universal sceptics, doubting everything”, unable to rely on “that vast body of knowledge which is daily growing upon the world”24. His answer is no: the second part of the essay, “The Weight of Authority”, sets out when it is right to believe on the testimony of others25.

Read the original

William James, "The Will to Believe" (1896)

“Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds; for to say, under such circumstances, ‘Do not decide, but leave the question open,’ is itself a passional decision,—just like deciding yes or no,—and is attended with the same risk of losing the truth.”26

James’s key move is the last clause. Suspending judgment is not a neutral default; it is a choice with its own risk, the risk of missing a truth. He also turns Pascal against himself: a belief adopted “after such a mechanical calculation would lack the inner soul of faith’s reality”, and for anyone without a prior inclination, Pascal’s option “is not a living option”27.

The arguments, step by step

The argument, step by step

Pascal's Wager (the decision-theoretic version)28

  1. Either God exists or not, and reason cannot settle which.
  2. You must “wager”: not believing is itself a bet.
  3. If you wager for God and God exists, you gain an infinite good; if God does not exist, you lose something finite.
  4. If you wager against God, you gain at most something finite.
  5. Any positive probability multiplied by an infinite gain outweighs any finite gain.
  6. So, as long as you give God’s existence some positive probability, you should wager for God.

The classic objections: the many-gods objection (premise 1 leaves out other possible gods with other demands), which defenders have tried to answer with more careful ways of comparing infinite rewards29; the objection that you cannot believe at will (which Pascal answered with his holy water); and a technical one about mixed strategies. If any strategy that leaves some chance of your coming to believe has infinite expected value, then ignoring the Wager and “having a hamburger” has the same expectation as wagering for God, so premise 5 picks out nothing in particular30.

The argument, step by step

Clifford's argument for evidentialism31

  1. The ship-owner, who believed his ship sound by suppressing his doubts, is guilty, whether or not the ship sinks.
  2. What makes him guilty is that he believed on insufficient evidence.
  3. Beliefs are not private: “no one man’s belief is in any case a private matter which concerns himself alone”. Every belief prepares us for more like it and guides future action, and believing on poor evidence makes us credulous.
  4. So “it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.”

The natural place to push is the jump from 1 to 4. The ship-owner’s belief had grave consequences for others; does that show that every belief on insufficient evidence is wrong, including trivial or comforting ones? Premise 3 is Clifford’s answer: no belief is truly trivial or private. And the conclusion can be read morally (believing so wrongs others) or epistemically (it is believing badly), which is why Clifford became the emblem of a strict “evidentialist” position in the ethics of belief, even though his considered view of “sufficient” evidence is more moderate32.

The argument, step by step

James's argument for the right to believe33

  1. Some options are genuine: living, forced and momentous.
  2. Some genuine options cannot be decided on intellectual grounds.
  3. In such a case, refusing to decide is itself a decision, with its own risk of losing the truth.
  4. “Believe truth!” and “Shun error!” are two separate commandments; no rule of reason fixes how to weigh them.
  5. So in such cases our “passional nature” may, and must, decide.

A natural objection is to premise 3: suspending judgment is a way of avoiding the risk of error, and treating it as just another bet assumes the very weighting of the two commandments that is in dispute. James’s reply is premise 4: our feelings of duty about truth and about error alike “are in any case only expressions of our passional life”34.

The positions

The positions

Evidence, practice and belief: the main views35

View Who Core claim The main objection or cost
Strict evidentialism Clifford Only sufficient evidence can make a belief permissible Some vital questions cannot be settled by evidence, and suspending judgment on them is itself a risky choice (James)
Practical non-evidentialism (pragmatist) Pascal, James Prudential considerations can make a belief permissible or even required Belief does not respond to such reasons at will; they bear on whether a belief would be good to have, not on whether it is true
Practical non-evidentialism (moral) Kant Moral grounds can justify a taking-to-be-true that is not knowledge Is the result belief (Glaube) or only acceptance (Annehmung)?
Recasting as acceptance Some writers on faith and the Wager Pragmatic arguments are reasons for commitment or acceptance, not belief Belief itself is left to the evidence
Doxastic involuntarism Alston, Williams Belief is not under direct voluntary control, so practical reasons cannot directly move it Contrived cases where believing at will seems possible (Peels, Reisner)

Where the debate stands

  • Is belief really involuntary? The standard view in contemporary philosophy is that it is. But in seven experiments, Turri, Rose and Buckwalter found that ordinary people’s judgments about belief provide “strong evidence that voluntary belief is conceptually possible”, and some evidence that it is psychologically possible36. Contrived cases in which the truth depends on what you believe point the same way37.
  • What “ought to believe” means. If belief is not chosen, epistemic norms cannot be rules for action. Chrisman’s proposal, that they are rules of criticism, is one way to keep them38. Alston made his case against voluntarism while arguing that the deontological picture of justification, in terms of duties and blame, needs voluntary control; its defenders disagree about whether it does (Steup says it does, Feldman that it does not)39.
  • Are practical considerations reasons for belief at all? Several authors argue that they are. Susanna Rinard holds that the rationality of beliefs “is determined in precisely the same way as the rationality of other states”, so that “moral or prudential considerations can be reasons for belief”40. Andrew Reisner has argued for prudential reasons for belief, including “Weighing Pragmatic and Evidential Reasons for Belief”41. On the other side, practical reasons are said to bear only on whether a belief would be good to have, not on whether p, the question a belief answers42.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Pascal’s precursors and parallels. Similar pragmatic arguments appear in al-Ghazālī and in the Buddhist discourse to the Kālāmas43, and in the Christian writers Sirmond and Chillingworth a few years before Pascal44.
  • Cases where belief and truth are linked. In Rik Peels’s example, Dr. Transparent promises you $10 if, within a minute, you come to believe that you will receive $10. The truth depends on the belief, so the practical reason and the evidence point the same way. Peels argues that such a belief can be formed at will, partly for practical reasons, and rationally; Andrew Reisner uses a perfectly reliable mind-reading machine to make a similar case45.
  • Kant’s practical faith. For Kant, moral considerations can justify a kind of belief (Glaube) or acceptance (Annehmung) that is not knowledge46.
  • Clifford on authority. The chapter presents Clifford as a lone sceptic; his essay’s second half is about when it is right to believe on testimony47.
  • Belief by upbringing. The most common non-evidential source of belief is not a wager but where you were born. Al-Ghazālī noticed it: “how easily the children of Christians become Christians, and the children of Moslems embrace Islam”, and set out to separate the “accidental beliefs imposed on him by the authority of his parents and his masters” from what he could know for certain48. Clifford condemns the same pattern, the man who “keeps down and pushes away any doubts” about a belief “taught in childhood”49.

What the chapter simplifies

  • “You probably cannot believe it at will.” Probably not. But whether this is a psychological fact (Alston) or a conceptual truth (Williams) is disputed, and Williams’s argument is widely thought to fail as it stands: Barbara Winters noted you could have the ability without knowing it, and Jonathan Bennett imagined the “Credamites”, who will themselves into beliefs and immediately forget having done so50.
  • Pascal’s Wager as one argument. It is the third of four in a single section of the Pensées51.
