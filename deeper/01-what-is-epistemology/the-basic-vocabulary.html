In short

The chapter defines the working vocabulary of the field: proposition, truth, evidence, justification, rationality, warrant, certainty, fallibilism, defeater and reasons. Each word has a plain core that the chapter gets right, and each hides a debate. This page gives the core again with examples, then shows where philosophers disagree about what each word really picks out.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. What is believed: propositions

“Snow is white” and “La neige est blanche” are different sentences that say the same thing. What they say, the thing that is true or false and that you believe, is a proposition. McGrath and Frank’s working definition: propositions are “the sharable objects of the attitudes and the primary bearers of truth and falsity”.

2. Truth and evidence

A proposition is true when things are as it says. Evidence is what makes a proposition more or less likely for you: the dark clouds, the forecast. In Ian Hacking’s phrase, evidence is “the evidence of one thing that points beyond itself”, as smoke points to fire.

3. Justification, warrant and certainty

A belief is justified when it is held for good reasons or formed in a good way; it can still be false. Warrant, in Alvin Plantinga’s sense, is whatever turns true belief into knowledge. Certainty comes in two kinds: feeling completely sure (psychological) and having the highest possible justification (epistemic).

4. Defeaters and reasons

A defeater removes or weakens justification. Thomas Kelly’s example: your testimony that your name is Fritz is evidence that it is. Credible testimony that it is really Leopold rebuts that evidence; evidence that you are a pathological liar undercuts it. A normative reason counts in favour of something; a motivating reason is the reason for which someone actually believes or acts.

Common confusions

  • A proposition is a sentence. No: two sentences in different languages can express one proposition, and one ambiguous sentence can express two.
  • Justified means true. No: a detective can be justified in a false belief.
  • Feeling certain is being certain. People feel certain about many falsehoods. And you can be epistemically certain without knowing it, because certainty need not “iterate”: being certain that p does not entail being certain that you are certain.
  • “Warrant” means justification. Elsewhere in philosophy it often does (“warranted assertibility”); in Plantinga’s usage it means what makes true belief knowledge.

Sources for this part: McGrath and Frank 2024, preamble; Kelly 2016, § 3; Plantinga 1993; Reed 2022, § 1; Kelly 2016, § 1; Alvarez and Way 2024, § 1; Forrest 2025, § 6.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • What evidence is. Philosophers have answered very differently: sense-data (Russell), stimulations of the sensory receptors (Quine), observation statements (the logical positivists), everything one knows (Williamson’s “E = K”), or one’s current mental states (Conee and Feldman)1. The choice matters: if evidence is knowledge, no body of evidence can rule out a truth; if it is experiences or beliefs, it can2.
  • Internal or external justification. Internalists hold that justification depends only on what is inside the believer: a brain in a vat with your experiences would be just as justified as you. Externalists deny this, wanting from justification the likelihood of truth that knowledge needs3.
  • The strange sound of fallible knowledge. Fallibilism is the majority view, yet “I know it’s raining, though I could be wrong” sounds odd; David Lewis said that fallible knowledge “just sounds contradictory”4. Stephen Hetherington argues the oddity does not show that knowledge must be infallible5.
  • Certainty in al-Ghazālī. Around 1100, al-Ghazālī set the bar for certain knowledge as knowledge that no possible challenge could shake: if a man insisted that three is more than ten and, “to prove it”, changed a rod into a serpent, “I should remain none the less convinced of the falsity of his assertion, and although his miracle might arouse my astonishment, it would not instil any doubt into my belief”. Testing what he knew against that bar, he found that nothing passed it “with the exception of sense-perceptions and necessary principles”, and went on to doubt even those6.
  • Defeat can be defeated. Evidence that the “pathological liar” story was planted by your enemy restores your original justification: “In principle, there is no limit to the complexity of the relations of defeat”7. John Pollock developed this into a theory of defeasible reasoning8.

What the chapter simplifies

  • Aristotle’s definition of truth. It is often treated as the founding statement of the correspondence theory, but Marian David calls it a “muted, relatively minimal” version, which is why deflationists also claim it; Plato gives almost the same formulation9.
  • The evidence slogan. “E is evidence for H when E is more probable if H is true than if it is false” is one standard probabilistic gloss; it does not settle what E can be10.
  • Three senses of “reason”. The threefold distinction is now accepted by most writers, but recent authors separate motivating from explanatory reasons more sharply than older usage did11.
