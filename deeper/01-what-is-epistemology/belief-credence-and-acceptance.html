In short

Three different attitudes can all look like “thinking something is true”. Belief is taking it to be true: all-or-nothing, a settled state that guides what you say and do. Credence is your degree of confidence, from 0 to 1: you can be 90% sure. Acceptance is a decision to treat something as true for a purpose, an argument, a calculation, a plan, whether or not you believe it. The chapter introduces all three. The harder questions are how they fit together: whether belief is just high enough credence, why that idea leads to paradox, and how much bad reasoning comes from sliding between them.

Layer 1Re-learn

Why this section is hard

Ordinary language uses “believe”, “think”, “suppose”, “assume” and “be sure” almost interchangeably. And the three attitudes often travel together: usually you believe what you accept, and you are confident in what you believe. The differences show up only in the cases where they come apart. Keep three questions in mind for any case: Is the person settled or not? (belief). How confident are they? (credence). Are they using it as a premise, for a purpose? (acceptance).

Step by step

1. Belief

To believe it is raining is to take it to be true that it is raining. You don’t have to be thinking about it: you believe that 2 + 2 = 4 while asleep. Philosophers call this a dispositional belief, as opposed to an occurrent one, the thought actively in your mind.

Belief has three classic options: believe, disbelieve, or suspend judgment.

2. Credence

Credence is confidence on a scale. You are more confident the sun will rise tomorrow than that your team will win. Locke already saw this: there are “degrees of assent from full assurance and confidence, quite down to conjecture, doubt, and distrust”. Bayesian epistemology adds two rules: your credences should obey the laws of probability, and they should change with new evidence in a particular way (by conditionalization).

3. How belief and credence relate

The natural thought, the Lockean thesis, is that to believe something is to have a high enough credence in it, above some threshold. It leads straight to paradox, as you will see below.

4. Acceptance

To accept something is to adopt it as a premise for a purpose. Accepting usually means deciding to stop inquiring and act as if the matter were settled. Two cases show that it can come apart from belief:

  • Acceptance without belief. A scientist may build a research programme on a theory she does not think is literally true.
  • Belief without acceptance. You may believe a ladder is stable, but because your neck depends on it, not accept that it is stable until you have checked.

Common confusions

  • “I accept your premise” means “I believe it”. No. In argument, accepting a premise “for the sake of argument” commits you to nothing; criticising someone for “assuming” what they only accepted for the sake of argument misses the point.
  • Credence is just belief with a number. Not quite. On the Lockean thesis it might be, but the lottery and preface paradoxes show that belief and high credence behave differently.
  • Suspending judgment is having no attitude. Epistemologists who work with all-or-nothing attitudes count suspended judgment as one of them, alongside belief and disbelief. Whether it is voluntary is debated: Booth argues that if we cannot believe at will, we cannot suspend judgment at will either.
  • Believing is a feeling of conviction. Some philosophers come close to this: Hume treated beliefs principally as occurrences, and Smithies argues that to believe is to be disposed to feel conviction. But most of your beliefs are not felt at any given moment: they are held dispositionally, as when you believe that 2 + 2 = 4 while asleep. And some beliefs have almost no connection with what you do, like, in Eric Schwitzgebel’s example, an American homebody’s belief “that there is at least one church in Nice”.
  • A model is believed. Usually it is accepted: scientists often work from theories they do not think are literally true, accepting them as an adequate basis for research.

Layer 2The full story

How the ideas developed

Timeline

Belief, degrees of belief and acceptance

  • 1690 · Locke: degrees of assent should be “regulated by the grounds of probability”1.
  • 1739 · Hume: belief is “a lively idea related to or associated with a present impression”2.
  • 1877 · Peirce: belief is a habit of action, doubt the irritation that drives inquiry3.
  • 1926–37 · Ramsey and de Finetti: the “Dutch book” argument ties degrees of belief to betting, and argues they must obey the laws of probability4.
  • 1961 · Kyburg’s lottery paradox5.
  • 1965 · Makinson’s preface paradox6.
  • 1980 · van Fraassen: scientists accept theories without believing them7.
  • 1992 · Cohen’s An Essay on Belief and Acceptance; Bratman’s “Practical Reasoning and Acceptance in a Context”; Foley states the Lockean thesis explicitly8.
  • 2000s–now · “Credence-first”, “belief-first” and dualist programmes9.

Three threads run through this history. The first is the question of what belief is: for Hume, an idea with special vivacity; for Peirce, a habit of action; for today’s theorists, a stored representation, a cluster of dispositions, or a state whose essence is to be correct only if true10. The second is the rise of degrees: Locke already had “degrees of assent”11, and the twentieth century gave them numbers and laws, with the Dutch book argument tying credences to fair bets12. The third is the discovery that the all-or-nothing and the graded do not fit together easily: the lottery and preface paradoxes show that rational high confidence cannot simply be added up into rational belief13. Acceptance entered the story as a fourth attitude, mainly to explain how scientists and planners can act on what they do not believe14.

Read the original

Read the original

Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book IV, chapter XV (1690)

“As it happened to a Dutch ambassador, who entertaining the king of Siam with the particularities of Holland, which he was inquisitive after, amongst other things told him, that the water in his country would sometimes, in cold weather, be so hard, that men walked upon it, and that it would bear an elephant, if he were there. To which the king replied, ‘Hitherto I have believed the strange things you have told me, because I look upon you as a sober fair man, but now I am sure you lie.’”15

Locke tells the story to show how degrees of assent should work. The mind, “if it will proceed rationally, ought to examine all the grounds of probability, and see how they make more or less for or against any proposition”, and then assent “more or less firm” in proportion16. The two grounds are our own experience and the testimony of others17. The king’s experience of water was all against the ambassador, so even an honest witness could not move him. The story is also a warning: proportioning belief to evidence can lead a rational person to a false conclusion when his experience is narrow.

Read the original

Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book I, part III, section 7 (1739)

“An opinion, therefore, or belief may be most accurately defined, a lively idea related to or associated with a present impression.”18

Hume thought the difference between believing that something happened and merely imagining it could not lie in the content of the idea, since you can imagine exactly what you believe. So it must lie in “the manner of its being conceived”: belief is an idea with more “force and vivacity”19. His example: two people read the same book, one taking it as history and the other as fiction. The believer “has a more lively conception of all the incidents”; the other “a more faint and languid conception”20. Hume’s account treats beliefs principally as occurrences; Braithwaite and Gertler have partly followed him21. The theories in “The positions” below look elsewhere: to what a belief represents, what it disposes you to do, or what norm governs it.

Read the original

Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief" (1877)

“The feeling of believing is a more or less sure indication of there being established in our nature some habit which will determine our actions. Doubt never has such an effect. ... Belief does not make us act at once, but puts us into such a condition that we shall behave in a certain way, when the occasion arises.”22

“The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term this struggle inquiry.”23

Peirce gives the dispositional view of belief its classic form: a belief is a habit of action, not a feeling. He also makes belief and doubt the two poles of inquiry. Doubt is “an uneasy and dissatisfied state from which we struggle to free ourselves”; belief is “a calm and satisfactory state”, and we cling “not merely to believing, but to believing just what we do believe”24. That clinging is a danger: the rest of the essay compares four ways of settling opinion, from simple tenacity to the method of science.

The arguments, step by step

The argument, step by step

The lottery paradox (Kyburg)25

  1. In a fair lottery of 100 tickets with one winner, each ticket has a 0.99 chance of losing.
  2. Suppose rational belief requires a credence of at least 0.99 (a Lockean threshold).
  3. So you may rationally believe of each ticket that it will lose.
  4. If you rationally believe each of several things, you may rationally believe their conjunction (“agglomeration”).
  5. So you may rationally believe that every ticket will lose.
  6. But you also rationally believe that one ticket will win. So you rationally believe a contradiction.

Something has to give. Kyburg’s own answer was to reject premise 4: reason forbids believing a contradiction, but permits a set of beliefs that cannot all be true together26. Others restrict premise 2, for instance by forbidding belief in members of a “probabilistically self-undermining” set (Douven); Kevin Korb produced a counterexample that tells against all proposals of this kind27.

The argument, step by step

The preface paradox (Makinson)28

  1. An author has carefully checked every claim in her book, and rationally believes each of them.
  2. She also knows that every long book contains errors, so she rationally believes that at least one of her claims is false.
  3. If agglomeration holds, she rationally believes the conjunction of all her claims.
  4. So she rationally believes both that all her claims are true and that not all of them are.

Raymond Wilder apologised in the preface of his 1952 textbook for the errors in the text; the 1982 reprint, Sorensen notes, has three pages of errata. The preface paradox matters because it “does not rely on a probabilistic acceptance rule”: the author’s modesty is a qualitative judgment, so restricting the Lockean threshold cannot dissolve it29.

The argument, step by step

The Lockean thesis from James's two goals30

  1. Believing a truth has some value R; believing a falsehood has some cost W.
  2. A rational believer maximises expected epistemic value, weighting outcomes by her credences.
  3. Believing X, with credence p in X, has expected value pR − (1 − p)W; suspending has value 0.
  4. So she should believe X exactly when p > W / (R + W).

This derivation, due to Hempel and developed by Easwaran and Dorst, gives the Lockean thesis a rationale: the threshold is the exchange rate between James’s commandments “Believe truth!” and “Shun error!”31. Pettigrew calls the assumption in premise 1 “Jamesian” for that reason. If error is three times as bad as truth is good, the threshold is 0.75; if they are equal, 0.532. See Epistemology is normative for James’s side of this.

The positions

The positions

What belief is, and how it relates to credence33

View Who Core claim The main objection
Representationalism Fodor, Dretske, Mandelbaum To believe P is to have a stored representation with the content P An alien who behaves exactly like us seems to share our beliefs, whatever is inside his head (Schwitzgebel’s “Rudolfo”)

Belief–credence dualism Staffel, Buchak, Jackson, Weisberg Neither reduces to the other The Bayesian Challenge: what does belief add?

Where the debate stands

Whether belief or credence is more basic is “one of the most significant divides in contemporary epistemology”34. There are four main answers:

  • Eliminativism. Get rid of one attitude. Richard Jeffrey thought talk of full belief would be superseded by degrees of belief: “I am inclined to think Ramsey sucked the marrow out of the ordinary notion”35. Richard Holton holds the reverse, affirming beliefs and denying that credences exist36. Both are minority views.
  • Credence-first. Belief is a kind of credence or reducible to it (Wedgwood, Greco, Weatherson)37.
  • Belief-first. Credence is a kind of belief, for instance belief about probabilities (Moon and Jackson, Holton, Easwaran)38.
  • Dualism. Both exist and neither reduces to the other (Staffel, Buchak, Jackson, Weisberg)39.

The defender of full belief faces what Mark Kaplan calls the Bayesian Challenge: Bayesian decision theory needs only credences and utilities, so what does belief add? Genin and Huber note that this may be “an accident of history”: had a theory of qualitative decision-making been developed first, “the shoe would now be on the other foot”. The Bayesian’s position would be weaker still if the qualitative decision-making we seem to use as a matter of course proved less cognitively demanding40. Formal alternatives to probability exist too: ranking theory gives a notion of qualitative belief that “incurs no Lottery-style paradoxes”41.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Acceptance’s critics. Chignell warns that acceptance “is typically a technical notion and characterizations of its nature and ethics differ radically in the literature”42. Cohen’s definition is the clearest: to accept p is “to have or adopt a policy of deeming, positing, or postulating that p” as a premise for deciding what to do or think in a context, “whether or not one feels it to be true that p”43.
  • Faith without belief. On “non-doxastic” accounts, faith is a practical commitment that need not include belief; William Alston suggested faith may involve acceptance rather than belief44.
  • Imagination and supposition. Philosophers disagree on how they differ: by content (imagining is richer and more sensory) or by function (imagining produces emotion and “imaginative resistance” to morally repugnant scenarios; supposing does not), and some doubt they differ in kind at all45.
  • Belief as aiming at truth. Many philosophers hold that belief by definition aims at truth: an attitude that does not aim at truth is not a belief46. Engel argues that this is what distinguishes belief from acceptance: belief is the only attitude whose “correctness condition” is truth47.
  • Clifford on acting under uncertainty. Even the strictest evidentialist granted that “there are many cases in which it is our duty to act upon probabilities, although the evidence is not such as to justify present belief”48: in effect, acceptance without belief.

What the chapter simplifies

  • “Beliefs are commonly understood as dispositional.” Two claims hide here. That most beliefs are held dispositionally (not occurrently) is uncontroversial. That belief is a disposition is one theory among several49.
  • Knowledge and belief. Gettier’s famous paper stated three versions of the justified-true-belief analysis: with “believes”, “accepts” and “is sure that”. Epistemologists have tended to follow him in treating them as effectively equivalent50. The difference between belief and acceptance was not in view.
  • The Lockean threshold “say 0.95”. The Lockean thesis is a family of norms, one for each threshold; nothing fixes the number except, on the derivation above, how you weigh truth against error51.
