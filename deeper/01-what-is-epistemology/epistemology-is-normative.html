Epistemology is normative

In short

Psychology describes how people do reason; epistemology asks how they ought to: what they are entitled to believe, and what counts as good evidence. That is what “normative” means. The chapter draws a parallel with ethics: duties, virtues, blame and good outcomes have counterparts for belief. The parallel raises real problems (we cannot choose our beliefs the way we choose actions), and it has been challenged from the other side by philosophers who think epistemology should become a branch of science.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Describing versus evaluating

“People tend to believe what their friends believe” describes. “You should not believe it just because your friends do” evaluates. Epistemology is mostly about the second kind of claim, though it uses the first.

2. The parallel with ethics

Ethics asks what to do; epistemology asks what to believe. So there are epistemic versions of duty (to consider the evidence), virtue (open-mindedness), blame (for believing carelessly) and good outcomes (true beliefs).

3. What is the goal?

The obvious answer is truth. William James noticed that this is really two goals: “We must know the truth; and we must avoid error”. They “are not two ways of stating an identical commandment, they are two separable laws”. Believing everything maximises the first; believing nothing maximises the second. Every standard of evidence strikes a balance between them.

Common confusions

  • Normative means moral. Not necessarily. “You should believe the forecast” is an epistemic “should”, not a moral one; whether epistemic duties are also moral ones is itself a debate.
  • If beliefs are involuntary, nothing normative applies to them. Evaluations don’t need control: we call a belief unjustified as we call a heart unhealthy. The real debate is about whether duties and blame make sense for belief (see below).
  • Every truth is worth believing. No rational person wants to know “the measurements of every grain of sand on a given beach”. That is why some philosophers say the goal is significant truth, or knowledge, or understanding.

Sources for this part: James 1897, 17–18; Boespflug and Jackson 2024, § 6.1; Pritchard et al. 2025, § 8.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • The challenge from naturalism. Quine proposed that epistemology “falls into place as a chapter of psychology”, studying how “meager input” produces a “torrential output” of theory1. Jaegwon Kim replied that this abandons justification, and with it epistemology: “For epistemology to go out of the business of justification is for it to go out of business”2. In later work Quine insisted that “the normative is naturalized, not dropped”: normative epistemology is “the technology of truth-seeking”3. So the question is not whether epistemology is normative, but where its norms come from.
  • Two objections to epistemic duties. First, control: duties seem to need control over belief, which we lack. Second, insufficiency: members of an isolated culture might break no duty and still hold unjustified beliefs. The rival view defines justification as believing “in a way that makes it sufficiently likely that [the] belief is true”4.
  • Epistemic consequentialism and its critics. On this view a way of forming beliefs is permissible insofar as it promotes true belief and the avoidance of false belief. Selim Berker, developing an argument of Roderick Firth’s, argues that it is false5. A related debate, permissivism, asks whether the same evidence can permit more than one rational response6.
  • James’s two goals, made precise. Give believing a truth the value R and believing a falsehood the cost W. Then believing maximises expected value exactly when your credence exceeds W / (R + W)7. If error is three times as bad as truth is good, believe above 0.75; if equal, above 0.5. Richard Pettigrew calls the assumption “Jamesian” for this reason8. See Belief, credence, and acceptance.
  • Why knowledge is worth more than true belief. The question goes back to Plato’s Meno. If a reliable method is valuable only because it gives true beliefs, the value of the true belief “swamps” the value of how it was produced: the swamping problem9.

What the chapter simplifies

  • “Consequentialism ↔ Veritism.” Veritism is a view about what is valuable (truth); consequentialism is a view about how value fixes what is permissible. One can hold either without the other, and Berker’s objection targets the second10.
  • The parallel with ethics. A belief is usually assessed when it is formed, while an action’s rightness may depend on consequences known only much later11.
  • “The most popular answer is truth.” Chignell reports that only “a few philosophers and psychologists” hold that truth is the only aim of belief. Other candidates are knowledge, justification, virtue and understanding12.
