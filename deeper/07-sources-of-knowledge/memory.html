In short

Memory lets knowledge survive beyond the experience that first supplied it. Remembering is often reconstructive, so it need not reproduce a stored scene unchanged. A recollection can match the original experience even when that experience was mistaken. It can also describe an event accurately without faithfully reproducing how it was originally experienced. Questions about accurate remembering and justified belief therefore need to be kept distinct.

Layer 1Re-learn

Two ways a recollection can be accurate

You saw a coat under unusual lighting and took it to be green. Later you remember seeing a green coat. This recollection may faithfully reproduce your experience even if the coat was blue. Its fidelity to your earlier appearance does not establish accuracy about the object.

Michaelian and Sutton distinguish authenticity, correspondence with the subject’s original experience, from truth, correspondence with the past event itself. Neither entails the other. A remembered representation may preserve a misperception, or accurately represent an aspect of the event that was not accurately perceived originally.

Now imagine that you later learn the lighting distorted the colour, and recall the scene as containing a blue coat. The corrected representation may get the coat right while changing how the scene originally appeared. That is useful for understanding what happened, but it is not the same achievement as faithfully recalling your initial experience. The two standards answer different questions.

Reconstructive does not mean fictitious

Generationist accounts treat remembering as an active construction of a representation of the past. This contrasts with a picture in which memory simply retrieves an unchanged recording. The survey traces the contrast through psychological work by Ebbinghaus and Bartlett, while noting the continuing influence of preservative conceptions in philosophy.

Construction can succeed. Reassembling a route from remembered landmarks need not produce an invented journey. It also permits error: a missing turn can be supplied from a similar trip. The relevant epistemic question concerns the reliability and evidential relation of this particular reconstruction, rather than whether any constructive activity occurred.

Similarly, vividness alone does not settle truth or authenticity. A forceful scene can still represent a mistaken appearance. A sparse recollection can still preserve an important fact. Assessing remembered detail requires distinguishing confidence in a representation from its connection with the event.

Sources for this part: Michaelian and Sutton 2026, § 6.1; Michaelian and Sutton 2026, § 4.2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Forgotten evidence and forgotten defeat

Suppose you remember that a train leaves at eight, but no longer remember whether you checked the timetable or heard an uncertain guess. Does forgetting the source remove the justification? The problem concerns retention of support rather than the mere capacity to retain a sentence. The literature also discusses forgotten defeat: an earlier warning may have undermined the belief even if that warning is no longer recalled1.

These cases differ from ordinary loss of confidence. A person might remain completely confident after forgetting an important correction. Another might become uncertain despite retaining information from an excellent source. A theory must explain the epistemic status of the retained belief, rather than simply equating justification with current confidence.

Does memory generate knowledge?

Whether memory only preserves earlier knowledge, or can generate knowledge not initially possessed, remains disputed. This question interacts with theories of remembering but is not resolved merely by calling memory reconstructive. Adding content to a recollection and acquiring knowledge through that content are different achievements2.

An archive provides a helpful comparison. Preserving a document, restoring its damaged wording, and discovering a new implication are three tasks. Their results can all be useful without sharing the same evidential basis. The comparison clarifies the questions; it is not a claim that human memory literally operates like document storage.

Connections

Internalism and externalism examines retained support when its details become inaccessible. Testimony introduces a parallel transmission/generation debate. The parallel deserves examination, but it does not prove that the two sources have identical requirements3.

Where to go next

Read Michaelian and Sutton’s section on theories of remembering first, then their distinction between truth and authenticity. Their epistemology section identifies the additional problems of forgotten evidence, forgotten defeat, and generation. It is a map of those debates rather than a complete defense of any one solution4.
