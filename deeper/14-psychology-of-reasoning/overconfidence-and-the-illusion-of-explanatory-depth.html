Overconfidence and the illusion of explanatory depth

In short

Overconfidence concerns a mismatch between confidence and what performance or evidence supports. The mismatch can involve an estimated score, a comparison with others, or an interval stated too narrowly. Explanatory depth raises a related question about understanding a mechanism. Recognising a device or knowing who can explain it differs from explaining it oneself. A useful assessment specifies which kind of confidence is being evaluated.

Layer 1Re-learn

Confidence in which claim?

Imagine a fictional mechanic who says she understands a cooling system. She recognises its parts and can replace a familiar component. When asked how pressure changes across the whole circuit, however, she cannot explain it. Her practical competence and explanatory understanding are different achievements. The gap does not show that she lacks every useful kind of knowledge.

Rozenblit and Keil’s abstract describes an illusion of explanatory depth: people feel that their understanding is more precise, coherent, and deep than it is. It reports comparisons across knowledge domains. Only the openly available publisher-deposited abstract was read for this page; the detailed methods, individual results, and extensions to political attitudes are not asserted here.

Separately, Bazerman illustrates overconfidence through intervals meant to contain uncertain quantities. The relevant comparison is between their stated confidence and how often they contain the correct values. An impressive-sounding narrow interval is not automatically a well-supported one.

Three comparisons

For the mechanic, compare an estimated test score with the observed score, an estimated position among peers with that position, and the stated confidence of an interval with its performance across suitable cases. These comparisons target different claims. They should not be collapsed into one judgment that she is generally too confident.

An interval can be too narrow even when the central estimate is good. Someone can outperform most colleagues while still overestimating her own score. These are conceptual possibilities, not reported rates in a sample.

Sources for this part: Rozenblit and Keil 2002, Abstract; Bazerman 2026, Overconfidence.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Ask what the explanation connects

For the cooling system, an explanation should connect the relevant parts, flows, constraints, and predicted changes. Naming components is a start but does not establish the mechanism. A verbal account can also sound coherent while failing to predict what changes when a valve closes.

This distinction is useful without treating an inability to explain as evidence that a person is incompetent at every task. Procedural expertise, factual recall, and explanatory understanding can come apart. Rozenblit and Keil’s abstract explicitly distinguishes explanatory knowledge from other kinds of knowledge; it does not provide a warrant for diagnosing an individual from one conversation.1

Records and feedback

Mellers and colleagues assess geopolitical forecasts using proper scoring rules and discuss calibration separately from other aspects of forecasting skill. Their study illustrates a way to compare confidence with recorded outcomes. It does not turn confidence into a personality verdict or establish that every forecast domain behaves similarly.2

For an explanatory claim, a record of predictions about the fictional system can help distinguish an account that merely sounds plausible from one that guides appropriate expectations. For numerical confidence, an appropriate forecast record provides a different comparison. One success is insufficient to establish a reliable pattern.

Read calibration and scoring rules for the numerical assessment and understanding and wisdom for the broader epistemic distinction. Humility adds the question of how someone responds to a limitation once it becomes visible.

Where to go next

Bazerman introduces interval overconfidence. Rozenblit and Keil’s public abstract supplies only the explanatory-depth claim and broad domain comparison. Mellers and colleagues supply an original example of assessing forecasting confidence. The full explanatory-depth paper and its later political extensions were not read or used as full-text evidence.3
