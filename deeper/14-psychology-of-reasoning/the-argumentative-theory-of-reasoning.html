In short

Mercier and Sperber propose that reasoning’s primary function is producing and evaluating arguments in communication. The theory offers an explanation of both persuasive skill and one-sided argument production. Its evolutionary claim is stronger than the observation that discussion sometimes helps. A useful critical exchange needs relevant evidence and opportunities to challenge reasons. Mere disagreement or group membership does not guarantee better reasoning.

Layer 1Re-learn

Two roles in an exchange

Imagine a fictional planner defending a transport proposal. She generates reasons for her preferred route. A critic evaluates those reasons and identifies a missing constraint. The exchange can make an initially one-sided argument answerable to another perspective.

Mercier and Sperber’s target article proposes an argumentative function of reasoning: devising and evaluating reasons intended to persuade. They connect that function with reliance on communication and vulnerability to misinformation. This is their hypothesis about function, rather than a definition that makes all reasoning persuasive by stipulation.

They review differences between producing an argument for a view and challenging another view, and discuss how argumentative settings can change performance. The reviewed studies give the theory material to explain. Whether they uniquely support its evolutionary explanation remains a further question.

Production is not evaluation

The planner might be adept at finding reasons for her route but weak at anticipating objections. The critic might identify a flaw without proposing a better route. These contributions differ. A successful inquiry needs the objections to affect the proposal, rather than using the exchange as a performance in which nobody can revise a position.

An argument also differs from the cognitive process that produces it. Mercier and Sperber distinguish an inference as a process from an argument as a representation linking premises and conclusion. An articulate representation does not fully expose the underlying processing.

Sources for this part: Mercier and Sperber 2011, PDF p. 1; Mercier and Sperber 2011, PDF p. 6; Mercier and Sperber 2011, PDF p. 2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

From present performance to historical function

Showing that people reason well in some exchanges does not by itself establish why the capacity evolved. Alternative accounts might also predict a benefit from shared information, external checking, or motivation. The function hypothesis needs its own comparative argument. The target article presents such a proposal and reinterprets a range of studies through it; it should remain identifiable as a theory.1

The fictional planning discussion illustrates a possible corrective mechanism. It supplies no historical evidence about the origin of reasoning. Keeping those levels apart allows the practical insight to be assessed without pretending that its usefulness proves the whole theory.

The group must permit the correction

Mellers and colleagues’ forecasting study discusses both potential gains from sharing information and costs such as herding and poor group dynamics. Their particular tournament reports benefits of teaming, but a group is not helpful solely because it contains several people.2

For the planners, a critic needs access to the evidence and a genuine opportunity to change the route. If status prevents correction, the group can merely strengthen the preferred case. If everyone copied the same source, apparent agreement may add little information.

See motivated reasoning for the defence of preferred conclusions and the wisdom and madness of crowds for conditions of aggregation. A good exchange explains what each participant contributes and how criticism changes the shared result.

Where to go next

Read the original Mercier–Sperber target article, distinguishing their theory from the experiments they review and from the accompanying commentaries. Mellers and colleagues provide a separate original study of group forecasting. Neither source warrants the maxim that any argument with opponents must improve judgment.3
