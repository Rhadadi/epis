Debiasing: what works

In short

Debiasing requires evidence that an intervention improves a specified judgment or behaviour. Learning a bias name is different from changing a procedure. Immediate improvement, persistence, and transfer to new tasks are separate claims. Training, feedback, group arrangements, and environmental changes can target different mechanisms. The question is what works, for whom, in which setting, and by which measure.

Layer 1Re-learn

Specify the improvement

Imagine a fictional analyst estimating delivery times. A workshop makes her better at naming anchoring. A comparison form makes her estimates more accurate. A feedback record helps her set intervals that contain the outcome at the stated rate. These are different results, and only the first concerns recognising terminology.

Bazerman distinguishes awareness of decision biases from procedures and environmental changes intended to improve decisions. Brownstein similarly discusses interventions aimed at changing implicit associations and interventions aimed at controlling their effects. Their targets differ, and boundaries between the categories can overlap.

For the delivery analyst, choose the outcome before assessing the intervention. Faster completion might matter, but speed alone does not establish improved accuracy. Better confidence ratings might matter, but they do not by themselves establish better central estimates.

An original study

Mellers and colleagues’ geopolitical forecasting study tests training and group arrangements in a tournament. In Year 1, participants are randomly assigned to combinations of training and group conditions. The paper reports improvements in forecasting accuracy from training and teaming across the reported years.

This provides evidence for interventions in a particular setting with a particular recruited population. It does not show that a short lesson removes every cognitive bias or transfers automatically to every everyday decision.

Sources for this part: Bazerman 2026, Fixing Our Decisions; Brownstein 2025, § 4.2, 4.2.2; Mellers et al. 2014, PDF p. 4, PDF p. 5.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Selection is not the same as intervention

The study’s elite Year 2 teams are selected by prior performance. Their good forecasts matter, but comparing them with other participants does not isolate a pure effect of assigning arbitrary people to an elite team. Ability, selection, engagement, and social arrangement must be distinguished.1

For the fictional analyst, choosing the best performers and finding that their next estimates are good would not establish the effect of the workshop. A suitable comparison should address the alternative explanation that they were already stronger forecasters.

Persistence, transfer, and consequences

Brownstein’s review identifies concerns about the duration, feasibility, and broader consequences of implicit-bias interventions. A changed laboratory measure need not establish durable behavioural improvement or repair an institution. That caution is relevant to how intervention claims are formulated, though results from one bias domain cannot simply be transferred to every other.2

A delivery-time form can have costs too. It might improve typical cases while obstructing exceptional ones, or make unsupported numerical precision look authoritative. Assessment should include the intended use conditions and important errors, rather than report only the most flattering outcome.

A practical proposal can still be worthwhile before broad validation if its rationale and uncertainty are explicit. Recording estimates before outcomes, preserving reasons for revisions, and checking performance against suitable cases are examples for the fictional analyst. They are not guarantees of a bias-free mind.

Read forecasting for the original tournament’s design and replication for testing improvement beyond an initial finding. Those pages connect a remedy with the evidence it actually needs.

Layer 4Sources

Where to go next

Start with Mellers and colleagues’ original study, keeping random assignment distinct from performance tracking. Bazerman provides introductory advice; Brownstein supplies a secondary discussion of intervention limits in implicit bias. The page does not claim that every attractive procedure has already been experimentally validated.3
