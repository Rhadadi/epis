Deeper study · Chapter 14

The Psychology of Reasoning: How Minds Actually Work

For when a section didn't click, or left you wanting more: each page explains the idea again, tells the full story, fills in what the chapter leaves out, and gives the sources.

Francisco Goya, The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters, plate 43 of Los Caprichos, 1799

← Back to Chapter 14

  • Short study · 3 minWhy psychology matters to epistemologyEpistemology evaluates belief and inquiry; psychology helps explain how people perform them. Describing a common response does not establish that it is correct.
  • Full study · 13 minDual-process theoriesDual-process theories distinguish kinds of cognitive processing, often described as intuitive and reflective. A quick answer and a checked answer can arise differently.
  • Short study · 3 minHeuristics and biasesA heuristic simplifies a judgment or decision. A bias is a systematic departure from a relevant standard, not just any isolated mistake.
  • Short study · 3 minConfirmation bias and myside biasConfirmation bias concerns favouring evidence or tests that support a hypothesis. Myside bias concerns favouring one’s own position in producing or evaluating reasons.
  • Full study · 13 minMotivated reasoningMotivated reasoning occurs when a desired outcome shapes how someone handles reasons and evidence. It can involve sophisticated argument rather than simply ignoring the facts.
  • Short study · 3 minBelief perseverance and biased assimilationBelief perseverance concerns retaining a belief after its original grounds are undermined. Biased assimilation concerns interpreting evidence through an existing position.
  • Short study · 3 minFluency and the illusory truth effectProcessing fluency concerns how easy information is to process. Ease and familiarity can affect judgments without supplying new evidence for a claim.
  • Short study · 3 minOverconfidence and the illusion of explanatory depthOverconfidence concerns a mismatch between confidence and what performance or evidence supports. The mismatch can involve an estimated score, a comparison with others, or an interval stated too narrowly.
  • Short study · 2 minThe Dunning-Kruger effect and its criticsThe Dunning–Kruger discussion concerns relations between performance and self-assessment. A pattern of larger overestimation among low performers does not uniquely establish a metacognitive explanation.
  • Short study · 3 minIdentity-protective cognitionIdentity can give a factual judgment social significance beyond its truth. Protecting one’s standing may then affect how evidence is handled.
  • Short study · 3 minMindreading: how we attribute knowledgeMindreading means interpreting other people in terms of beliefs, knowledge, desires, and intentions. It involves ordinary inference rather than literal access to another mind.
  • Short study · 3 minThe argumentative theory of reasoningMercier and Sperber propose that reasoning’s primary function is producing and evaluating arguments in communication. The theory offers an explanation of both persuasive skill and one-sided argument production.
  • Short study · 3 minEcological rationalityEcological rationality assesses a decision procedure in relation to its environment. A simple rule can exploit a useful regularity and fail when that regularity changes.
  • Short study · 2 minReplication and the psychology of psychologyResearch about reasoning needs the same scrutiny as other empirical research. Repeating an analysis, repeating an experiment, and testing a hypothesis in a new setting are different achievements.
  • Short study · 3 minDebiasing: what worksDebiasing requires evidence that an intervention improves a specified judgment or behaviour. Learning a bias name is different from changing a procedure.
  • Short study · 3 minThe scout mindsetScout mindset is Julia Galef’s name for trying to see things as they are rather than as one wishes them to be. It directs attention to testing assumptions and noticing mistakes.
  • Short study · 2 minForecasting and superforecastersForecasting makes a probability claim answerable to a later outcome. A good record needs clearly resolved questions and a consistent scoring rule.
