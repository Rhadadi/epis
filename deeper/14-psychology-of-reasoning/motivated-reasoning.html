In short

Motivated reasoning occurs when a desired outcome shapes how someone handles reasons and evidence. It can involve sophisticated argument rather than simply ignoring the facts. Disagreement or error alone does not establish this mechanism. A diagnosis needs to identify the asymmetric treatment and consider alternative explanations. Improving inquiry means changing the work that reasons do, not merely asking people to think harder.

Layer 1Re-learn

The same evidence under two standards

Imagine a fictional engineer reviewing two proposed machines. She helped design one. When its test result is favourable, she accepts it after a quick look; when the rival’s result is favourable, she demands repeated testing, investigates every uncertainty, and rejects small imperfections. Both results might contain real limitations. The problem is the unexplained difference in how the limitations count.

Motivated reasoning can recruit actual reasons for a preferred conclusion. Mercier and Sperber’s review discusses experiments in which participants evaluate evidence differently depending on whether it supports an outcome they favour. They distinguish that process from desire simply producing belief with no justificatory work.

Motivation is not always distortion

The engineer’s desire for a safe machine could motivate more careful inquiry. Her interest in the design does not prove that she is biased. We need to distinguish motivation to investigate from motivation that selectively changes the treatment of the evidence. An unfavourable answer is not proof of impartiality, either: someone can distort evidence toward a feared conclusion.

The self-deception literature discusses both desire- and anxiety-related distortions and disputes whether they require an intention to deceive oneself. Those questions caution against reducing the phenomenon to conscious dishonesty.

Sources for this part: Mercier and Sperber 2011, PDF p. 10; Deweese-Boyd 2023, § 3.1.

Layer 2The full story

An explanatory question, rather than an accusation

Motivated reasoning is a proposed explanation of a pattern in inquiry. The investigator asks whether a motivation affects which evidence is sought, how it is interpreted, which objections receive attention, and what standard ends the investigation. Merely discovering a false belief does not answer that question. A person can be mistaken because a source was unreliable, a task was misunderstood, or relevant information was unavailable. Conversely, a conclusion can be true even though the process favouring it was distorted. The explanatory target is the handling of reasons.1

That framing prevents the label from becoming a way to win a disagreement without answering it. If the fictional engineer identifies a genuine flaw in the rival’s test, the flaw still deserves assessment. Her investment in her own design does not make the criticism false. The important comparison asks whether she recognises the same flaw when it appears in her preferred test, whether its relevance differs for a defensible reason, and whether her motivation explains the asymmetry better than the alternatives.

Reasons can be part of the mechanism

Mercier and Sperber review motivated reasoning as an instance of looking for arguments that defend opinions one may have to justify. Their theoretical interpretation connects this with an argumentative function of reasoning. It is important to separate the reviewed observations from that interpretation: biased treatment of evidence can support a finding without uniquely establishing an evolutionary account of why reasoning exists. Their article supplies both a review and a theory, and each should be assessed at its own level.2

Read the original

Justification can serve a preferred opinion

All these experiments demonstrate that people sometimes look for reasons to justify an opinion they are eager to uphold.

3. The authors’ word *sometimes* matters. The passage does not say that every argument is rationalisation or that truth-directed reasoning is impossible.

The engineer can supply a detailed explanation of why the rival’s sample was too small. Detail by itself does not show impartiality, because producing detail might be the task her preference has assigned to reasoning. Yet a sparse explanation is not automatically more honest. The appropriate assessment compares the standards, the available evidence, and the inferential contribution of each objection. Neither verbal fluency nor discomfort provides a shortcut to that evaluation.

A reviewed experiment and the limits of the inference

The article reviews Ditto and colleagues’ experiments involving a fictitious medical test. Participants receive information about a test result and have reasons to prefer a healthy outcome. In one version, the information about false positives is used differently depending on whether it helps reject or accept the relevant conclusion. Another gives participants an opportunity to identify events in their history that might undermine the test. The review uses these patterns to illustrate the recruitment of reasons in support of a preferred conclusion.4

This is a secondary account of those experiments: the original Ditto papers were not independently read for this page. The reviewed result should therefore be stated at its reported scope, without adding a numerical effect size, a universal prevalence claim, or a guarantee that the same mechanism explains every real medical disagreement. The evidence is useful because the study contrasts ways of handling similar information under different motivations. That structure is more informative than simply observing that people dislike bad news.

In an actual diagnostic setting, differences in interpretation could also arise from prior information, misunderstanding, trust in the procedure, or different consequences of a decision. The fictional engineer similarly might have real information about her own machine that she lacks about the rival. A controlled comparison tries to separate those possibilities. A practical allegation often cannot do so cleanly. The sensible response is to identify the observed asymmetry and investigate it, rather than jumping from a disliked conclusion to a complete psychological story.

Search, interpretation, and stopping

The mechanism can be divided conceptually into three stages. Search determines what becomes available for judgment. Interpretation determines how the information bears on the claim. Stopping determines when the person regards inquiry as sufficient. This division is an explanatory aid, not a claim that the brain contains three separate modules. It helps locate what the engineer might need to change. If she never requests the rival’s favourable results, correcting the interpretation of a result she has already seen will not address the missing search.

Likewise, a person can gather a broad selection of evidence yet treat objections differently. Or she can apply the same inferential rule at every step but stop as soon as a desired answer appears, while continuing indefinitely when the answer is unwelcome. These are different possible failures. A useful intervention specifies which stage it targets and what observation would show improvement. Without that specification, an instruction to consider all the evidence can remain an aspiration that the person believes she already fulfils.

The theoretical literature on self-deception discusses selective responsiveness and the costs associated with different errors. It includes accounts on which motivation affects the threshold for accepting an unwelcome conclusion, without requiring an explicit intention to deceive oneself. Critics ask whether such accounts sufficiently explain why motivation distorts belief in some cases but not others. The selectivity question therefore remains part of the theory rather than an inconvenient exception to suppress.5

Desired conclusions and feared conclusions

Wanting a proposition to be true is one motivational possibility. Anxiety or fear can also influence a judgment. Deweese-Boyd’s survey discusses non-intentionalist accounts involving desires, emotions, and anxiety, and disagreements about the attitudes needed for self-deception. These approaches resist the idea that every motivated distortion must be a pleasant belief adopted for comfort. A disturbing conclusion can become the object of persistent, selective attention.6

Imagine a second fictional reviewer who fears that she overlooked a defect. She might discount repeated independent checks and preserve a suspicion through increasingly speculative possibilities. That could be a motivational distortion, but it could also reflect a justified concern about a limitation all the checks share. The distinction requires examining the grounds. A comforting answer is not necessarily biased, and an alarming answer is not necessarily impartial. The emotional valence of the conclusion leaves the epistemic question open.

This also explains why a motivation need not determine the result whenever it is present. A strong wish for the machine to succeed could coexist with an even stronger concern about harming a customer or being wrong publicly. The literature considers error-cost accounts of such selectivity and objections to their completeness. Describing a person’s motive is therefore only the beginning of an explanation; one must show how it affected the relevant process in that situation.7

Biased assimilation and perseverance

Mercier and Sperber review work in which supporters and opponents of a policy evaluate conflicting studies differently. In their account of Lord and colleagues’ experiment, similar methodological strengths and weaknesses are treated according to whether the conclusion fits the participant’s position. They discuss this as biased assimilation or evaluation. The relevant distinction is between responding differently because the evidence differs and responding differently because the conclusion is favoured.8

Their review also discusses belief perseverance after feedback is revealed to be bogus. A later experiment distinguishes favourable from unfavourable feedback and examines whether participants incorporate the debriefing differently. The review interprets the asymmetry as evidence of motivated reasoning rather than mere inertia. This is a reported experimental contrast, not a rule that every retained belief after a correction is self-deceptive. The original studies are mediated here through the target article.9

For the engineer, an initial report might have generated a plausible causal story about her design. If the report is withdrawn, she must distinguish reasons that depended on it from independent reasons that remain. Maintaining the conclusion can be legitimate if genuinely independent support survives. What would be problematic is treating the story generated by the withdrawn report as new evidence merely because it now feels familiar and coherent. A correction of one premise requires tracing its role in the argument, not mechanically reversing every associated conclusion.

Myside bias and motivated reasoning

Myside bias concerns favouring one’s existing position when generating or evaluating reasons. Motivated reasoning offers an explanation involving a goal or concern that affects the process. The two descriptions can overlap, but the explanatory claim is stronger than identifying the pattern. Mercier and Sperber discuss the search for supporting arguments in production and the evaluation of arguments whose conclusions a person already favours. Their argumentative interpretation gives one account of why the pattern may arise.10

The engineer’s existing belief that her design is better could direct search even before she feels a strong personal stake. Conversely, a stake in a desired outcome might shape inquiry about a question on which she has not yet formed a belief. Separating prior belief from motivation helps identify what a comparison should hold fixed. It also prevents a circular explanation in which we infer the desire solely from the favoured conclusion and then use that inferred desire to explain the conclusion.

Reflection is not an automatic antidote

The review describes bolstering and overconfidence arising when people generate reasons supporting an answer. It also discusses conditions in which considering reasons against an answer reduces overconfidence. The distinction concerns what reflection is directed to do. It does not justify the generalisation that educated or intelligent people are always more biased, nor that reflection usually makes things worse. A theory needs to connect the particular task, motivation, and outcome.11

The fictional engineer could spend another hour defending her design and become more confident without learning anything new about its performance. Alternatively, she could use the hour to identify what result would count against it and check whether the same criterion applies to the rival. The duration is identical; the epistemic task differs. This is why dual-process theories cannot be converted into a simple promise that slower thought is better. The reasons produced and the comparisons permitted need assessment.

The social audience

An audience can change what a person needs reasons for. Mercier and Sperber’s argumentative theory treats preparation to defend an opinion as a route through which reasoning may bolster it. Deweese-Boyd’s discussion of collective self-deception distinguishes people who are merely mistaken together from social arrangements in which others help sustain a motivationally distorted belief. These are theoretical approaches to social mechanisms, not a claim that all group agreement is deceptive.12

In the fictional workplace, the engineer might expect her colleagues to applaud defending the team’s design and punish acknowledgment of a flaw. An independent review with explicit criteria could change that audience pressure. But simply adding more colleagues to the discussion might amplify it. The relevant question is what contribution the additional people make: new evidence, independent criticism, social reassurance, or a reason to maintain a public position. Counting participants alone does not identify that contribution.

Diagnosis without mindreading

A comparison for the fictional review

Question Evidence that would help
Are the standards asymmetric? Comparable treatment of the same methodological limitation
Does the reviewer know more about one machine? A record of relevant information available before judgment
Does preference predict the asymmetry? A suitable comparison with preference varied or controlled
Does the conclusion survive correction? An argument showing what independent support remains
Does a proposed safeguard help? Performance assessed against criteria fixed in advance

The table is a proposed diagnostic structure, not a validated instrument. Its purpose is to make the inference from behaviour to motivation explicit. A person’s private state may remain uncertain even when the comparison reveals a procedural problem worth fixing.

This matters because public accusations can change the inquiry itself. If the reviewer is told she is a biased person before any specific asymmetry is identified, she may reasonably demand grounds for the allegation. The discussion can then become a contest over character instead of the machines. Pointing to a particular discrepancy allows a more tractable response: explain why the standards differ, correct the discrepancy, or investigate the missing information. A procedural remedy can be justified even when the complete psychological diagnosis remains unsettled.

Interventions and what their evidence establishes

The forecasting study by Mellers and colleagues provides a useful example of testing an intervention rather than merely recommending a good attitude. It combines probability training, different group arrangements, and performance tracking in a geopolitical tournament. Year 1 assigns participants to training and group conditions at random; later comparisons also include teams selected by prior performance. The study reports improvements in forecasting accuracy for training and teaming. It does not show that one intervention eliminates all motivational distortions in every context.13

The distinction between random assignment and selection is especially important. A comparison involving high-performing forecasters can demonstrate useful predictive performance without isolating the effect of belonging to an elite group. An intervention that improves one task also needs evidence of persistence and transfer before becoming a general prescription. Brownstein’s review of implicit-bias interventions makes those limitations explicit in a related domain. The evidence should match the claim made for the remedy.14

Procedures with an intelligible target

For the engineer, one proposed remedy is to specify failure criteria before opening either test report. Another is to have a reviewer unaware of the preferred machine apply the same checklist. A third is to preserve reasons for changing the criteria, so that a legitimate revision can be distinguished from opportunistic adjustment. These proposals target comparison and stopping. They are not claims that blinding, checklists, or precommitment always work or that judgment can be eliminated.

The costs also matter. A rigid criterion can become obsolete if new evidence reveals a genuine flaw in the method. An anonymous review can conceal information relevant to reliability. An adversarial critic can misunderstand the task. The objective is answerable inquiry: changes should have reasons that remain applicable when the favoured conclusion is reversed. Improving a procedure involves checking these trade-offs, not declaring that a preferred method has removed every source of bias.

A better endpoint

The engineer should finish with an account of the machines that another reviewer can inspect: the evidence compared, limitations recognised, standards applied, and uncertainties left open. She may still choose her own design. What matters is whether the choice survives the same scrutiny applied to the rival and whether the explanation depends on evidence rather than a protected preference. An unwelcome decision can be motivationally distorted too, so its emotional difficulty is not proof of good inquiry.

Motivated reasoning is consequently a warning about a possible role of reasons, rather than a reason to abandon reasoning. The proper response is to make the role observable and correctable. State the claim, compare the standards, identify independent support, and test proposed safeguards at the task they are meant to improve. The diagnosis may remain partial. The inquiry can nevertheless become better when a specific asymmetry is removed and relevant criticism receives a route to affect the result.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Distinctions that constrain the label

Self-deception, wishful thinking, motivated reasoning, and collective error are related without being identical. The literature disputes intentions, beliefs, selectivity, and responsibility. Those disagreements should prevent the label from becoming a universal explanation of people who resist our conclusions.15

Read identity-protective cognition for the social mechanism, belief perseverance for correction, and debiasing for evaluating interventions. The ethics of belief adds the separate question of responsibility.
