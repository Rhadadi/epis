In short

Dual-process theories distinguish kinds of cognitive processing, often described as intuitive and reflective. A quick answer and a checked answer can arise differently. The familiar System 1 and System 2 labels summarise a family of proposals rather than identify two little thinkers in the brain. The features associated with each label need separate evidence. Reflection can correct an impression, but it can also help defend an error.

Layer 1Re-learn

An answer that arrives and an answer that is checked

Consider an invented pricing problem. Two services cost £44 together; the more expensive costs £40 more than the cheaper. A tempting response is £4 for the cheaper service. But then the expensive one costs £44, making the total £48. Writing the cheaper price as x gives x + (x + 40) = 44, so it is £2.

This example distinguishes the arrival of a plausible answer from a procedure for checking it. It is not a reported experiment and does not show how many people would make the mistake. A dual-process interpretation would ask what generated the first response, what prompted checking, and how the competing response was selected. The labels do not answer those questions on their own.

Bazerman presents the familiar System 1/System 2 contrast as a way of understanding intuitive and deliberative decision-making. He also recognises that ordinary intuition is often adequate and that deliberating over every minor choice would be impractical. The point is selective control, not replacing all spontaneous cognition with calculation.

Different distinctions can travel together

Fast, automatic, unconscious, effortless, associative, and intuitive are not synonyms. Nor are slow, deliberate, conscious, effortful, and correct. Mercier and Sperber criticise the vagueness of bundling contrasting features together and offer their own distinction between intuitive and reflective inference. A good explanation states which feature it means.

Sources for this part: Bazerman 2026, Fixing Our Decisions; Mercier and Sperber 2009, PDF p. 3.

Layer 2The full story

Why a two-process picture is attractive

Psychology must explain both successful everyday judgment and errors on tasks where an apparently simple answer violates the relevant standard. A single description of people as excellent or hopeless reasoners misses that variation. Dual-process approaches propose that different kinds of processing contribute to different performances. Mercier and Sperber introduce their discussion against this background, describing how intuitive responses and controlled reasoning can sometimes compete. Their account also stresses variation across problems, contexts, and people.1

The attraction is explanatory rather than merely verbal. In the invented pricing problem, the first response can be readily available even though a short calculation defeats it. We want to explain why availability differs from correctness, why someone sometimes notices the conflict, and why checking sometimes occurs. A theory that predicts those differences could improve on simply calling one answer intuitive after seeing it. The danger is that the vocabulary can remain a redescription unless it produces independently testable expectations about the processing involved.

From useful labels to specified mechanisms

Bazerman’s overview groups fast, automatic, effortless, implicit, and emotional processing under System 1 and slower, conscious, effortful, explicit, and logical decision-making under System 2. That is a useful introductory contrast, especially for showing why a first impression should sometimes be examined. But a collection of common associations should not be treated as a guarantee that every feature is present whenever one appears. The theoretical task is to say what distinguishes the processes and how the proposed features relate.2

A caution within the literature

There is, however, some vagueness in the way the two systems are characterized.

3. Mercier and Sperber raise this objection before proposing an alternative. The short quotation comes from the author-hosted chapter, not a popular paraphrase of the theory.

Suppose a fictional musician immediately notices an incorrect note in a familiar sequence. The judgment is fast. Whether it is automatic, consciously experienced, verbalised, emotionally charged, or reliable are further questions. Conversely, slowly searching for a lost word does not automatically involve logical comparison. These examples make no empirical claim about a particular population. They show why speed alone cannot define every other feature. A proposed classification needs to say which dimensions are constitutive and which are merely frequent companions.

Systems, types, and levels of explanation

Mercier and Sperber discuss the proposal to speak of types of process rather than two systems. Their own account works within a modular view, where many specialised mechanisms produce intuitive inferences and reflective inference has a distinctive relationship to representations of reasons. A distinction between two broad types can therefore coexist with a more plural architecture. This is an important alternative to imagining one intuitive engine and one reflective engine responsible for everything in their respective columns.4

The level of explanation matters. An account can classify tasks, describe a person’s conscious activity, or propose mechanisms operating below that level. Those claims need not coincide. A person can know that she checked a calculation without knowing the cognitive processes that made the checking possible. Similarly, identifying a broad class of controlled activities does not by itself locate a unique anatomical unit. A theory must explain the relation between its psychological descriptions and its proposed mechanisms rather than allowing the familiar labels to supply that relation by implication.

Intuitive inference is not necessarily a guess

In Mercier and Sperber’s account, intuitive inference produces a conclusion without the person attending to the reasons that justify the modification of belief. Reflective inference involves representing reasons to accept the conclusion. That distinction concerns the relation to reasons, rather than the simple difference between correct and incorrect outcomes. Their account treats many specialised mechanisms as inferential and argues that the mechanisms can operate without conscious control even when their outputs become available to awareness.5

This can be illustrated without endorsing every part of their architecture. The fictional musician may hear that the sequence is wrong before she can articulate the harmonic relation. An audience member might instead compare notes against a written pattern. Both could reach a correct judgment, and either could fail under relevant changes in conditions. What distinguishes the proposed routes is not the truth value alone. The example therefore avoids defining intuitive as irrational: that would make the theory’s verdict true by definition rather than allowing the reliability of the route to be assessed.

Reflection can contain intuitive components

Mercier and Sperber propose that the production and evaluation of arguments themselves involve intuitive inferential mechanisms. A person can consciously entertain an argument while the processes that make its steps compelling remain opaque. They distinguish the represented derivation from the underlying cognitive activity. On their proposal, reflection is not a wholly separate world without intuitive processing; the relation between intuitive and reflective inference is asymmetric and layered. This is their theoretical account, rather than an established anatomical finding.6

In the pricing example, writing an equation gives an explicit reason to reject £4. Yet the person may immediately recognise that adding the same amount to both sides preserves equality. The consciously accessible derivation still depends on capacities that are not each separately defended. That observation does not undermine the calculation. It shows why a demand that every element of reflection be itself reflectively justified would generate a further problem. The relation between intuitive competence and explicit checking deserves explanation rather than an ideal of completely transparent thought.

Association and proposition: another distinction

Brownstein describes models of implicit cognition that distinguish associative from propositional processing. In the Reflective-Impulsive Model and Associative-Propositional Evaluation model, associative processes concern activated associations, while propositional processes involve validation related to truth and consistency. These proposals resemble some dual-process distinctions, but they are not simply claims that people possess two attitudes or two separate representations of every object. The target is a distinction in processing and its role in evaluative responses.7

The entry also separates learning, storage, and expression. Evidence for distinguishing processes in one of those roles does not automatically support the same separation in another. That caution is useful beyond implicit bias. A measure of how quickly an answer is expressed is not a direct measurement of how the information was originally acquired or stored. An experiment needs a bridge from its observation to the mechanism it claims to test, with alternatives considered. Otherwise an attractive process label can hide a gap in the inference.8

What an error can and cannot diagnose

A wrong answer provides evidence about a performance relative to a specified standard. It does not uniquely identify its cause. The person could misunderstand the question, apply the wrong procedure, lack relevant information, fail to notice a conflict, or experience pressure to finish quickly. Wheeler’s discussion of bounded rationality warns that apparent departures from a normative standard can depend on how participants represent the task and how information is presented. Those possibilities belong in the explanation of an error, rather than being dismissed as excuses after the fact.9

The argument, step by step

From performance to a process claim

  1. A specified task has a relevant standard for evaluating the response.
  2. Observed responses differ from that standard in a systematic way.
  3. A proposed cognitive mechanism predicts that pattern and relevant changes under controlled conditions.
  4. Alternative explanations do not account for those observations equally well.
  5. The observations therefore provide support for the proposed mechanism.

This reconstruction identifies what a process explanation would need. Premise 2 alone establishes neither the mechanism nor a universal judgment about human rationality. Premises 3 and 4 carry the explanatory comparison.

For the fictional pricing problem, observing an answer of £4 does not prove that one system failed to override another. We would need further evidence about comprehension, the first response, awareness of discrepancy, checking, and subsequent selection. Even then, different theoretical accounts might fit the pattern. This does not make process research pointless. It gives the research a precise question: which manipulation or observation could distinguish accounts that otherwise predict the same answer? Merely giving the error a name cannot answer that question.

Content and response format

Wheeler’s discussion of probability tasks shows why the format of information matters to their interpretation. A response to an everyday question about what seems likely may not express the same representation as a response to a specified frequency problem. Changes in wording, sampling, and background assumptions can affect what task the person actually performs. An error measured against the experimenter’s intended task therefore requires an account of how that task was understood. This is not a general excuse for incorrect calculation; it is a reason to check the link between instruction, representation, and response.10

In the invented pricing case, a person might misread the £40 as the expensive service’s price rather than the difference between prices. Explaining the phrase could correct the answer without demonstrating a transition between two processing systems. Another person could read it correctly but neglect the total-price constraint. A third could do the calculation and make a transcription error. The same written answer might arise through these different routes. A process theory becomes more informative when it predicts which clarification or checking step should affect which route, rather than assuming that every incorrect answer shares one origin.

What would count as a useful correction?

A practical intervention can change accuracy, speed, confidence, or the ability to explain a response. These outcomes are distinct. Suppose a fictional comparison form makes the buyer more articulate but leaves the selected supplier and its underlying justification unchanged. That might improve communication while failing to improve the decision. Suppose another form slows the buyer without making the criteria more consistent. More deliberation would then be an observation, not evidence that the intended correction occurred. The outcome needs to be tied to the particular epistemic failure the form was designed to address.

The same discipline applies to the theory. A model may be revised because its defining features do not explain some observation, yet retain a useful distinction at another level. Rejecting a crude two-engine picture would not by itself show that conscious checking and spontaneous inference are indistinguishable. Conversely, demonstrating a difference in response time would not secure every claim about a two-system architecture. The explanatory question is which distinctions earn their place by accounting for observations and guiding further comparisons. A vocabulary becomes useful through that work, rather than through the confidence with which its columns are presented.

Resources and the relevant standard

Wheeler distinguishes descriptive theories of what people do, normative standards for assessing performance, and prescriptive advice that takes human capabilities into account. He also explains the costs of operating decision procedures and the trade-off between effort and accuracy. These distinctions prevent an ideal of unlimited calculation from becoming practical advice. An accurate method can be too costly to use in every setting; a cheap procedure can be appropriate without achieving the best imaginable result.11

Our fictional planner might check a large contractual payment carefully while rounding a minor grocery estimate. That difference can be reasonable if the saved effort and acceptable error are specified. It does not show that truth matters only when money is at stake, nor that practical costs change the correctness of arithmetic. The normative result, the procedure available to the person, and the decision about how much checking to undertake are separate. Confusing them makes both criticism and advice less useful.

Heuristics do not belong automatically to one side

Wheeler contrasts two traditions studying heuristics. In one, shortcuts help explain characteristic judgment errors; in another, heuristics are specified algorithms whose performance depends on environmental conditions. He notes that Gigerenzer does not endorse classifying heuristics as necessarily non-deliberative System 1 processing. A person can consciously use a simple rule. Consequently, simple versus complex is another contrast that should not be treated as identical to intuitive versus reflective.12

Imagine a fictional buyer using a written rule to compare three suppliers: eliminate those that fail a required safety standard, then choose the least expensive remaining option. The rule deliberately ignores some available details. That can be an explicit heuristic, not an impulsive response. Whether it works depends on the problem and the ignored information. If reliability differs importantly among the remaining suppliers, the procedure may be inadequate. The appropriate criticism identifies the missing decision-relevant information instead of merely announcing that a shortcut was used.

Reflection can defend a preferred answer

The idea that reflective thinking always corrects bias is too strong. Mercier and Sperber review motivated reasoning, where people recruit arguments to support a preferred view, and reinterpret it within their argumentative theory. Their review includes differential scrutiny of favourable and unfavourable evidence and the bolstering of opinions. A conscious series of reasons can therefore be part of an error-maintaining process. The literature reviewed in that article supplies examples; it does not establish that every act of reflection has this character.13

The fictional buyer might use deliberation to justify choosing a friend’s company. He can calculate accurately, identify real advantages, and still apply a different standard to the alternatives. Additional effort could strengthen the preferred conclusion without improving the comparison. The remedy would concern the task of reflection: state comparable criteria, seek relevant objections, and preserve reasons for rejection. Asking him simply to think harder does not specify what should change. The motivated reasoning study examines this problem in depth.

Environments can support checking

Bazerman discusses changing decision environments rather than relying solely on people to overcome biases. Brownstein similarly distinguishes proposals aimed at changing associations from those aimed at controlling their expression, while noting uncertainty about persistence and practical efficacy. These approaches illustrate why an individual’s performance depends on available support. A theory of processing should leave room for tools, formats, time, and social arrangements rather than placing every burden on an internal switch between two labels.14

For the buyer, a comparison form could display the same criteria for every supplier and require a reason when a criterion is waived. This is a proposed safeguard for the fictional case. Its effectiveness would need assessment. It makes the intended checking operation visible and reduces reliance on remembering to be fair at the moment of judgment. Whether the form improves outcomes is different from whether it makes the person slower, more confident, or more verbally elaborate. A good evaluation selects the relevant outcome in advance.

A disciplined use of the vocabulary

The two-process picture is most useful when it directs attention to a question that can be investigated. What made the response available? Was a discrepancy detected? What evidence or procedure could check it? How did the person decide whether to use that procedure? Which features of the environment supported or obstructed it? These questions are more informative than identifying every good answer with System 2 and every bad answer with System 1. They make a process account answerable to independent observations.

They also preserve the difference between a model and advice. A model proposes how cognition works; advice proposes how a person should handle a task. Advice can be valuable even when several models explain its benefit. Conversely, a well-supported distinction in processing does not automatically establish that one type should always dominate. The relevant standard remains the quality of the inquiry or decision in its conditions. Slowness, effort, and self-reported rationality are possible observations, not substitutes for that evaluation.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the familiar table simplifies

The chapter’s paired columns offer an introduction. The fuller literature includes disputes about bundles of features, modular architectures, associative and propositional processing, and the classification of heuristics. These should not be compressed into a single discovery of two brain compartments.15

Connect this page with ecological rationality, debiasing, and intellectual virtues. They add questions about environments, interventions, and the aims of inquiry that speed alone cannot answer.
