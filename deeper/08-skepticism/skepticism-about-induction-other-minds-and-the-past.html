Skepticism about induction, other minds, and the past

In short

Future events, other people’s minds, and the past present different epistemic challenges. They need not share one skeptical argument or one solution. An induction problem questions an extension beyond observed cases; an other-minds problem questions access to another subject’s experience. A past-directed challenge concerns the relation of present traces to earlier events. Comparing them works best when these differences remain explicit.

Layer 1Re-learn

Extending beyond what was observed

You have seen a machine operate successfully many times and expect it to work tomorrow. Past success does not deductively entail the next success. The skeptical question asks what supports the extension, rather than merely noting that a breakdown is possible.

Comesaña and Klein’s Humean pattern challenges knowledge through inferential but non-deductive evidence while allowing direct experience and deduction. They use the label as a contemporary taxonomy, with an explicit warning about historical accuracy.

The detailed debate belongs in Hume’s problem of induction. Here the contrast matters: this skeptical challenge can leave current perceptual knowledge intact. It need not deny that the machine is operating now.

Another person’s experience

You observe a person withdrawing a hand from a hot surface and reporting pain. You do not undergo that person’s pain in the way you undergo your own. An argument from analogy connects similarities in body and behaviour with similarities in experience. Avramides discusses Mill’s version and objections concerning its starting point, its dependence on one’s own case, and the checking of its conclusion.

An explanatory approach instead treats mindedness as an explanation of the observed behaviour. Avramides presents this as an alternative to the analogical route. Its strength depends on the explanation’s merits, rather than on obtaining the other person’s experience as one’s own.

The epistemic question should be distinguished from the conceptual one: how can I think about another person’s mental states at all? Avramides explains why attempts to solve the epistemic problem can encounter this further difficulty. Evidence and possession of the relevant concept are different conditions.

Sources for this part: Comesaña and Klein 2026, § 2; Avramides 2023, § 1.1; Avramides 2023, § 1.2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Present traces and a past event

Consider an invented hypothesis that all present records and apparent memories were created together recently. By design it reproduces current appearances. Like an experience-matching vat hypothesis, it raises questions about whether matching appearance requires equivalent evidence and whether knowledge requires independent exclusion of the alternative1.

This radical construction differs from a specific correction to a memory. A dated receipt conflicting with a recollection supplies a discriminating reason to revise that recollection. A hypothesis reproducing every current receipt and recollection creates a more general challenge to the relation between traces and events.

Memory theory also introduces a finer distinction. A recollection can faithfully reproduce an earlier experience while that experience misrepresented the event. Authenticity and truth are therefore different kinds of accuracy. Assessing knowledge of the past must attend to both the original encounter and later remembering2.

Avoiding one universal demand

These domains encourage different questions. Does repeated observation support projection? Does behaviour disclose mental life? Does retained information appropriately connect to an earlier event? A demand to experience every target directly would reject many familiar routes to knowledge before examining how they work.

Conversely, identifying an inferential route does not automatically establish its reliability. Analogy, explanation, and memory each require an account of their conditions and failure modes. The comparison should guide investigation rather than license confidence by analogy alone.

Connections

Inference separates input support from the transition. Memory examines retention and reconstruction. Responses to skepticism identifies assumptions shared by experience-matching challenges. The next chapter develops induction in its own right.

Layer 4Sources

Where to go next

Begin with Avramides’s comparison of analogy and explanation. Michaelian and Sutton clarify how remembered experience can differ from the past event. Comesaña and Klein provide the broader taxonomy and discussion of symmetry. Their combined contribution is a map of distinct problems, not a proof that one argument settles all three3.
