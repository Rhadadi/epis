In short

Responses to skepticism disagree about what knowledge requires, what evidence includes, and what an argument must accomplish. Some preserve ordinary knowledge by rejecting the skeptic’s account of error; others restrict closure or explain changes in knowledge language. Hinge approaches question whether basic commitments require the same support as ordinary hypotheses. An argument can preserve knowledge while failing to persuade a committed skeptic. Comparing responses requires identifying their exact target and cost.

Layer 1Re-learn

Which claim is being challenged?

The closure argument combines an ordinary proposition, its implication that an incompatible skeptical scenario is false, and the alleged inability to know that the scenario is false. A response can question different parts of this package. There is no need to identify every answer with a new piece of evidence that the skeptic already accepts.

Suppose you know that a kettle is boiling. One answer gives priority to that ordinary knowledge. Another distinguishes the kind of error that would undermine it from a remote constructed possibility. A third explains why a statement about knowing is assessed differently in ordinary and philosophical conversations. These are different strategies rather than interchangeable ways of reassuring yourself.

Three standards of success

An answer might show that the ordinary belief can be knowledge. It might provide new support for rejecting a skeptical hypothesis. Or it might convince an interlocutor who doubts the starting point. Those achievements can come apart. A closure relation need not guarantee that a particular argument independently transmits warrant to its conclusion.

This is why simply calling a response question-begging can be insufficient. The objection must specify what the answer was supposed to do. A proof offered as independent certification faces one challenge; an account explaining why a person already knows faces another.

A response can expose an assumption

Matching experiences are often treated as matching evidence. But that step requires a theory of evidence. An account on which evidence consists of what a person knows can allow different evidence in the veridical and deceptive cases. The skeptical premise cannot then be derived from shared experience alone.

Sources for this part: Comesaña and Klein 2026, §§ 4, 4.1, 4.2; Comesaña and Klein 2026, §§ 4.1, 4.2; Comesaña and Klein 2026, § 4.2.

Layer 2The full story

Ordinary knowledge as a starting point

A Moorean strategy reverses the skeptical order of priority. Instead of accepting the alleged inability to know that the hypothesis is false and rejecting ordinary knowledge, it retains ordinary knowledge and uses closure to reject the skeptical premise. The logical form is familiar: if the ordinary proposition is known and its incompatible scenario can thereby be excluded, the scenario’s negation can be known1.

The force of the strategy depends on the status of the starting point. The skeptic questions that ordinary knowledge, so the inference will not automatically persuade them. Yet failure to persuade is different from proving that the premise cannot be known. A defender can argue that the skeptic’s demand is itself unsupported.

This comparison can be framed without pretending that strong conviction guarantees knowledge. A person’s certainty about an ordinary proposition supplies no universal immunity from deception. The relevant question is which account best explains the epistemic position and why the skeptical premise should take priority over it.

Closure and transmission complicate the answer. If support for the ordinary proposition already presupposes an entitlement to accept the background world, the argument through that proposition may not generate independent warrant for the background. Comesaña and Klein discuss that antecedent direction of support, along with proposals rejecting its necessity2.

Denying closure

Dretske and Nozick retain certain ordinary knowledge claims while denying a principle that extends them to the rejection of skeptical scenarios. Their tracking conditions assess what would happen to a belief under relevant changes. A belief about an ordinary object may track its truth while a belief denying a deceptive scenario fails the corresponding counterfactual test3.

This strategy grants part of the skeptical worry: the person need not know that the skeptical hypothesis is false. It denies that this removes all ordinary knowledge. The cost is that knowledge can fail to extend through an implication recognized by competent deduction4.

The cost must be assessed alongside the attraction of tracking. It would be too quick to accept or reject the whole theory because one conjunction sounds odd. Specify the method of belief formation, the relevant counterfactual cases, and the content being assessed. Luper explains why variations in method matter in comparing the tracking theories5.

Sensitivity and safety answer different questions

Sensitivity asks roughly whether the person would still believe the proposition if it were false. Safety asks whether the person could easily hold the belief on that basis while it was false. The two counterfactual patterns are not interchangeable. Sosa’s proposal uses the distinction to argue that denying a skeptical hypothesis can be safe despite being insensitive6.

Consider a very remote deceptive scenario. If it occurred, you would mistakenly deny it. That observation concerns sensitivity. It does not yet show that your current belief could easily be false in circumstances nearby to the actual situation. Whether those circumstances exclude deception is a further substantive issue, not something established by the word remote.

The distinction can therefore preserve ordinary knowledge without claiming that the subject has eliminated every logically possible error. But it raises questions about which possibilities count as nearby and how the belief’s basis is individuated. A safe belief must not be defined merely as a belief the theorist wants to count as knowledge.

Comesaña and Klein also survey counterexamples to safety. Their Halloween-party case introduces a nearby contingency in which the same informant would give a false direction. Whether the actual person nevertheless knows is used to put pressure on safety as a necessary condition. The survey presents safety as a debated proposal rather than an uncontested solution7.

Changing the account of evidence

The skeptical symmetry argument begins from the thought that a subject has the same evidence in a good case and in a matching deceptive case. On an experience-centered conception this can seem immediate: the experiences were stipulated to match. But the sameness of experience and the sameness of evidence are different premises8.

Williamson’s account identifies evidence with what is known. A subject in a good case may know external-world facts absent from the deceptive subject’s knowledge. If those facts constitute evidence, the good case is not epistemically exhausted by the experiences shared with the bad case9.

This proposal targets symmetry. It does not supply a neutral experiment whose results every skeptic must accept. The skeptic can object that using the ordinary knowledge to describe the evidence assumes the point under dispute. The defender can reply that restricting evidence to shared experiences also assumes a disputed epistemology.

The exchange clarifies the burden of argument. A thought experiment stipulating matching appearances does not settle what evidence is. To derive skeptical conclusions from that stipulation, one needs an account that connects appearance, evidence, truth, and knowledge. A response can challenge that account at any of these links.

Epistemological disjunctivism

World-involving accounts of perceptual justification allow successful perception to supply a factive reason, such as seeing that an object is present. A hallucination does not supply that same factive reason, even when it seems indistinguishable to the subject. Lyons explains differences among these theories and their relation to reflective access10.

This is distinct from simply asserting that perception is reliable. The proposal concerns what the good-case rational support consists in. If the reason entails the relevant worldly fact, the skeptic’s restriction to a common non-factive evidential core can be rejected.

An access problem remains. How can a reason be reflectively available if the subject cannot infallibly discriminate the good and bad cases? Different theorists understand the required access differently. The epistemological account should also be distinguished from a metaphysical theory about the constitution of perceptual experience11.

The perception page treats that distinction at length. Here the relevant point is that a response can challenge the assumed common ground without claiming that everyone can perform an unerring internal test for hallucination.

Hinge commitments and non-evidential entitlement

Another family of responses questions whether background commitments must be supported in the same way as ordinary hypotheses. Comesaña and Klein discuss Wright’s non-evidential entitlement to accept propositions excluding skeptical scenarios. Without such acceptance, ordinary evidential inquiry could not get started12.

Wright distinguishes evidential justification from entitlement and belief from acceptance. Acceptance here concerns acting, including inferential activity, approximately as one would if one believed the proposition. It should not be paraphrased as finding new empirical proof that the skeptical scenario is false13.

This precision matters. If a person is entitled to operate with a background commitment, it does not follow that they possess the same kind of evidence they possess for an ordinary measured result. The response changes the category of support being demanded at the background level.

An objection asks why indispensability for inquiry should provide an epistemic entitlement. A practice can require an assumption while that assumption remains questionable. The proposal therefore needs to explain its normative connection, rather than merely point out that abandoning the commitment is inconvenient.

The survey also notes Coliva’s related development and Cohen’s distinct account through the rationality of inferential rules. These should not be collapsed into one doctrine. They offer different ways to explain support for rejecting skeptical hypotheses without an ordinary independent evidential proof14.

Immediate perceptual support

Modest foundationalist approaches can deny that perceptual justification requires prior independent certification of the perceptual source. An experience can supply initial, defeasible support. The debate about antecedent justification for rejecting skeptical hypotheses includes Pryor’s opposition to the requirement15.

The everyday meaning of dogmatism is misleading here. The philosophical proposal need not say that a person’s belief should resist all evidence. Defeasibility is central: a warning about misleading circumstances can undermine an initially supported belief. The dispute concerns which support must already exist before perception supplies any support.

This approach faces a concern about using the output of a source to certify the source itself. If the premise’s standing depends entirely on the source, a series of deductions might seem to deliver reliability too easily. Identifying that difficulty requires a careful account of transmission, rather than assuming that all basic support is either infallible or circular.

The foundationalism and phenomenal conservatism pages develop the conditions and objections. The present contrast is between needing certification before any support and allowing defeasible initial support.

Contextualism and subject sensitivity

Speaker-centered contextualism proposes that the truth conditions of knowledge attributions vary with the attributor’s conversational context. Ordinary and skeptical conversations can therefore involve different standards without the account abandoning closure inside each context16.

The account explains a pattern in knowledge language; it is not merely the claim that people speak more cautiously in worrying circumstances. A semantic thesis concerns which proposition is expressed and what makes it true. Explaining cautious conversational behaviour alone would not establish that thesis.

Subject-sensitive invariantism instead keeps the attribution’s content invariant while allowing features of the subject, such as practical interests, to affect its truth. Comesaña and Klein emphasize the difference between the context of a sentence and the situation of the person described17.

For example, an observer discussing another person’s knowledge and the other person facing a serious decision need not share a conversational setting. That separation allows the theories to make different predictions. A useful case holds evidence fixed while distinguishing attributor and subject, rather than loosely saying that context changes everything.

Contextualism also needs an account of why ordinary attributions are true. The survey argues that explaining contextual variation does not alone establish the subject’s ordinary justification against skeptical hypotheses. The response needs substantive epistemology alongside its account of language18.

Explanatory responses in a primary text

Read the original

Russell on the common-sense hypothesis

But although this is not logically impossible, there is no reason whatever to suppose that it is true; and it is, in fact, a less simple hypothesis, viewed as a means of accounting for the facts of our own life, than the common-sense hypothesis that there really are objects independent of us, whose action on us causes our sensations.

19. Russell grants the logical possibility of a dream-like world and then compares explanations. His quoted judgment belongs to that explanatory argument; it should not be mistaken for a demonstration that deception is logically contradictory.

His cat example contrasts an independently existing animal moving between locations with appearances that begin and cease whenever observation begins and ceases. The independent object supplies a simpler account of continuity and behaviour. Russell uses this explanatory advantage to support the common-sense hypothesis20.

An objector asks why simplicity should guide truth rather than convenience. Another asks whether a sophisticated skeptical hypothesis could reproduce the relevant explanatory pattern. Those questions identify what must be defended about the inferential standard; merely pointing to a possible alternative does not compare their merits.

An explanatory response thus differs from a deductive refutation. It can allow the alternative’s possibility while arguing that the evidence favours the ordinary account. Its success depends on what counts as a good explanation and whether those criteria supply rational support in this setting.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What an answer need not promise

Preserving knowledge does not require a universal procedure that silences every possible doubter. Inquiry normally depends on some starting points and competences. The philosophical dispute asks which dependencies are legitimate and how they differ from an unsupported assumption, rather than demanding agreement by definition.

Conversely, identifying a dubious skeptical assumption does not immunize every ordinary belief. Genuine evidence of deception can defeat a claim. The contrast between a global skeptical scenario and a particular warning should remain visible when the theory is applied to ordinary life.

Connections

Read the closure argument for the exact formulation and internalism and externalism for the different requirements on accessible support. Healthy and corrosive skepticism asks what these distinctions contribute to evaluating specific doubts. The answer depends on the quality of evidence, not on selecting a philosophical label as a shortcut.

Layer 4Sources

Where to go next

Start with Comesaña and Klein’s separate treatments of the two skeptical premises. Luper gives the detailed closure alternatives and contextualist response. Lyons supplies the distinction between factive perceptual support and metaphysical directness. Russell’s second chapter gives the complete primary explanatory argument. Read these in that order to avoid treating every response as an attempted independent proof with identical starting points22.
