In short

A brain-in-a-vat scenario tests whether matching experiences could conceal a radically different situation. It is a constructed possibility, not a discovered explanation of your current life. Putnam’s semantic response concerns the special case of lifelong envatment and the reference of words. Recent envatment raises a different issue. A response must specify both the scenario and the knowledge it claims to preserve.

Layer 1Re-learn

Specify the deception

Imagine a computer supplying a disembodied brain with experiences matching those of ordinary embodied life. To test knowledge of hands, specify that the brain is handless. To test knowledge of an external physical world, specify which parts of that world the brain cannot appropriately encounter. The details determine what the hypothesis excludes.

Matching appearance alone does not establish matching evidence. Comesaña and Klein discuss accounts that identify evidence with experiences and accounts that identify it with what is known. A stipulation about appearance does not settle that debate by itself.

Likewise, failing to know in the deceptive case does not immediately establish failure in the actual case. If the brain falsely believes it has hands, the truth condition already explains its lack of knowledge. Additional assumptions are required to transfer that result to a person who really has hands.

What changes when words are included?

Putnam’s response considers a brain that has always lived in the vat. On a causal constraint on reference, it lacks the relations needed for its words to refer to ordinary brains, vats, and trees. Its apparent use of the same vocabulary therefore need not express the same content as an ordinary speaker’s use.

The point concerns meaning, not a physical diagnostic test. A simulated tree experience is not automatically a causal relation to an ordinary tree. Different proposed interpretations of the brain’s vocabulary include image features, electrical impulses, or program features.

Sources for this part: Comesaña and Klein 2026, § 4.2; Ebbs 2025, §§ 7, 7.1; Ebbs 2025, § 7.1.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Lifelong and recent envatment differ

Ebbs contrasts the lifelong scenario with a brain transferred last night. The recently transferred subject’s words can retain their former content, at least for a while. Their thought that they have hands can then be straightforwardly false. The semantic response to lifelong envatment cannot simply be extended to this case1.

That is a limitation of scope, not a reason to ignore the argument. The lifelong scenario shows how a skeptical construction can interact with a theory of content. The recent scenario shows why eliminating one construction need not eliminate every epistemic threat.

A sentence’s truth and its content

Standard reconstructions infer that a lifelong brain’s utterance describing itself as a brain in a vat is not true under the stipulated interpretation. But reaching an ordinary conclusion about the speaker’s actual situation requires care about the sentence’s truth conditions. Ebbs discusses Brueckner’s reconstruction and its contested disquotation step2.

Disquotation moves from describing a sentence as true to using the sentence itself. In an ordinary setting it seems unproblematic. Under a hypothesis that the speaker’s vocabulary has a different reference, the move becomes part of the issue. One cannot silently switch between the investigator’s language and the brain’s language.

The practical reading question is therefore: whose words are being interpreted at this step, and what do they refer to? Keeping that question visible prevents a quick semantic argument from masquerading as a universal proof that deceptive experience is impossible.

Connections

Sense and reference introduces the language distinction. The closure argument explains how an incompatible hypothesis threatens ordinary knowledge. The simulation argument concerns a probabilistic population claim with a different structure.

Where to go next

Read Ebbs’s account of Putnam’s first formulation before the reconstructed argument. It states the lifelong limitation and the causal reference assumption. Then compare the modern skepticism survey’s discussion of evidential symmetry. These texts show why specifying a skeptical scenario is substantive philosophical work3.
