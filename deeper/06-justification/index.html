Deeper study · Chapter 6

Justification: The Structure of Good Reasons

For when a section didn't click, or left you wanting more: each page explains the idea again, tells the full story, fills in what the chapter leaves out, and gives the sources.

Giovanni Battista Piranesi, The Drawbridge, plate VII of the Imaginary Prisons (Carceri d'invenzione), 1761

← Back to Chapter 6

  • Short study · 3 minWhat justification isJustification concerns the epistemic standing of a belief or the support available for believing it. It is different from truth: a reasonable belief can turn out false.
  • Short study · 3 minDefeatersA defeater reduces or removes a belief’s support. A rebutter supplies evidence against the conclusion; an undercutter challenges the connection between the grounds and the conclusion.
  • Short study · 3 minThe regress problemA reason can need support of its own, so an explanation of one belief can generate a chain of further beliefs. The regress problem asks what gives that chain justificatory force.
  • Full study · 12 minAgrippa’s trilemmaAsking for a reason can lead to another reason, a return to the original claim, or a stopping point. The skeptical challenge is that none of those patterns seems to establish anything if every reason must first be established independently.
  • Full study · 12 minFoundationalismFoundationalism says that some justified beliefs do not get their justification from other beliefs. Those basic beliefs can support further conclusions through appropriate inference.
  • Full study · 12 minCoherentismCoherentism locates justification in how beliefs fit together as a system. Fitting means more than avoiding contradiction: explanation, evidential connections, and responsiveness to information can matter.
  • Short study · 3 minInfinitismInfinitism says that an appropriate chain of reasons has no arbitrary endpoint and no question-begging repetition. Further reasons must remain available, but a person need not recite infinitely many reasons at once.
  • Short study · 3 minFoundherentismFoundherentism combines experiential input with support among beliefs. The crossword analogy shows how a clue and already filled answers can constrain the same entry.
  • Short study · 3 minCritical rationalismA critical rationalist shifts attention from securing a final foundation to exposing claims to criticism. A theory should risk failure rather than accommodate every possible outcome.
  • Full study · 12 minInternalism and externalismInternalists say that justification depends on the relevant features of a person’s perspective or mental life. Externalists say that some facts beyond those features can make a difference.
  • Short study · 3 minEvidentialismEvidentialism connects justified belief with what a person’s evidence supports at a time. Support can favor believing, disbelieving, or suspending judgment.
  • Short study · 3 minReliabilism about justificationReliabilism explains justification partly through processes that tend to produce true beliefs. A reliable process can make mistakes, and a correct result can arise from an unreliable process.
  • Short study · 3 minPhenomenal conservatismPhenomenal conservatism says that something’s seeming true can provide some support for believing it, when defeating considerations are absent. A seeming is not simply a belief or a desire that the claim be true.
  • Short study · 3 minThe problem of the criterionTo identify knowledge, we seem to need a criterion; to assess a criterion, we seem to need examples of knowledge. The problem asks how inquiry can begin without assuming what is disputed.
  • Short study · 3 minDeontology and doxastic voluntarismA deontological account describes justification through duties and permissions. That raises a question about control: can people be obliged to believe something they cannot choose?
  • Short study · 3 minFallibilismFallibilism allows adequate justification, and on many accounts knowledge, without a guarantee against error. It does not make a false proposition knowledge.
