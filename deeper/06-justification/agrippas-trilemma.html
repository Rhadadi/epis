In short

Asking for a reason can lead to another reason, a return to the original claim, or a stopping point. The skeptical challenge is that none of those patterns seems to establish anything if every reason must first be established independently. Philosophers disagree about that requirement as much as about the patterns themselves. An experience might support a belief without being another belief, and a whole system might support its members without passing support around a circle. Agrippa’s challenge exposes those choices; it does not settle them just by naming three possibilities.

Layer 1Re-learn

A dispute about a receipt

Imagine that a receipt says a payment succeeded. You believe the payment went through because the receipt says so. Someone asks why you trust the receipt. You answer that the machine is reliable. Asked why, you point to earlier successful payments. Asked how you know those payments succeeded, you mention their receipts. You have returned to the starting kind of evidence.

That return is not automatically a logical contradiction. The problem is whether you have supplied any support that someone who doubts the receipts can accept. A chain can contain true claims and still fail to answer the challenge it was meant to answer. Agrippa’s modes include just this difficulty: something offered to establish a claim needs that claim to establish it in turn.

You could keep adding reasons instead: bank statements, balance checks, messages from the seller. If each needs another established reason, the chain seems never to reach a point where support starts. Or you could stop and insist that one statement needs no defense. The skeptic asks what distinguishes that stopping point from a convenient assumption.

The word basic does not answer the challenge

Foundationalists do not ordinarily claim that an arbitrary starting statement becomes justified when someone calls it basic. They claim that some beliefs have support that is not supplied by inference from other beliefs. Seeing a payment appear in a bank account might be a candidate, depending on the account of perception. The skeptic can still challenge that account, but must address its proposed support rather than treating every endpoint as unsupported by definition.

Coherentists also reject the simple picture in which justification must begin at one belief and travel along a line. Their proposal concerns an interconnected system. Infinitists challenge the assumption that an endless supply of further reasons must be useless. These responses disagree with parts of the skeptical argument, rather than accepting its conclusion and giving the three failures reassuring names.

Five modes, three structures

The three familiar possibilities are drawn from a set of five modes preserved by Sextus Empiricus: disagreement, infinite regress, relativity, hypothesis, and reciprocity. Disagreement creates the demand for a decision; the other modes challenge ways of making that decision. Sextus attributes the modes to more recent skeptics. The connection with Agrippa comes through Diogenes Laertius, so the attribution is less direct than the familiar title suggests.

Sources for this part: Morison 2026, § 3.5.2; Comesaña and Klein 2026, § 6; Hasan and Fumerton 2022, § 1; Comesaña and Klein 2026, §§ 6.1, 6.2.

Layer 2The full story

Read the challenge as a procedure

Sextus’s skeptical practice is directed toward suspension of judgment. When opposing claims appear equally persuasive and a satisfactory adjudication is unavailable, the skeptic suspends rather than affirming either. The modes help produce that situation. Understanding their function matters: a procedure for challenging an opponent’s claim is different from a doctrine proving that nobody can ever have any justification1.

A skeptical exchange can begin locally. Someone claims to have established that a particular practice is just, that one object has a certain nature, or that a certain standard is authoritative. A disagreement is introduced. The claimant offers a way of resolving it. The skeptic examines whether the proposed standard is itself disputed, rests on an unargued assumption, or depends on what it was introduced to establish. A new philosophical theory can become the next object of the same scrutiny2.

This procedure need not begin by demanding an impossible quantity of evidence. It can begin with a real disagreement about a proposed test. In the receipt example, the critic might have evidence that the machine sometimes prints success after a failed transaction. The original believer now needs to distinguish this receipt from those cases. Whether every subsequent reason must receive its own separate justification is a further question. That question is where the local dispute becomes the general regress problem.

Read the original

An assumption cannot establish its own authority

If it is absurd to assume the thing questioned, it is also absurd to assume that upon which it rests.

3. This is from Patrick's public-domain translation of Sextus, included after her historical study. In context, the skeptic objects that someone who is entitled simply to assume a supporting claim could equally assume the conclusion. Or an opponent could assume its contrary. A premise's role in an argument does not by itself give it greater authority than the disputed conclusion.

Notice what the passage attacks. It concerns taking something for granted as a way to escape the demand for proof. It does not examine every later theory according to which an experience, an acquaintance relation, or a reliable process supplies non-inferential justification. Applying its challenge to those views requires explaining why their proposed support fails. Otherwise the critic has quietly equated lack of proof with lack of justification, the very equation those views contest4.

Why disagreement and relativity matter

Disagreement is not merely the observation that somebody somewhere disagrees with you. In the modes, it is disagreement that cannot be satisfactorily decided by the resources offered. If a measuring instrument has a well-established calibration and the apparent disagreement comes from misreading its scale, a simple appeal to disagreement does not show that the result is unsupported. The skeptical pressure returns when the authority of the instrument and its calibration are themselves disputed5.

Relativity concerns how things appear in relation to a judge and circumstances. A drink can taste sweet after one food and less sweet after another. This difference does not establish that there is no drink or no fact about its composition. It challenges a move from an appearance in some circumstances to an unrestricted claim about what the thing is in itself. Deciding which circumstances reveal its nature introduces a standard; that standard can then face the other modes6.

These two modes make the structural challenge concrete. Without a disputed claim or standard, a request for a further reason might seem gratuitous. Once alternatives are on the table, simply repeating the original claim no longer settles the matter. Yet it remains open whether satisfying every imaginable opponent is necessary for being justified. A belief’s epistemic standing and its ability to persuade an interlocutor are related questions that should not be collapsed.

Make the skeptical premises visible

The contemporary survey of skepticism reconstructs a general argument using the regress modes. Its force depends on several premises that philosophers reject in different ways. The following version keeps the main dependencies explicit7.

The argument, step by step

A regress argument for skepticism about justification

  1. A justified belief is either basic or justified through an inferential chain.
  2. No belief has basic justification.
  3. An inferential chain must continue indefinitely, repeat, or terminate without justified support.
  4. An indefinitely continuing chain cannot justify its members.
  5. A repeating chain cannot justify its members.
  6. A chain terminating without justified support cannot justify its members.
  7. Therefore no belief is justified.
8. Premise 3 classifies chains; the conclusion also requires the adverse verdicts on their possible structures. Those verdicts are substantive. Classifying a chain as infinite is not yet showing that it fails, and classifying a belief as basic is not yet showing that its support is arbitrary.

Premise 2 carries the dispute with foundationalism. A foundationalist can accept that inference from an unsupported belief does not give a conclusion adequate support while denying that every non-inferential belief is unsupported. Premise 4 carries the dispute with infinitism. Premise 5 carries part of the dispute with coherentism, although holistic coherentism questions whether individual chains capture its explanation at all. The argument is useful because it shows where a reply must do work9.

The skeptical conclusion is stronger than the observation that nobody can give an unchallengeable speech. To reach it, the premises must concern having justification, including justification that a person has not articulated. If they concern only winning every possible debate, the conclusion concerns debate. Moving between the two changes the subject without repairing the gap.

Foundationalism’s response: distinguish support from further belief

Consider a person who feels a sharp pain and believes that she is in pain. One candidate explanation is that she is directly aware of the pain. A foundationalist need not say that she infers her belief from another belief stating that direct awareness is reliable. If every such awareness had to be supported by another justified belief before it could justify anything, the foundational proposal would indeed face a regress. But imposing that demand is a premise against the proposal, not a neutral description of it10.

The hardest issue is how awareness supplies rational support. Critics argue that if awareness already involves a judgment, that judgment needs support; if it is merely a nonconceptual occurrence, its ability to justify a proposition is unexplained. Foundationalists develop different answers, including acquaintance theories and theories of defeasible seemings. The absence of an inferential predecessor therefore opens a problem that needs an account. It does not establish that no account is possible11.

There is also a difference between a foundation that cannot be wrong and one that provides some support while remaining vulnerable to correction. Agrippa’s challenge does not require all foundationalists to defend certainty. Modest foundationalists can grant that perceptual beliefs sometimes fail and that evidence of abnormal conditions defeats them. Their claim is that ordinary experience can still confer initial justification without a completed argument establishing its reliability12.

Coherentism’s response: question the transmission model

A simple circle passes no independent support: the receipt is trustworthy because the payment succeeded, and the payment succeeded because the receipt is trustworthy. Holistic coherentism does not propose that every circle of that form establishes its contents. It proposes that sufficiently rich relations within a whole system can confer justification on its members. Explanatory integration, consistency, and relations with observational beliefs matter to different accounts13.

Imagine an inquiry using a timestamp, an account balance, the seller’s stock records, and a delivery confirmation. A connected explanation might explain all four better than a rival. The coherentist must explain what makes that integration epistemically valuable. Merely drawing arrows among the claims will not do. A made-up story can also contain many connections. The input and truth-connection objections ask whether the account gives a system a reason to answer to the world14.

This response shifts the unit of assessment. If justification belongs initially to systems, analyzing one member’s support as an isolated line may misdescribe the theory. A critic should then assess the system-level account. Does it distinguish independent checks from copies? Does it penalize a belief retained only by rewriting everything around it? Can competing systems receive equal support? Those questions press coherentism without assuming that it is just a two-premise circle.

Infinitism’s response: reasons need not arrive all at once

Infinitists reject both arbitrary endpoints and question-begging repetition. Their positive proposal is that further reasons remain available. The fact that a person has considered only finitely many reasons does not by itself refute that possibility. A person can have capacities extending beyond what she has performed: she can count further without having counted every number. This analogy illustrates the distinction between completion and availability, although it does not prove that an adequate epistemic chain exists15.

The survey distinguishes reason-enhanced justification from the picture in which fully established justification must begin at the far end of a chain and travel forward. On an enhancement view, supplying an appropriate further reason can improve a belief’s status even when that reason has not itself received a completed defense. Infinitism’s account thus disputes the demand for a final source of fully established support as well as the need for a finite chain16.

Availability requires explanation. It cannot mean merely that infinitely many sentences could be written by somebody. The reasons must be appropriately connected to the believer and must genuinely support the relevant beliefs. Otherwise an infinitely long sequence of irrelevant propositions would count. Nor can repetition disguised by new wording evade the no-circle requirement. These demands leave substantive work for infinitists after the finite-mind objection is answered17.

Different questions about standards

The challenge applies to epistemic theories themselves. If a theory says that a process or a mental state supplies justification, why accept that standard? A proposed answer may rely on cases of justified belief; identifying those cases may already rely on a standard. This produces the problem of the criterion. It concerns how we assess our methods of assessment, not simply how an ordinary person’s first-order belief is supported18.

For example, a person can have perceptual justification for thinking a lamp is on while lacking a worked-out theory of perceptual justification. Asking how philosophers establish the theory is legitimate. Inferring from an incomplete theoretical defense that the person’s ordinary belief has no support requires an additional premise. That premise would make possessing justification depend on successfully theorizing about it. Many regress arguments become much more demanding at precisely this point19.

The difference also affects the demand for neutrality. A defense that satisfies a foundationalist may not satisfy a skeptic who permits only already-established inferential premises. A defense that satisfies a coherentist may appeal to the system whose standing the opponent disputes. Whether an account must persuade every opponent from premises accepted by both is a separate standard of success. Failure by that standard does not immediately establish failure by every standard of epistemic support.

What the trilemma establishes without extra premises

The trilemma is a diagnostic map. When a justification ends, ask what supplies support there. When it returns, ask whether the return adds anything and whether the theory is actually holistic. When it continues, ask how further reasons are available and how they enhance support. Those questions identify explanatory obligations. They do not give all three possibilities the same answer in advance.

A map also has limits. Real inquiries may combine direct experience, inferential connections, and revisable assessment of methods. Their structure need not look like a single chain on paper. Showing that one interpretation of each traditional response fails would still leave the question whether a hybrid account satisfies the underlying demands. The structural classification is clearest when its assumptions are stated, and least useful when it replaces examination of a proposed theory20.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Historical and interpretive caution

The ancient modes survive through reports, and Sextus does not name Agrippa when presenting them. Morison’s survey discusses both the attribution and the question whether a skeptic applies standards she endorses or standards her dogmatic opponent endorses. Avoid treating the text as a surviving treatise in which Agrippa announces a modern universal theory of justification21.

Connections

Foundationalism, Coherentism, and Infinitism develop the disputed premises. The central questions develops the problem of the criterion. For the broader ancient practice, go to Pyrrhonian skepticism.
