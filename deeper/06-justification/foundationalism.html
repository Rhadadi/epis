In short

Foundationalism says that some justified beliefs do not get their justification from other beliefs. Those basic beliefs can support further conclusions through appropriate inference. A foundation can be fallible: basic does not mean certain, permanent, or exempt from criticism. The central difficulty is explaining how experience or some other non-inferential source supplies rational support. The theory succeeds only if it explains both that starting support and the inferences that build on it.

Layer 1Re-learn

Seeing a glass, then making an inference

You look at a table and see a glass. You believe a glass is there. You also remember that the glass holds two hundred milliliters and infer that it will not hold all the water in a half-liter bottle. These beliefs need not acquire support in the same way. The capacity conclusion uses premises. The initial perceptual belief might be supported directly by your experience.

The foundationalist calls a belief basic when its justification does not depend on inference from other beliefs. Basic is a description of how it is supported, not how important it is. A belief about a glass can be basic; a deeply important belief about justice can require substantial argument. Nor does basic mean unsupported. The experience is doing the supporting, according to a perceptual version of the theory.

This distinction prevents a common false choice: either you give another belief as a reason or you just guess. A foundationalist proposes a third possibility, non-inferential support. Whether that possibility can be explained is the central controversy. Naming it does not explain it, but denying it without argument does not show that all beliefs must be inferred either.

A foundation can move

Suppose you learn that the apparent glass is a realistic projection. The new information can defeat your perceptual belief. That does not show that it previously lacked all support. A modest foundationalist can say that the experience supplied initial justification which the discovery removed. Immediate justification can be defeasible.

The building metaphor becomes misleading here. Concrete foundations are supposed to remain fixed while floors stand on them. An epistemic foundation may lose support, be corrected, or be replaced. What matters to the structure is that at least some justification is supplied without inference from other beliefs, not that particular beliefs survive every revision.

Experience is not a hidden premise

There is a difference between having a visual experience and believing a proposition about that experience. A modest perceptual foundationalist need not say that you reason from I seem to see a glass and such experiences are reliable to there is a glass. The experience can be the source of support directly. Requiring an additional justified reliability belief would be a different account.

Layer 2The full story

The regress argument supports less than it first seems

If every justified belief depends on another justified belief, the supporting beliefs need their own support. A finite believer cannot explicitly complete an infinite list. A circular list seems unable to begin the transmission of justification. An unsupported last premise seems unable to supply it either. This motivates the idea that some support must be non-inferential1.

But the regress argument does not by itself establish a positive theory of foundations. It might instead lead to skepticism. And critics can dispute the judgments about infinity and holistic support. Hasan and Fumerton emphasize that rejecting the claim that all justification is inferential requires fewer commitments than establishing every element of foundationalism2.

The argument, step by step

A conditional route to foundations

  1. At least some beliefs are justified.
  2. A justified belief receives support either non-inferentially or through justified inferential premises.
  3. Inferential support cannot be supplied entirely by an unsupported endpoint, a vicious circle, or an endless ungrounded chain.
  4. Therefore some justification must be non-inferential.
3. Premise 1 rules out the skeptical response. Premise 3 must be defended against coherentist and infinitist accounts. Even if the conclusion succeeds, it leaves open which beliefs have this support, what supplies it, and how much inferential justification can rest on it.

This last distinction matters in ordinary inquiry. An investigator can observe a meter reading directly while making a complicated inference about a machine’s condition. Establishing that some observation is immediately justified does not establish the whole inference. The meter could measure a relevant property poorly; an auxiliary assumption could be false; a calculation could be mistaken. Foundations address the source of support, not every subsequent failure.

Two demands can generate different regresses

One demand says that the premises of an inference must be justified. If you infer that a medicine is safe from a fabricated trial result, valid reasoning does not repair the fabrication. A second demand says that you must also have justification for believing that those premises support this conclusion. These demands are distinguishable. The second can produce higher-level chains about the adequacy of each inference4.

For example, you believe a plant is poisonous because you see markings that you take to indicate poison. Your belief about its markings could be justified while your assumption about their significance is groundless. This motivates the second demand. Yet if every adequate inference required another consciously held and independently justified belief about its adequacy, ordinary reasoning would face a multiplying burden. Some accounts instead permit an appreciation of the connection that is not another premise5.

The dispute is not solved by insisting that people should be careful. Care can improve reasoning without supplying an infinite hierarchy of beliefs about reasoning. A theory must say what epistemic relation is required at each level and why. Otherwise a sensible demand for relevant support gradually turns into a requirement that finite reasoners possess a complete philosophy of inference.

Classical ambition: support that excludes error

Classical forms set a demanding standard for basic justification. The survey distinguishes an infallible belief, whose truth cannot fail in the relevant circumstances, from infallible justification, which is supposed to rule out the belief’s falsity. These are connected but not interchangeable claims. A necessary truth can be believed for a bad reason; the truth’s necessity does not improve that reason6.

Candidates for secure foundations include certain beliefs about present experience and relations apprehended directly. If you experience pain, perhaps your belief that you are in pain is supported in a way that a belief about a distant object is not. An acquaintance account develops this suggestion by appealing to direct awareness of a fact and its relation to a thought. The work lies in explaining that relation, rather than inferring certainty from how compelling the belief feels7.

Read the original

Descartes's rule of caution

The first was never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such; that is to say, carefully to avoid precipitancy and prejudice, and to comprise nothing more in my judgement than what was presented to my mind so clearly and distinctly as to exclude all ground of doubt.

8. This passage states a rule from the *Discourse on Method*. It shows the ambition to withhold assent where doubt remains. It is not, by itself, an argument that clear presentation guarantees truth, nor a statement of every modern form of foundationalism. The later modest theories discussed below relax precisely the demand for a foundation that excludes error.

A narrow foundation creates a problem of reach. If what is secure concerns only present inner experience, how does one gain justified beliefs about enduring objects, other minds, or the past? Deduction cannot simply create claims about an external world from premises that remain compatible with its absence. The survey treats this threat of skepticism as a serious objection to stringent classical accounts9.

One can therefore accept the structural claim that some justification is basic while rejecting the classical demand for certainty. Conversely, one can admire Descartes’s demand for caution without holding that only indubitable beliefs are justified. Separating those commitments makes the family of views easier to evaluate: the argument against a certainty requirement need not be an argument against non-inferential support.

Modest foundations: immediate and defeasible

Modest theories allow basic beliefs to have some degree of justification even when their supporting state does not guarantee truth. A perceptual seeming can support a belief about an ordinary object. A memory seeming can support a belief about the past. Counterevidence or information about unreliable conditions can defeat the support. The positive account needs to identify the relevant state and the conditions under which it supplies justification10.

This view explains a natural order of inquiry. You need not first assemble a historical proof that your vision is reliable before being justified in believing a glass is visible. Nevertheless, after discovering a projection device you should reconsider. Having no preliminary reliability proof and having positive evidence of unreliability are different situations. Immediate justification does not imply immunity to information about the source.

The survey warns that Pryor’s use of dogmatism is broader than one particular principle saying that every seeming supplies support. Immediate, undermining-sensitive perceptual justification is one structural proposal; a universal account of all seemings is another. A thinker can favor the former and restrict the latter. Careful descriptions should state what kinds of appearances the theory covers rather than treating related labels as interchangeable11.

Modest foundationalism can also permit induction and inference to the best explanation. Accepting a fallible basis while insisting that every inference must be deductive would leave many ordinary conclusions unavailable. The general structural thesis does not determine one universal inference rule. Its account of derived beliefs must explain how supported premises can justify conclusions that go beyond what those premises entail12.

The Sellarsian dilemma

The central challenge asks how a non-belief state can be a reason. If an experience involves a conceptual judgment, perhaps that judgment requires justification. If it is entirely nonconceptual, perhaps it lacks the logical or representational relation needed to support a proposition. The survey discusses Sellars’s challenge and BonJour’s related formulation under this dilemma13.

The argument, step by step

A challenge to experience as a foundation

  1. A foundation must supply rational support without depending on another justified belief.
  2. If the supporting awareness is itself a judgment, it requires justification and threatens a regress.
  3. If it is not appropriately related to propositional content, its rational support is unexplained.
  4. Therefore the foundationalist must explain an awareness that avoids both difficulties.
14. This formulation states an explanatory challenge rather than assuming that the two difficulties exhaust every possible kind of awareness. Replies question the move from possessing propositional content to being a belief that needs justification, or offer acquaintance with a fact, a thought, and their correspondence.

A photograph represents a tree without believing that a tree exists. That analogy helps show why representation and judgment are different notions, but it does not yet solve the dilemma: photographs do not automatically give their viewers justification. The relevant reply must explain why the subject’s experience has epistemic significance. A foundationalist cannot replace that explanation with the observation that some non-beliefs have content.

An acquaintance theory faces its own questions. What is direct awareness of a fact? Must one be aware that the correspondence between thought and fact obtains? Does that awareness create a further layer of the same problem? The survey presents these as substantive disputes about access and correspondence. Giving the relation a name leaves those questions in place15.

Believing and seeming must remain distinct

Doxastic conservatism says that already believing something can itself give a degree of initial support. Phenomenal conservatism instead appeals to its seeming true. The survey treats both as possible permissive accounts while explaining their different costs. If merely adopting a belief raises its justification, a person might cross the justificatory threshold by believing prematurely. A seeming-based account avoids that particular move only if seemings are genuinely distinct from beliefs16.

Persistent illusions illustrate the distinction. A line can continue to look longer than another line even when you know they are equal. You then have an appearance without the corresponding belief. Knowing about the illusion can defeat the appearance’s support for the length judgment without removing the appearance itself. The example separates a psychological occurrence, a belief, and the all-things-considered justification for that belief17.

There are still difficult cases. A fear or prejudice might influence how a face looks, so that an unsupported suspicion generates the very appearance later invoked to defend it. A foundationalist must explain whether the history of an experience can undermine its support. If the account treats every appearance as equally innocent, biased cognition may acquire support it did not earn18.

Externalist foundations

Foundationalism is not inherently internalist. An externalist version might say that a basic belief is justified because a reliable process produces it, even when the person does not possess a justified belief about the process’s reliability. That account still distinguishes basic and inferential support. It changes what gives the basis epistemic standing19.

The structure and the source should therefore be kept apart. An experience-based foundation can be assessed from the subject’s perspective. A reliability-based foundation can be assessed partly through facts about the actual method and environment. Both might allow basic perceptual beliefs. Their disagreement concerns why those beliefs are justified and which differences between circumstances matter20.

Norman’s reliable clairvoyance illustrates the difficulty for a simple externalist account. Reliability can be stipulated while Norman has no appropriate grounds available for taking the deliverance seriously. Critics find justification absent; defenders can add conditions concerning defeat or refine the relevant notion of reliability. The example tests the account of the foundation, not the bare possibility of basic beliefs21.

Bootstrapping and easy knowledge

Suppose you read a fuel gauge, believe the tank is full, and use the agreement between the gauge and that belief to conclude that the gauge is reliable. Your check has not introduced independent evidence of the tank’s contents. Repeating that procedure many times can make a record look impressive without improving its authority. This illustrates why basic justification and knowledge of a source’s reliability need separate treatment22.

A foundationalist may permit you to have initial justification for ordinary gauge readings without permitting you to establish the gauge’s reliability through those readings alone. The distinction is not an admission that every reading is unsupported. It is a restriction on an inference about the method. A source can supply some first-order support while an attempted higher-order validation remains defective.

The philosophical task is to explain that restriction consistently. If the premises are justified and the inference seems good, why does the reliability conclusion fail? Answers can constrain transmission, require independent checks in certain source-evaluation arguments, or revise the account of initial support. The problem shows that stopping a regress is only one part of an adequate epistemology23.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

A structural view and its rival accounts

Keep three questions separate: whether any justification is non-inferential, what supplies it, and how far it reaches. The regress argument primarily concerns the first. Classical acquaintance, modest seemings, and reliable processes offer different answers to the second. Objections about skepticism, cognitive penetration, and easy knowledge press the third and its interaction with the others24.

Connections

Agrippa’s trilemma explains why an endpoint needs support. Phenomenal conservatism develops one permissive account. Internalism and externalism compares perspectives on what confers that support. For the source itself, see Perception.

Where to go next

Hasan and Fumerton provide the main reading path: start with § 1 for the two inferential demands, then § 3.2 for the dilemma and § 4 for modest views. §§ 6–8 develop externalist foundations and easy knowledge. Their entry is a survey of competing accounts; it should not be read as endorsing every view it explains25.

Descartes’s Discourse, Part II, supplies an accessible primary example of a demanding method. Read the quoted rule as one historical proposal, then return to the contemporary survey to distinguish the structural thesis from a demand for certainty26.
