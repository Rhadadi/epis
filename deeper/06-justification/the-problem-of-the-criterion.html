The problem of the criterion

In short

To identify knowledge, we seem to need a criterion; to assess a criterion, we seem to need examples of knowledge. The problem asks how inquiry can begin without assuming what is disputed. Particularists start with cases, methodists with a standard, and skeptics challenge both starts. Each response carries an explanatory burden. The puzzle concerns assessing epistemic standards, not whether every ordinary belief must wait for a complete theory.

Layer 1Re-learn

Choosing a check for a check

You compare two medical tests. To assess which is better, you need to know which patients really have the condition. But your way of identifying those patients is itself a diagnostic procedure. What validates it? A new test introduces another assessment problem. The same structure appears when philosophers ask which beliefs count as knowledge and what standards identify them.

McCain formulates the problem as one of epistemic priority: which accepted claims or criteria may guide the rest without first being established by the other? The issue is not the metaphysical nature of truth. You can have a theory of what truth is while still lacking a defensible way to distinguish knowledge from error.

Particularism begins with accepted cases and develops standards from them. Methodism begins with a criterion and uses it to classify cases. The skeptical response insists that neither can be answered without first settling the other. These responses disagree about the dependence requirement, not just about which list of cases or which standard to choose.

A classification does not validate a starting point

Calling a starting point particularist does not explain why its cases deserve confidence. Calling it methodist does not explain why the rule is authoritative. A skeptic also needs to defend the requirement of prior answers that blocks both approaches. Otherwise the skeptical conclusion assumes its own standard as readily as its rivals assume theirs.

Chisholm favors particularism while acknowledging the difficulty of answering the other approaches without begging the question. McCain’s survey distinguishes this preference from a decisive solution acceptable to every opponent. Do not describe the position as if identifying familiar knowledge claims dissolved the puzzle.

Sources for this part: McCain n.d., § 1; McCain n.d., § 2; McCain n.d., §§ 1, 2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

First-order belief and philosophical certification

A person can see a lamp illuminated while lacking a theory of perceptual knowledge. To conclude that she has no support until philosophers settle the criterion problem requires a further demand: first-order justification must wait for justified theoretical certification. The regress discussion explains why adding higher-level conditions can substantially change an argument1.

That observation is not a solution to the criterion problem. It separates the question of how ordinary cognition works from the question of how we defend our account of it. A theory can preserve ordinary justification while remaining incomplete as a response to every skeptical challenge.

Mutual revision needs an account too

Moving between cases and principles can help expose mistakes: a criterion excludes an apparently clear case, so you reconsider the criterion or the case. Yet movement alone does not establish epistemic authority. A mutually reinforcing collection can be mistaken. The same isolation and alternative-system questions that face coherentism arise if fit is the only proposed validation2.

In practice, identify what a correction adds. A new observation can supply information beyond internal fit. An independent check can constrain a favored method. A revised standard can make a prediction open to failure. Those improvements matter more than the mere fact that a circle has become larger.

Where to go next

McCain’s §§ 1–2 separates the priority problem from theories of truth and explains the three responses. Read Hasan and Fumerton’s § 1 to keep first-order support distinct from stronger certification requirements. Olsson’s § 1 provides the caution about treating mutual fit as complete validation3.
