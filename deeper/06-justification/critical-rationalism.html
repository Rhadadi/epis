In short

A critical rationalist shifts attention from securing a final foundation to exposing claims to criticism. A theory should risk failure rather than accommodate every possible outcome. Passing a test is valuable without guaranteeing truth. Tests also use assumptions and observations that can themselves be challenged. The approach therefore needs an account of rational stopping points and of how criticism supports choosing between surviving alternatives.

Layer 1Re-learn

A claim that can lose

Someone proposes that a faulty battery explains a machine’s failures. If replacing the battery changes nothing, that result counts against the proposal. Another person insists that the battery explains the failures whatever happens: improvement proves it, continued failure proves a deeper hidden battery effect. The second proposal has insulated itself from the apparent test.

Popper emphasizes attempts to refute theories and the need to specify relevant tests. A claim’s ability to explain observed events after the fact is insufficient if it offers no conditions under which it could fail. The SEP survey explains this motivation in Popper’s assessment of competing intellectual systems.

The practical lesson is to state what would challenge your explanation before treating every result as support. That does not mean one disappointing observation automatically settles a complex theory. A measurement can be defective, the intervention can be incomplete, or an auxiliary assumption can be wrong. Criticism examines those possibilities too.

Passing is different from proof

A machine working after a battery replacement does not establish that every failure of that kind has that cause. The successful test can leave rival explanations open. Popper’s distinction between corroboration and guaranteed truth reflects this difference. The survey also discusses his effort to describe progress toward truth through verisimilitude, and the subsequent defects found in his formal definitions.

This makes criticism more than habitual disbelief. You develop proposals, derive consequences, and arrange discriminating tests. A conclusion remains revisable, but there can still be a reason to prefer a proposal that has survived serious scrutiny over one protected from every challenge. How that preference is explained is an important philosophical question.

Sources for this part: Thornton 2026, § 2; Thornton 2026, § 6.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Tests have their own stopping points

Popper treats basic statements used in tests as open to further testing. Actual testing nevertheless stops through a decision to accept some statements, provisionally and intersubjectively. Experience can motivate that decision without furnishing the kind of ultimate justification he rejects. This distinguishes a practical stopping point from an infallible foundation1.

That distinction also creates a burden. If the accepted observation is wrong, the attempted refutation may be wrong. A critical account needs standards for assessing observations and deciding when further testing is appropriate. Agreement by a research community is a condition of using a statement in a test, not an automatic guarantee that the statement is true.

Falsifiability and actual falsification

The survey’s critical evaluation discusses how high-level theories depend on auxiliary assumptions. A failed prediction can be attributed to one of those assumptions rather than the central theory. It recounts Lakatos’s objection that scientific theories persist through anomalies instead of being abandoned after isolated critical tests2.

Consequently, identifying a possible contrary result is the start of a methodological account. It leaves open when revisions are responsible and when they merely shield a favorite view. Surviving criticism also needs comparison: two proposals can both survive because the tests were weak. Counting survival without assessing the tests supplies little guidance.

Connections

Agrippa’s trilemma explains the demand for ultimate support that motivates a change of task. Popper and falsificationism and The Duhem–Quine problem develop the scientific-method questions.

Where to go next

Thornton’s § 2 explains the testability motivation; § 4 addresses basic statements and stopping; § 6 discusses progress and verisimilitude. Finish with § 12 rather than treating the opening account as a complete uncontroversial method.
