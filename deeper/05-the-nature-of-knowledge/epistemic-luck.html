In short

Being fortunate to learn something is different from being fortunate that your belief happens to be true. A chance encounter can deliver excellent evidence; a coincidence can also conceal the failure of a method. The important question is where luck enters the relationship between evidence, belief and fact. Modal accounts examine nearby possibilities of error, while evidence-based accounts examine the support for this particular truth. Their disagreement matters because a belief can be stable across nearby situations and still rest on evidence unrelated to what makes it true.

Layer 1Re-learn

Luck before the evidence, or luck after it?

Suppose a traveller happens to arrive while a station employee is announcing a platform change. The encounter is fortunate, but the announcement can still give good information. Now suppose a traveller reads yesterday’s announcement, and today’s train coincidentally uses the same platform. The coincidence makes the answer correct without making the old announcement a dependable guide. These invented cases isolate two different places at which luck can enter.

The IEP distinguishes evidential luck from veritic luck. Evidential luck concerns having the evidence at all. Veritic luck concerns the belief being true given the evidence. Its example is a bank teller seeing a robber’s face when the mask slips: the opportunity is lucky, but the clear view can reveal the robber’s identity. A lucky opportunity is therefore not automatically a lucky guess.

The difference does not require every accidental encounter to confer knowledge. You might glimpse a face too briefly to recognize it, or hear an unreliable rumour by chance. The point is that the accident of access does not by itself undermine knowledge. The evidence still needs evaluation on its merits.

Do not confuse a lucky fact with a lucky belief

Winning a prize is lucky. After checking the official results, the winner can know that they won. The event’s improbability and the reliability of the belief are different questions. The IEP calls luck in the truth of the proposition propositional luck, and also distinguishes existential and facultative luck: being alive to know and possessing the relevant capacities.

This prevents an overly sweeping anti-luck rule. Knowledge does not require that the world contain no accidents or that the knower have earned every opportunity. The task is to identify the luck that compromises the epistemic relation, not to purge contingency from human life.

The cause can be right while the environment is dangerous

Fake-barn country separates actual success from nearby error. Henry looks at a real barn, but many indistinguishable facades surround it. The actual causal route is intact; the worry is that the same visual procedure could easily have delivered a false belief. The SEP uses this case to challenge accounts focused on the actual causal chain or a broadly reliable faculty.

The hidden-dog case has another structure. The visible object is a robot, while a concealed dog makes the belief true. Here the support is misdirected in the actual case, not merely at risk in a neighbouring situation. A theory may need to explain both kinds of defect.

Layer 2The full story

The paradox of rejecting all epistemic luck

The IEP sets out an apparent conflict: we take ourselves to know many things; epistemic luck seems incompatible with knowledge; and fortunate circumstances pervade our epistemic lives. If the incompatibility claim covers every kind of luck, the other two claims cannot both stand. The article’s resolution is to reject the indiscriminate incompatibility claim and distinguish harmless from harmful forms1.

That distinction is more than a terminological escape. The bank teller’s knowledge does not become worse merely because the mask might not have slipped. Conditional on the good view, the identity is supported. Conversely, the outdated timetable’s fortunate agreement with today’s schedule does not improve the evidence. The two cases offer different explanations of why the belief is true.

An objection might say that both travellers could easily have failed to know. That is correct but ambiguous. One might have missed the announcement and formed no belief. The other might have formed the same belief and been wrong. Failure to acquire a belief is not the same as forming a false belief in the same way. Which comparison an account uses can change its verdict.

Historical development without a single settled taxonomy

Timeline

Distinguishing luck in knowing

  • 1968 · Unger identifies forms of epistemic luck compatible with knowledge2.
  • 1992 · Engel distinguishes evidential and veritic luck3.
  • 2001 · Vahid argues that justification-oriented luck may also undermine knowledge4.
  • 2003 · Pritchard offers a modal characterization of veritic luck5.
  • 2007 · Hiller and Neta’s case challenges whether safety alone excludes the relevant luck6.

The labels in the chapter are useful, but they should not be treated as a classification endorsed identically by every theorist. The IEP contrasts an evidence-based account with a method-based modal account. It also discusses disagreement over whether luck affecting justification deserves the same status as luck affecting truth. A taxonomy is part of a theory, not a neutral list that settles the theory beforehand7.

Evidence-based veritic luck

Engel’s formulation asks whether, given the subject’s evidence, it is merely lucky that the belief is true. The formulation keeps the actual support in view. Smith’s evidence concerns Jones owning a Ford, not Brown’s presence in Barcelona. The latter makes the disjunction true while the former supports Smith’s acceptance. The gap between those roles explains why this is a paradigm of veritic luck8.

This perspective is especially useful when a true answer can be reached through several routes. Suppose a student gets an arithmetic result right by applying an invalid rule that happens to work for these numbers. A teacher need not deny the correctness of the answer to investigate the inadequacy of the method. The question concerns why the answer is correct relative to the support the student used.

The perspective also has an explanatory burden. Merely declaring that success was lucky can redescribe the judgment that there was no knowledge. A full analysis needs an account of the luck relation clear enough to yield verdicts without presupposing the verdict about knowledge. The SEP explicitly identifies this requirement of informativeness and noncircularity9.

Modal veritic luck

Pritchard’s modal formulation, as presented in the IEP, examines nearby possible worlds where the subject forms the belief in the same manner. A belief is veritically lucky when it is true here but false in nearly all those nearby worlds. This changes the focus from evidence for the target truth to a pattern of error across circumstances10.

Possible worlds here are a way to express variations on a situation, not distant locations one could visit. For a station display, variations might include an ordinary schedule change, a stale data feed or a different platform assignment. The account needs to decide which variations are relevant and how the method is kept fixed. Keeping the broad category of reading fixed is different from keeping this particular source and kind of display fixed.

The modal approach explains why environmental deception matters even if the actual object is genuine. Many nearby applications of Henry’s apparent-barn recognition would encounter facades. The actual hit does not make the surrounding pattern disappear11.

Yet frequency across possibilities is not self-interpreting. Nearly all nearby worlds presupposes a way to select and compare worlds. It should not be read automatically as a measured statistical frequency from a sample. A high observed success rate and modal safety can come apart, especially if the observed sample misses a nearby kind of failure.

Strong and weak safety

The IEP distinguishes more demanding and less demanding safety conditions. Strong safety excludes error across the relevant nearby belief-forming situations. A weaker condition tolerates error in a small minority. The article argues that weak safety can admit lottery beliefs: a ticket loses in the overwhelming majority of relevant circumstances, but many theorists deny that its holder knows it will lose before the result12.

This disagreement shows why an anti-luck condition cannot simply become a demand for high probability. A lottery ticket can be extremely unlikely to win while the evidence still fails to discriminate this losing ticket from the winning one. Whether that discriminative gap rules out knowledge is contested, but a theory must address it rather than assuming that enough decimal places settles the matter13.

Strong safety has its own complications. The IEP describes a county where all facades are red, while genuine barns can be blue or red. A belief that a visible object is a blue barn may be safe even when the broader belief that it is a barn is vulnerable to red facades. The discussion uses this to press a closure worry for the strong formulation14.

The moral is not that specificity always improves knowledge. It is that modal evaluation depends on the proposition and the belief-forming route. When a theory tests each belief separately, it must explain how knowledge is preserved through competent inference from a more specific claim to a consequence.

A stable truth can still be epistemically disconnected

Hiller and Neta’s example, as presented in the IEP, replaces Brown’s contingent location with the proposition that Brown will not win a Grammy. Suppose Brown’s lack of musical talent makes that proposition true throughout the relevant nearby worlds, though Smith has no evidence about it. Smith infers the disjunction that Jones owns a Ford or Brown will not win a Grammy from misleading Ford evidence15.

The disjunction is safe in those worlds because the second disjunct remains true. But the second disjunct is not part of Smith’s evidence. Its stability does not improve the connection between the evidence used and the truth obtained. The IEP uses this case to argue that safe true belief alone is insufficient and to criticize the purely modal analysis of veritic luck16.

That argument identifies a substantive difference between safety and evidence-based anti-luck evaluation. A theorist might refine safety to focus on the route or relevant contents, add an ability condition, or contest the verdict. What cannot be assumed is that stability of the whole proposition automatically establishes epistemic credit for believing it.

Justification-oriented luck

Vahid’s proposal concerns how easily a justified belief could have been unjustified. In Harman’s assassination case, an agent misses misleading news that would have undermined her evidence. The IEP then tests that proposal against the mask-slipping teller: the teller could easily have failed to see the face and lacked the justification she actually has, yet she appears to know17.

The question is whether the nearby absence of evidence is itself disqualifying. If it were always disqualifying, the fortunate discovery of a crucial clue would rarely produce knowledge. If it never mattered, misleading evidence the subject happens not to encounter could never matter either. A developed view needs to distinguish those situations rather than treating all missed information alike.

This is another reason to ask where luck operates. It might affect having evidence, retaining justification, selecting a method, reaching a truth, or recognizing one’s status. A single verdict about luck in general can obscure those different relations18.

Reflective luck and the external world

Reflective luck concerns what can be settled from the subject’s reflective standpoint. The IEP presents Pritchard’s account as ordering possibilities by what the subject can know through reflection alone. A deceived subject and an ordinary subject can share internally accessible evidence, even though their beliefs fare differently in their environments19.

On the reported view, this can threaten a demanding kind of reflective knowledge without excluding all ordinary knowledge. The distinction matters because an externalist account does not require the subject to verify every external condition of knowing. It might allow accurate perception in an ordinary environment even where reflection alone cannot eliminate systematic deception20.

The IEP also raises an objection to the distribution of such possibilities: for each deceptive world one might imagine an equally internally indistinguishable world arranged benevolently so that beliefs are true. Indistinguishability by itself therefore does not establish that beliefs are false in nearly all relevant worlds. The modal formulation needs more than the existence of a deceiving alternative21.

A changed circumstance can change the method’s standing

An invented camera-identification system may be accurate in an ordinary doorway but unable to distinguish faces from photographs in a doorway used for a staged demonstration. An accurate identification in the second setting needs evaluation against that setting’s actual alternatives. This is why environmental comparisons cannot be replaced by a person’s general success rate. At the same time, including every logically possible deception would threaten ordinary knowledge. The account must explain which circumstances are relevant rather than switching opportunistically between a broad and narrow evaluation22.

Probability and the explanation of success

A rare event and a poorly grounded belief need not coincide. The winner who reads an authenticated result knows an improbable outcome through discriminating evidence. The observer who accepts an unauthenticated prediction may believe a highly probable outcome without comparable support for this particular case. The harmless propositional-luck examples and the lottery objections concern different comparisons; the probability of the event cannot do both jobs at once23.

Consider an invented warehouse with one mislabeled box among thousands. An employee may have a high chance of selecting a correctly labeled box by random choice. If another employee opens a particular box and checks its contents, that examination supplies a different epistemic route even if occasional checking errors are possible. The comparison asks whether evidence identifies the particular truth, rather than whether most selections from the warehouse would have been successful.

That does not prove a general theorem that probability never suffices for knowledge. The lottery verdict is disputed, and different theories relate knowledge to probability in different ways. It does show why a bare statistic may leave the explanatory question unanswered. The theory must say whether its relevant comparison concerns an outcome selected at random, the actual evidence or nearby applications of a method.

Risk as another vocabulary

The SEP notes Pritchard’s later suggestion that the relevant issues may be expressed in terms of risk: a belief falls short of knowledge if too much risk of false belief attends its formation. Risk language can make the practical issue clearer, but it does not remove the requirement to specify relevant possibilities or explain the relation without presupposing knowledge24.

For an instrument reading, asking what ordinary changes could make it misleading may be more informative than merely calling the reading lucky. But low risk still needs a reference situation and a method. A method that succeeds in one environment can fail in another. Explaining those dependencies is the work the vocabulary must support.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Unearned opportunities do not invalidate good evidence

A person might gain access to an archive through sheer good fortune, then examine a well-authenticated document carefully. Another might work diligently through a misleading archive and reach a true conclusion by coincidence. Effort and epistemic success can come apart. The anti-luck distinction concerns the relationship between the conclusion and the inquiry, not whether every opportunity was deserved.

Connections

Responses to Gettier compares safety with sensitivity and aptness. Knowing that you know separates meeting conditions from identifying them. The value of knowledge asks whether success through ability has value beyond the truth obtained. The larger skeptical setting belongs with Cartesian skepticism.
