In short

A true belief can guide a successful action just as knowledge can, so the extra value of knowledge needs explaining. Stability, reliability, achievement and suitability for practical reasoning offer different answers. The swamping argument challenges an account that values a reliable process only for producing a truth already possessed. It does not show that reliability is useless for choosing sources or conducting future inquiry. The debate also asks whether understanding, rather than knowledge alone, is the epistemic good we especially want.

Layer 1Re-learn

Two successful guides

Imagine two people directing you to a station. One knows the route; the other guesses correctly. If you follow the same route and arrive safely, the immediate result is identical. Why prefer the knowledgeable guide? One obvious answer is that before choosing, you want a dependable guide. But that answer concerns expected success; the philosophical puzzle holds the successful outcome fixed and asks what knowing adds.

The puzzle is not an invitation to stop checking sources. Before acting, you usually do not know which answers are true. Evidence and reliability matter greatly to that decision. The value problem asks a different question: once a belief is true, is knowing it still better than merely believing it truly? Keeping the comparison fixed prevents the practical importance of reliability from automatically becoming an answer to the puzzle.

Three questions hidden in one

The primary value problem asks why knowledge is more valuable than true belief. The secondary problem asks why it is better than epistemic standings falling short of knowledge, including justified true belief. The tertiary problem asks why knowledge is better in kind, rather than simply occupying a point on a continuum of improvement.

A proposal can answer one without answering the others. If justification adds value, it may explain why knowing is better than an unsupported guess. That does not yet explain why a justified true belief rescued by coincidence is worse than knowledge. And a small extra value does not establish that knowing marks a distinctive category of achievement.

A process and its product

Zagzebski’s coffee analogy asks whether a good cup from a reliable machine is better than an equally good cup from an unreliable machine. If the machine’s reliability matters only because it produces good coffee, that value seems exhausted once the coffee is already good. The swamping argument applies that reasoning to true beliefs produced by reliable cognitive processes.

The word only does important work. If a process has other valuable features, or if knowledge includes more than a detached product, the conclusion needs further argument. That is where competing accounts of epistemic value enter.

Sources for this part: Pritchard et al. 2025, § 1; Pritchard et al. 2025, § 2.

Layer 2The full story

Plato’s question and the held-fixed comparison

In the Meno, Socrates asks whether someone with true opinion about the road to Larisa can be as good a guide as someone who knows it. Meno initially replies that the knower is always right while the holder of opinion is sometimes wrong. Socrates presses the qualification: can someone be wrong while holding true opinion? The comparison concerns a true opinion, not a mixture of true and false guesses1.

That logical move remains central to the contemporary problem. A record of future errors may make an unreliable source a worse choice. It does not establish that one already true belief differs in present value from another solely because its source usually errs. The arguments for an additional value need to say what is being evaluated: an isolated belief, a continuing capacity, a relationship to evidence, or a whole episode of inquiry.

Read the original

Plato's Meno, in Jowett's translation2

Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it.

The translation links fastening with the dialogue’s account of recollection. It should not simply be rewritten as a modern assertion that every instance of JTB is stable. The SEP uses the passage to introduce a practical advantage: knowing the way may make someone less easily discouraged by an initially puzzling stretch of road3.

Stability is a capacity to withstand misleading changes

Suppose the station route begins by turning away from the visible station building. Someone who has only a lucky true hunch may abandon it; someone who understands the street layout may continue. Stability here need not mean stubbornness. Persisting through every counterargument would be a defect. The valuable stability would be a capacity to retain a truth through misleading appearances while remaining responsive to genuine corrections.

The SEP’s discussion treats this as a practical instrumental advantage. It broadens the comparison from a single successful action to what happens when the agent encounters new information. That can help explain why knowing is useful over time. It also shows why the answer does not consist merely in pointing at the truth of the present belief4.

There is a further question about necessity. If some knowledge is fragile because the person later becomes confused, does that remove its current value? A stability proposal needs to specify whether it attributes a general tendency, an essential feature or a particular practical benefit. The mere possibility of instability does not refute a tendency claim, while a universal stability claim faces a stronger burden.

The swamping argument unpacked

The argument, step by step

A challenge to purely instrumental reliability5

  1. Suppose the reliability of a belief-forming process is valuable only as a means to true belief.
  2. Suppose the target belief is already true.
  3. If attaining a valuable end exhausts the value of a property valuable only as a means to that end, reliability adds no further value to this true belief.
  4. Therefore, an account relying only on that instrumental value does not explain why knowing is better than merely believing truly.

The third premise states the swamping assumption. It is not a truth of logic. The SEP discusses objections to that assumption as well as responses that accept the challenge but change the account of knowledge’s value.

The argument also reaches beyond one particular reliabilist theory. Any view that treats every additional epistemic value solely as a tool for obtaining truth can face the same question after truth is obtained. The SEP notes that internalist and veritist approaches can share the relevant structure. Labeling an account internalist therefore does not automatically protect it6.

Conversely, the argument does not condemn reliable inquiry. At the stage of selecting a process, its expected tendency to produce truth is relevant. A challenge about the completed product’s extra value does not imply that the process has no prospective value. Distinguishing those evaluation points prevents the coffee illustration from becoming an argument for indiscriminate trust.

Is value exhausted when the end is absent as well?

Carter and Jarvis challenge the swamping thesis by considering a complementary principle: if a property matters only as a means to an end, and the end is absent, perhaps the property also adds no value. The SEP reports their argument that combining these principles with further assumptions would make nonfactive epistemic properties such as justification never valuable7.

The objection is not simply that the original argument has an unpopular conclusion. It asks whether the proposed account of instrumental value leaves any room for evaluating an epistemically good attempt. A careful investigation ending in error may seem better than an irresponsible guess ending in error, even though both fail to obtain truth. If that comparison is legitimate, the relation between means and ends is more complex than the simple exhaustion model suggests.

The SEP also records replies and alternative challenges. This disagreement is a reason to state the swamping premise expressly rather than presenting the coffee analogy as a decisive demonstration all parties accept8.

The machine-product picture

Zagzebski’s further diagnosis concerns treating belief like a detachable product of a machine. A valuable machine does not automatically make each product more valuable. On the reported alternative, the knowing state includes both the true belief and the manner of acquiring it. The process is then part of the evaluated epistemic state, rather than merely an external means whose value must somehow transfer to the product9.

Consider an invented comparison between an independently completed scientific investigation and an answer copied without understanding. The written sentences may be identical. The episodes of inquiry are not. If the object of evaluation is the whole achievement, their difference can matter without changing the content of the answer. Whether knowledge must always have that achievement structure is another issue.

Credit approaches developed by Riggs and Greco emphasize the agent’s responsibility for arriving at truth. The SEP also notes disagreement within virtue epistemology about whether credit theories actually establish extra value. It would be misleading to treat the virtue label as a single answer shared by everyone10.

Final value and intrinsic value

A distinction from value theory clarifies another response. Something is finally valuable when it is valued for its own sake; it is intrinsically valuable when its value rests on its intrinsic features. Those categories need not coincide. The SEP uses a dress formerly belonging to Princess Diana: its historical relation can make it worth valuing for its own sake even though the relation is extrinsic11.

Applied to belief, the distinction opens a possibility. A true belief’s relation to a valuable cognitive trait might affect its final value even if the belief’s internal content is the same as another’s. This does not prove that the relation has that value. It shows why one cannot establish sameness of all value merely by pointing to sameness of intrinsic properties12.

That is a conceptual repair to a premise, rather than an answer to every value problem. A theory must still say which historical or causal relations warrant positive evaluation and why. Otherwise any unusual origin could be counted as extra epistemic value without explanation.

The achievement proposal

The SEP formulates a robust virtue approach using two theses: knowledge is a cognitive achievement, and achievements have final value unlike merely lucky successes. A success because of ability seems different in kind from a fortunate hit. If both theses hold, the proposal can address not just the primary or secondary problem but the tertiary problem of distinctive value13.

The theses are separate. Valuing achievements does not establish that all knowledge is achievement. Identifying knowledge with achievement does not establish that achievements have the relevant value. The survey considers objections to both, including the thought that trivial achievements may have very little overall value14.

The reply distinguishes value as achievement from value all things considered. Completing a pointless task skilfully may display a positive feature while wasting time or serving a bad goal. Likewise, a knower might learn a harmful fact or pursue a morally objectionable inquiry. A claim about epistemic value should not silently become a claim that every instance of knowing is ethically or practically desirable.

Environmental luck presses on achievement

The SEP imagines a skilful archer hitting the only target without an arrow-repelling forcefield. Nothing disrupts the actual performance. The success can seem creditable to ability, even though the surrounding circumstances make failure easy. This differs from the two-gust example, in which an intervening coincidence restores a disrupted shot15.

Fake-barn country has the corresponding epistemic structure. If the perceptual success is an achievement while knowledge is absent, achievement alone is insufficient. But the verdict about knowledge is disputed. Greco revises the account of relevant ability, while Sosa distinguishes animal from reflective knowledge and permits the former in the barn case. The survey makes those differences explicit16.

This matters to the value debate because an achievement theory cannot get its result merely by changing the word used for knowing. It needs the independently understood achievement category to fit the epistemic cases. When the fit fails, the theorist must either revise the achievement account, revise the knowledge verdict or add something to the theory.

Understanding as a competing good

Kvanvig directs attention to understanding. The SEP distinguishes objectual understanding of a subject matter from propositional understanding and describes his view as requiring a grasp of coherence among beliefs. The suggestion is not that any coherent story is valuable understanding; the account has truth-related constraints as well17.

A student may accumulate correct facts about a mechanism without grasping how its parts depend on one another. Explaining its operation requires more than repeating the facts. That gives a reason to value understanding separately, regardless of whether knowledge also has a distinctive value.

The alternatives need not be exclusive. One can value reliable answers for immediate decisions and explanatory grasp for transfer to unfamiliar situations. The philosophical challenge is to identify their structures and values precisely, not to crown one word as the name of every cognitive good. Understanding and wisdom develops that distinction.

The object of evaluation can be larger than one belief

Imagine paying for an investigation that yields one correct answer, and comparing it with an equally correct answer found by guessing. The answers as inscriptions may be equally useful for one immediate purpose. The investigation may also have established a method, documented dependencies and developed a capacity for further questions. Those are benefits of a larger epistemic undertaking. An explanation relying on them should acknowledge that it has enlarged the object evaluated, rather than announcing that an isolated true sentence contains all those benefits.

The SEP’s contrast between a detached product and a knowing state incorporating its source is relevant here. It permits evaluating a relation or activity as part of the epistemic good. The disagreement then concerns whether the larger state is what knowledge is, and which features explain its extra value18.

This also clarifies why a proposal can be practically persuasive without resolving every philosophical comparison. Institutions reasonably prefer dependable investigators to lucky guessers. That preference does not by itself show that each individual true belief from the investigator is finally valuable in a distinct way. A defence of that further conclusion needs a value theory and an account of the belief’s relation to the process. Keeping the levels separate gives the practical argument its due without claiming that it settles more than it does.

Knowledge and practical reasoning

The survey presents lottery cases as support for a practical role for knowledge. A person may have extremely strong probabilistic support that a ticket will lose yet be criticized for throwing it away before the draw. After reading the result in a reliable newspaper, the person may appropriately discard it even though the newspaper’s error rate exceeds the ticket’s probability of winning19.

The comparison suggests that the epistemic standing needed for practical reasoning is not reducible to a single numerical confidence level. Knowing might authorize reliance in a way a stronger but nonknowledgeable justification does not. The SEP presents this as a potential answer to at least the secondary problem, conditional on the proposed link between knowledge and practical reasoning20.

It does not settle every decision theory or every knowledge norm. A cautious gambler might have other reasons to keep a ticket, and a newspaper reader might face unusual stakes. The example’s role is to compare routes to a belief, not to legislate one action for every situation.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Evaluate what is valuable, and to whom

A discovery can be valuable to the discoverer as an achievement, to a patient as usable information, and to a scientific community as an explanation. Those are different comparisons. A patient may not care who deserves credit for a correct diagnosis, while a research evaluation may care a great deal. Asking about knowledge’s value becomes clearer when the object, beneficiary and kind of value are specified.

Where to go next

Read Meno 97a–98a for the held-fixed comparison and the fastening metaphor. The Value of Knowledge § 1 distinguishes the three problems, § 2 examines swamping and kinds of value, and § 3 considers achievements. Its §§ 4 and 6 widen the discussion to understanding and practical reasoning. Read those as competing proposals with separate premises, rather than as a list of interchangeable solutions21.
