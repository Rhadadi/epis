The tripartite analysis

In short

The three-part analysis asks whether a belief is true, whether the person holds it, and whether it has adequate support. These are different questions: evidence can be good when the conclusion is false, and a conclusion can be true when the evidence is bad. An analysis claims more than a useful checklist; it claims that its conditions capture every possible case of knowing. That ambitious claim makes a single counterexample decisive. The history and even the meaning of the three conditions are less settled than the familiar formula suggests.

Layer 1Re-learn

Three questions about one belief

Suppose a station employee tells you that the evening train leaves from platform four. You accept her answer. There are now three matters to separate. Does it actually leave from four? Do you believe that it does? Does the employee’s answer give you adequate support? The tripartite analysis treats truth, belief and justification as individually necessary and jointly sufficient for propositional knowledge.

The first question concerns the train, the second your attitude, and the third the standing of your belief. A reliable employee might be working from an outdated timetable. You would have support for a false belief. Conversely, you might invent the platform number and accidentally get it right. Then the world agrees with your belief, but it does not agree because your belief was appropriately supported. Distinguishing these questions explains why adding truth to belief, or support to belief, appears insufficient.

Truth here does not mean that a claim has already been proved. An unexamined coin can have landed heads even though nobody knows which side is up. A truth can exist without anyone having access to it. The truth condition therefore does not give us a procedure for finding truths; it specifies a constraint on what can be known.

Support is not a speech you have already given

In ordinary conversation, justifying a claim often means defending it to someone. That is an activity. The justification condition need not require that activity. A person can hold a well-supported belief without having rehearsed an argument for it, and can deliver an eloquent argument for a poorly supported belief. Ichikawa and Steup warn against treating justification as a dialectical performance the believer must already have completed.

There is a further distinction between having good evidence available and forming this belief on its basis. Imagine that you have the current timetable open but ignore it and trust a horoscope. The timetable does not automatically repair the way you reached your answer. The literature distinguishes propositional justification, concerning what your evidence supports, from doxastic justification, concerning a belief appropriately based on that support.

Belief does not simply mean high confidence

The analysis usually concerns outright belief: treating the question as settled, rather than merely assigning an answer a high probability. The relation between belief and confidence is disputed. In Radford’s examinee case, a person answers historical questions correctly while taking the answers to be guesses. One response attributes a tacit belief; another denies that sufficiently uncertain answering amounts to knowledge. The example leaves room for argument about both belief and knowledge, rather than proving that confidence never matters.

The practical distinction is between having information and recognizing that you have it. An unconfident but well-trained employee may remember the platform correctly. A confident stranger may have no information at all. Neither confidence nor its absence settles the three questions by itself.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

A checklist and an analysis have different ambitions

A checklist helps you notice relevant considerations. An analysis claims that those considerations give the conditions under which someone knows, including in hypothetical cases. Its usual target is a necessary connection, not a correlation that happens to hold in the actual world. That is why imaginary examples matter: a coherent possible case satisfying the conditions without knowledge challenges sufficiency, even if such cases almost never occur1.

This also explains why the Gettier problem does not make the three questions useless. A fourth question may be needed about the relationship between support and truth. Truth, belief and support can remain necessary even when their conjunction is insufficient. Rejecting sufficiency is different from rejecting each constituent condition2.

The historical label needs caution

Calling JTB the traditional analysis can create a false impression of a formula accepted unchanged for centuries. The SEP survey says that its canonical history is a convenient fiction: the formulation was especially prominent in twentieth-century criticism, while earlier views were more varied. Plato’s treatment of true opinion matters to the story, but that does not license attributing the modern package to every earlier philosopher3.

Connections

The next page, The Gettier problem, asks how a belief can meet all three conditions and still be true accidentally. The problem of the criterion asks how we decide which cases of knowing are the right tests in the first place. The distinction between evidence and properly using evidence belongs with Justification.

Where to go next

Start with Ichikawa and Steup’s account of the three conditions in § 1, especially the warning about the history of JTB and the distinction between justification and actively justifying. Their preamble explains what an analysis aims to deliver; § 3 explains the failure of joint sufficiency. Read them in that order before comparing proposed repairs4.
