Knowing that you know

Going deeper on a section of “What Is Knowledge?”.

In short

Knowing a proposition and knowing that you know it are different claims. The KK principle connects them, but treating that principle as universal has substantive consequences. A formal model can include positive introspection without establishing that real people always possess it. The subject’s awareness of meeting a condition also differs from actually meeting it. Separating those questions helps keep skepticism about reflection from becoming skepticism about every ordinary belief.

Layer 1Re-learn

A claim at two levels

Let Kp mean that a subject knows p. KKp then means that the subject knows that they know p. The principle Kp implies KKp is commonly called positive introspection. The SEP discussion of epistemic logic treats it as one of several optional principles governing access to one’s own epistemic state and discusses Hintikka’s defence.

If a doctor knows the test result but remains unsure whether the instrument met an obscure calibration requirement, there is a possible gap between a correct epistemic standing and recognizing that standing. This invented example does not establish a counterexample without further assumptions about knowledge. It identifies what a proposed counterexample would have to separate.

The gap is not merely the difference between confidence and knowledge. A person could confidently think that they know while being wrong at both levels. Conversely, a person could lack confidence about their knowledge while still meeting the relevant first-order conditions. The belief condition itself is disputed in uncertain cases, so one should avoid assuming that every hesitant answer already constitutes knowledge.

A condition can hold without a certificate

The IEP discussion distinguishes being free of Gettier luck from knowing that one is free of it. A knowledge analysis requiring the first need not automatically require the second. The subject must meet the conditions; meeting them and verifying them are different demands.

That distinction applies to other conditions too. A belief might be formed safely even if the subject cannot assess every nearby possibility. The SEP describes safety as externalist when its obtaining depends on features outside the subject’s accessible standpoint.

Sources for this part: Rendsvig et al. 2025, § 2.6; Ichikawa and Steup 2026, § 1.2; Engel Jr. n.d., § 3; Ichikawa and Steup 2026, § 5.2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Why repeated introspection is not a free assumption

If KK applies universally, it can be reapplied: Kp leads to KKp, which leads to KKKp. The logical iteration is straightforward. Whether its result is acceptable depends on what the operator represents. A highly idealized logic may describe commitments or available consequences rather than a finite person consciously rehearsing every iteration. The discussion of epistemic logic makes the interpretation of its principles central1.

That prevents two mistakes. One should not refute an idealized model merely by observing that a person cannot recite an infinite list. One should also not export the model’s introspection principles to ordinary psychology without defending that application.

Margins for error

Weisberg’s Formal Epistemology presents Williamson’s argument against KK using a jar of jellybeans. A safety-related margin links knowledge of one quantity to nearby cases in which a slightly different quantity would be hard to distinguish. Combined with repeated introspection, the argument leads to implausible consequences about what the subject knows. The force depends on the premises connecting knowledge and the margin, not on the notation alone2.

The same discussion connects rejection of KK to skeptical arguments that demand access to every relevant alternative. Rejecting the universal principle can block an argument relying on it, while leaving other skeptical arguments untouched. It is therefore neither a complete refutation of skepticism nor a declaration that reflective knowledge is impossible3.

Connections

Epistemic luck distinguishes reflective from ordinary epistemic standing. The closure argument concerns knowing consequences of known propositions, which is different from knowing one’s own epistemic status. The two principles should not be merged merely because both contain nested claims about knowledge.

Layer 4Sources

Where to go next

Read Epistemic Logic § 2.6 for positive introspection and its interpretation. Formal Epistemology § 4.3 gives a concrete argument testing it. Epistemic Luck § 3 compares different views of second-order knowledge. These texts distinguish a formal principle, an argument about its limits, and a debate about reflection rather than treating them as the same question4.
