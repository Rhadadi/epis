Knowledge, assertion, and action

Going deeper on a section of “What Is Knowledge?”.

In short

Asserting a claim and relying on it in action give knowledge a possible practical role. The knowledge norm says that proper assertion requires knowing; competing norms require truth or justified belief instead. Evidence from conversational challenges does not uniquely establish one norm. Whether a speaker did wrong also differs from whether the speaker is blameworthy. The action debate similarly asks whether knowing authorizes reliance, rather than merely raising confidence.

Layer 1Re-learn

What a norm of assertion regulates

A person can state something sincerely and still lack adequate evidence. Sincerity answers whether the statement expresses the person’s belief; it does not answer whether the assertion is properly made. The knowledge norm proposes a further requirement: assert a proposition only if you know it. The SEP discussion compares that proposal with weaker truth and justification requirements.

An invented example helps separate them. A traveller guesses the platform correctly and tells a family where to go. The statement is true and sincerely believed. It nevertheless lacks the support a justification norm demands. If the traveller instead uses excellent but coincidentally misleading evidence, a Gettier-style case could meet a justified-belief norm while failing a knowledge norm. The norms differ precisely in these boundary cases.

A conversational challenge is not a proof

Questions about how someone knows what they asserted can suggest that speakers represent themselves as knowing. But that representation might be explained without a constitutive knowledge norm. The SEP considers a combination of self-representation and an independently applicable prohibition on misleading listeners. That can explain some of the same conversational patterns.

The distinction is between what the act represents and what rule defines its proper performance. Evidence for the former does not automatically prove the latter. A developed defence of the knowledge norm needs to explain why its account is better than those alternatives, not merely observe that knowledge questions are familiar.

Error and blameworthiness

An assertion can violate a proposed norm even where the speaker reasonably thinks its condition is satisfied. A stopped-clock assertion is a useful test because the speaker may have a justified true belief without knowing. The SEP presents such Gettiered assertions among objections that knowledge requirements are too strong.

That does not automatically settle the objection. A norm’s defender might distinguish failing to satisfy it from deserving blame for that failure. An opponent might argue that ordinary evaluation concerns the speaker’s reasonable epistemic position instead. The dispute should keep compliance, excuse and criticism distinct.

Sources for this part: Pagin and Marsili 2025, § 5.1.4.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

The audience can change the focus

Some proposals make the audience’s opportunity to know central: the speaker should assert only if the assertion puts hearers in a position to know. The SEP reports García-Carpintero’s transmission formulation and related views. This changes the focus from a speaker’s possession of knowledge to the communicative relation1.

A speaker may know something while communicating it in a misleading or inaccessible way. Conversely, a listener may have independent evidence that makes an assertion informative even when the speaker’s standing is poor. A transmission norm therefore needs an account of how speaker, content and audience interact; it is not merely another spelling of the same speaker condition.

Action and probability

The lottery comparison in The Value of Knowledge contrasts discarding a ticket on highly probable expectations with discarding it after reading the announced result. The latter may involve knowledge even if the newspaper has a larger error probability than the ticket’s chance of winning. The argument is that the epistemic standing appropriate for reliance is not exhausted by numerical confidence2.

Pragmatic encroachment goes further by proposing that practical stakes can affect whether a person knows, even without a change in evidence. The SEP stresses that this is a disputed thesis rather than an analysis accepted across epistemology3. See Pragmatic encroachment for the larger question, and The value of knowledge for its bearing on epistemic value.
