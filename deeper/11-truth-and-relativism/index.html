Deeper study · Chapter 11

Truth, Relativism, and Objectivity

For when a section didn't click, or left you wanting more: each page explains the idea again, tells the full story, fills in what the chapter leaves out, and gives the sources.

William Anders, Apollo 8, Earthrise, 24 December 1968

← Back to Chapter 11

  • Short study · 3 minWhy truth mattersA belief can be sincere, well supported, or comforting while still being false. Truth marks a kind of correctness that those features do not replace.
  • Short study · 3 minTruth-bearersBefore judging a statement true or false, identify what it says. Sentences, utterances, beliefs, and propositions are different candidates for bearing truth.
  • Full study · 12 minTheories of truthA theory of truth asks what it is for a claim to be true, rather than merely how we can check it. Correspondence connects truth to the world; coherence connects it to a specified system; pragmatist approaches connect it to inquiry and practice.
  • Short study · 3 minThe liar paradoxThe liar combines self-reference with familiar principles about truth. If a sentence says that it is false, apparently reasonable steps lead to conflict.
  • Short study · 3 minRealism and anti-realismA claim can concern a reality we may be unable to investigate. Realism and anti-realism dispute how truth in such cases relates to knowability, verification, and meaning.
  • Full study · 12 minRelativismRelativism makes a claim about what depends on which standard or framework. Different beliefs, different evidence, and different truth values are not the same kind of dependence.
  • Short study · 3 minSocial constructionismCalling something socially constructed leaves several questions unanswered. The target might be a classification, an object, an institution, or a human trait.
  • Short study · 3 minPerspectivism and objectivityInquiry takes place from a position with particular information, skills, and interests. Acknowledging that position can help expose limitations.
  • Short study · 3 minPost-truth and bullshitA false statement can result from error, deliberate deception, or indifference to whether it is true. Frankfurt’s account of bullshit focuses on that indifference.
