In short

Before judging a statement true or false, identify what it says. Sentences, utterances, beliefs, and propositions are different candidates for bearing truth. The same sentence can express different contents on different occasions. Different sentences can express the same content. Specifying the content can dissolve an apparent contradiction without settling every debate about truth.

Layer 1Re-learn

Expression and content

Imagine two people using the sentence I arrived yesterday. The words match, but they refer to different speakers. If uttered on different days, they also concern different arrival dates. One utterance can be true and the other false without one fully specified claim having incompatible truth values.

Now imagine two languages reporting that an invented station has closed. Their sentences differ, but the reports can concern the same closure. This motivates distinguishing the expression from what is expressed. A proposition is one philosophical candidate for that content.

Glanzberg lists beliefs, propositions, sentences, and utterances as candidate truth-bearers. Different theories assign primacy differently, and choosing a particular account of truth does not automatically settle the choice of bearer. Meaningful content is a shared concern across the options.

Specify what changed

In the arrival example, the sentence type did not change. The occasion and reference changed. A disagreement about the arrival date should therefore identify the speaker and relevant date before drawing a conclusion about relative truth.

This familiar context dependence is distinct from contemporary truth relativism. Baghramian and Carter explain that contextualists allow context to affect expressed content, whereas New Relativists propose assessment sensitivity for a single content in selected domains. Resolving an indexical ambiguity therefore does not alone refute that further proposal.

Sources for this part: Glanzberg 2025, § 6.1; Baghramian and Carter 2025, § 5.1.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Different theoretical jobs

Tarski takes sentences as bearers for the purpose of defining truth for a language, without thereby settling every metaphysical dispute about propositions. Glanzberg emphasizes that these are fully interpreted sentences and, in the simplified presentation, are assumed not to shift their content across occasions.1

A formal example’s clean assumptions should not be silently carried over to ordinary speech. If a sentence contains here, today, or this, its use needs further specification. A claim that a quoted sentence is true can otherwise conceal disagreement about which occasion or reference is meant.

A change of belief is another change

Suppose someone first believes the station is open and later believes it is closed. The change in their attitude does not establish that one proposition became true by their adopting it. Perhaps the station actually closed; perhaps they corrected a mistake; perhaps their evidence became misleading. These possibilities differ and need separate investigation.

Russell’s account makes beliefs dependent on minds for their existence while their truth depends on the corresponding facts. That is one historical theory of the relation between bearer and truth, not an uncontested definition of every candidate bearer.2

What to preserve in a reconstruction

When making a claim precise, preserve its scope and reference. A report that some station closed is weaker than a report that this station closed. A claim about yesterday differs from a claim about now. Precision helps only if it keeps the original claim rather than replacing it with an easier one.

See sense and reference for content and reference, and relativism for the distinction between changed content and changed assessment. Theories of truth explains why the choice of bearer influences, but does not uniquely determine, a theory.
