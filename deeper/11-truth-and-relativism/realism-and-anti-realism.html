Realism and anti-realism

In short

A claim can concern a reality we may be unable to investigate. Realism and anti-realism dispute how truth in such cases relates to knowability, verification, and meaning. The answer can vary by domain and by the form of realism at issue. Uncertainty is not itself a rejection of realism. Nor does using a Tarskian truth definition automatically settle the dispute.

Layer 1Re-learn

What if no check survives?

Imagine an unrecorded event in a fictional village centuries ago. There were either six or seven people at a particular meeting, but every relevant record is gone. A realist can hold that the claim about six people has a truth value even though we cannot determine it.

Glanzberg connects one influential characterization of realism with bivalence: each relevant bearer is true or false. Dummett makes this principle central to realism about a domain. Other characterizations emphasize an objective world and objective reference, so the semantic formulation is not the only approach.

Distinguish lacking an answer from denying that the claim has either truth value. The first can simply be an epistemic limitation. The second is a stronger proposal about truth or meaning.

Verification and assertibility

Glanzberg reconstructs a verificationist example of anti-realism in which truth is connected to in-principle verifiability. For a claim that cannot be verified or refuted in principle, the account need not maintain bivalence. Warranted assertibility supplies another epistemic formulation.

This should not be reduced to the claim that whatever anyone currently believes is true. The relevant notions concern verification or warrant, with standards needing specification. Nor does one anti-realist formulation define every use of the label.

Sources for this part: Glanzberg 2025, § 4.1; Glanzberg 2025, § 4.2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

The T-schema leaves work to do

Glanzberg explicitly notes that Convention T does not discriminate between realist and anti-realist accounts. Both can use the semantic apparatus while interpreting its fundamental notions differently. The background logic and the account of reference or satisfaction may carry the substantive disagreement.1

Thus the instance linking the fictional meeting report to the number present does not, on its own, resolve the status of an inaccessible past claim. One must investigate how the relevant account treats the right-hand condition and the principles of evaluation.

An ideal is more than current acceptance

Putnam’s internal realism connects truth to what would be justified under ideal epistemic conditions. Glanzberg explains the relation to pragmatist themes and Putnam’s preference for distinguishing the view from metaphysical realism. An idealized condition is different from a vote among present inquirers.2

The approach still owes an account of the ideal and its connection to truth. If the ideal is defined as whatever reliably finds truth, the explanation may rely on the concept it was meant to illuminate. If it is defined independently, one must defend why it has the required relationship. Theories of truth develops the related debate about inquiry.

Keep the domain visible

Realism about a past event differs from realism about unobservable scientific entities, and both differ from a debate about moral properties. A position can accept one while questioning another. Glanzberg explicitly allows restrictions by subject matter even while using a global formulation for exposition.3

Scientific realism distinguishes metaphysical, semantic, and epistemic commitments in that setting. Relativism examines framework dependence, which should not be identified with every rejection of bivalence. Truth-bearers explains why specifying the claim remains essential.
