Rhetorical tactics that corrupt discussion

In short

Discussion can fail even when nobody has offered an invalid inference. Participants can block questions, disguise a claim, or make answering impossible. Assess what a move does to the shared inquiry before diagnosing the speaker’s motives. Reasonable persuasion presents a position effectively while keeping it open to criticism. A useful response restores the claim, the evidence standard, and a fair opportunity to answer.

Layer 1Re-learn

A process can be undermined

Imagine a tenant meeting about proposed repairs. One speaker makes ten accusations, changes the subject whenever an answer arrives, and treats requests for clarification as admissions of guilt. The defect is partly procedural: the group cannot identify which claim remains disputed or what would settle it.

Hansen’s account of pragma-dialectics explains a theory that evaluates fallacies as violations of rules for critical discussion. Those rules include allowing positions to be expressed, defending challenged claims, avoiding equivocation and acknowledging the discussion’s outcome. This is a normative model of reasonable discussion, rather than a psychological finding about every argument.

In the invented meeting, write down one accusation and one requested kind of support. Resolve or record that issue before opening another. A collection of unsupported accusations does not acquire support merely because answering it takes time. Nor does a delayed response show that the person cannot answer.

Distinguish clarification from retreat

A speaker may reasonably narrow an overstatement after criticism. The repair is complete when they acknowledge the narrower position and abandon the unsupported part. If the broader accusation returns whenever convenient, the discussion has not resolved it. Track the exact claim rather than assuming that a defensible weaker statement vindicates the stronger one.

Likewise, a request for evidence can advance inquiry or obstruct it. Ask whether it identifies a consequential uncertainty and whether the answer is taken into account. A new request can be reasonable when it exposes a genuine weakness in what was supplied. Repeating an already answered request without engaging the answer leaves the disputed issue untouched.

Sources for this part: Hansen 2024, § 3.6.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Persuasion has legitimate forms

Clear examples, memorable wording and attention to an audience’s concerns are not automatically manipulative. Hansen describes strategic manoeuvring through topic choice, audience orientation and presentation. In the pragma-dialectical model, these become fallacious when they violate the standards of reasonable discussion. Walton’s approach, also reviewed there, distinguishes different dialogue purposes rather than treating every exchange as one kind of critical discussion.1

A negotiation about repair dates can legitimately seek compromise. An investigation of whether repairs occurred needs evidence about events. Confusing those purposes can make an acceptable bargaining move look like a factual concession. State the task before evaluating the tactic.

Make correction possible

An accusation whose every denial counts as further proof has removed a possible route to correction. Ask what evidence could count against it. That question does not guarantee that such evidence exists; it asks the claimant to specify a testable evidential relationship. If no answer is allowed to matter, continuing the exchange may add no information.

The burden of supporting a claim cannot be discharged merely by demanding its disproof. The IEP misplaced-burden entry explains that error. Similarly, responding to a weaker substitute for someone’s actual position is a straw-man failure. Return to the position that was stated and the evidence offered for it.2

A fair endpoint can be agreement, a recorded uncertainty, or a clearly located disagreement. Nobody owes unlimited time to an exchange that repeatedly prevents progress. Ending it does not establish the truth of either side’s position. See open-mindedness and its limits.

Where to go next
