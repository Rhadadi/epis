In short

Evidence may leave more than one theory possible, but possibility is weaker than equal support. Underdetermination concerns both which part of a tested package should be revised and which rival package should be accepted. Its force depends on the evidence available, the alternatives considered, and the standards used to compare them. Some disputes can be resolved by further observations; stronger cases concern rivals that agree on every relevant observable result. The lesson is to investigate the source and scope of the remaining uncertainty.

Layer 1Re-learn

A failed prediction has several possible explanations

Imagine an invented experiment testing whether a material expands at a specified temperature. The calculation predicts a change, but the instrument registers none. The material theory might be wrong. So might the temperature reading, the instrument calibration, or the assumption that the sample was pure.

The tested prediction came from a package of claims. Its failure shows that the package cannot all be right, assuming the conflicting report is accepted. It does not by itself identify the particular claim to abandon. This is holist underdetermination.

Now imagine two accounts of the material that both fit all the measurements collected so far. Choosing between these rival accounts presents contrastive underdetermination. More measurements might separate them, but that possibility must be established rather than assumed.

Three questions that should stay separate

Can a rival be made compatible with the data? Is it as well supported as the leading account? Would it make the same predictions under all relevant future tests? Those are progressively stronger questions. A theory rescued by an unsupported exception may fit an observation without gaining the same evidential standing as a well-tested rival.

Likewise, a theory that fits existing measurements might disagree with its rival outside the measured range. That is different from empirical equivalence across all possible observations. A finite curve-fitting example establishes only the first kind of openness unless additional arguments are supplied.

The practical lesson

State the disputed claim precisely. List which alternatives genuinely fit the relevant evidence and which assumptions generate that fit. Then ask what information would distinguish them. If there is no available discriminator, identify what narrower conclusions they share. Uncertainty about one component does not automatically erase everything the evidence establishes.

Sources for this part: Stanford 2023, § 2.1; Stanford 2023, § 3.1; Stanford 2023, § 2.2, 3.2; Stanford 2023, § 1, 3.2.

Layer 2The full story

Why non-entailment is only the beginning

Many scientific conclusions reach beyond the data from which they are inferred. A set of recorded temperatures does not deductively entail a universal law about every future sample. Underdetermination becomes a more demanding problem when it concerns genuine competitors: different hypotheses or revisions that survive the evidence and might also survive the accepted standards of comparison.1

Start with an invented sequence of measurements. A straight line passes through them. A more complicated curve also passes through them but diverges between the measured points. There are infinitely many such mathematical constructions. This shows that matching the observed points does not logically determine one function. It does not establish that all those functions are plausible scientific explanations.2

Further premises would be needed to move from compatibility to equal justification. We might ask whether the mechanism generating the measurements supports one function, whether a proposed curve violates other established results, and whether its complicated bends have any independent basis. These are evidential questions even though their answers do not deductively follow from the list of points.

The strongest skeptical conclusions therefore need stronger underdetermination claims. Showing that the evidence does not entail a hypothesis is not enough to show that belief in it is unreasonable. Conversely, reminding someone that deduction is not the only kind of reasoning does not show that a particular rival has been adequately excluded.3

A primary text: Mill on unimagined competitors

Mill discussed a difficulty with hypotheses that explain familiar phenomena. His warning includes the possibility that our available alternatives are limited by what we can conceive, not by what the world permits. The passage occurs in his discussion of the limits of hypothetical explanation, rather than a modern theorem about every possible scientific theory.4

Read the original

Accounting for the known phenomena

Accordingly, most thinkers of any degree of sobriety allow that an hypothesis of this kind is not to be received as probably true because it accounts for all the known phenomena; since this is a condition sometimes fulfilled tolerably well by two conflicting hypotheses; while there are probably many others which are equally possible, but which, for want of any thing analogous in our experience, our minds are unfitted to conceive.

5. The text here is the wording of the corpus edition. Its central warning concerns the gap between explanatory fit and a sufficiently complete comparison of possibilities.

There are two worries within the passage. Known competitors may explain the same phenomena. Unknown competitors may fail to appear in our comparison at all. The first asks whether evidence discriminates among options we have. The second asks how confident we should be that our options include an adequate account.

That second worry does not imply that any imagined alternative deserves equal attention. It concerns the possibility of serious alternatives beyond the present menu. The practical response can include deliberately seeking different mechanisms, extending tests into new domains, and specifying which claims would remain supported if the current account were replaced.

Duhem: tests of a package

Stanford’s reconstruction distinguishes Duhem’s argument about physical theory from Quine’s broader holism. For Duhem, testing a physical hypothesis requires other theoretical assumptions and an interpretation of the experimental situation. An apparent failure of the prediction does not establish which component is responsible.6

The point can be represented without committing to either philosopher’s entire epistemology. Let T be the main theory, A the auxiliary assumptions, and E the expected observation. A prediction has the form: if T and A, then E. Suppose E does not occur. We may reject the conjunction T and A, but choosing between rejecting T and revising A requires more information.

The argument, step by step

What the failed prediction establishes

  1. If the theory and the auxiliaries are correct, the predicted observation occurs.
  2. The predicted observation does not occur.
  3. Therefore the theory and the auxiliaries are not all correct.
  4. It does not follow that the main theory, specifically, is false.
7. The logical conclusion concerns the conjunction. The methodological question is how to locate the failure.

This problem arises before one considers a fully developed rival theory. A researcher might remain within the current framework while changing a model of the apparatus. Another might question the framework itself. Both moves can remove the inconsistency; their logical availability does not settle their relative merit.8

Quine’s broader web

Quine extends holism beyond the physical theory involved in a particular test. On the account Stanford discusses, experience bears on a wider network of beliefs, and revisions can occur in different parts of that network. Claims that seem remote from the observation can be retained or revised through changes elsewhere.9

The extension matters. A practical difficulty in identifying a faulty auxiliary is not automatically a demonstration that every belief can be rationally preserved against every possible experience. Nor should Duhem’s more restricted argument be attributed every consequence of Quine’s broader proposal.10

Quine’s position also needs care about the word pragmatic. Stanford explains that Quine does not simply oppose pragmatic considerations to rational or epistemic considerations. His naturalistic account treats the principles governing successful revision as part of how evidence works, rather than separating them from evidential reasoning altogether.11

The reader need not endorse that account to appreciate the issue it raises. If a method of revision depends on background commitments, one must ask how those commitments are assessed. Merely demanding revision without background assumptions does not provide a method that can actually guide inquiry.

The historical contrast: Neptune and Vulcan

Stanford uses the contrasting fortunes of attempts to account for planetary discrepancies to illustrate why a prediction failure does not decide where revision should occur. An additional planet could rescue an existing gravitational account while generating independently checkable consequences. A similar kind of proposal could also fail.12

The familiar Neptune and Vulcan contrast is therefore useful without becoming an automatic rule. Success in one case does not justify every future appeal to an unseen object. Failure in another does not show that proposing unobserved objects is always illegitimate. The assessment turns on the particular prediction, the independent support, and the subsequent tests.13

One should also resist judging every earlier choice solely by the final outcome. A hypothesis that eventually fails might have been a reasonable candidate to investigate. A successful proposal could have been poorly supported when first suggested. The evidential standing at a time and the eventual fate are related, but they are not identical.

Laudan’s challenge: which strength of thesis?

Laudan argues that dramatic conclusions about scientific rationality often rely on a stronger version of underdetermination than the argument establishes. Stanford distinguishes compatibility, explanatory fit, and equal empirical support, as well as the mere possibility of retaining a belief and the rational defensibility of doing so.14

Suppose someone saves the invented expansion theory by saying the material works exactly as predicted except during the failed test. That statement avoids contradiction with the record. It does not explain why the exception occurred or supply evidence that similar exceptions will behave predictably. Logical consistency has been purchased without an evident gain in understanding.

Laudan’s objection is not that evidence deductively forces every rational choice. It is that the absence of deductive compulsion leaves room for ampliative principles of good reasoning. If defenders of a stronger thesis claim those principles still fail to discriminate, they must argue for that further claim.15

Stanford also qualifies the criticism. Quine’s conception of the relation between pragmatic principles and evidence is more complicated than a distinction between psychological possibility and epistemic justification suggests. Invoking simplicity or coherence will not settle every dispute if their application and authority are themselves questioned.16

The exchange gives a useful discipline: specify both the available alternatives and what underdetermined means in that case. A strong conclusion should not quietly borrow evidence for a weaker one.

Contrastive underdetermination and empirical equivalence

Contrastive underdetermination concerns alternatives to the theory as a whole. The scope of the evidence matters: agreement with currently available data is different from agreement with every possible observable result. The latter is usually called empirical equivalence, though the precise formulation depends on what counts as observable and on the relevant background assumptions.17

In the curve example, a new measurement can distinguish the rivals because they disagree at unmeasured points. A proposed pair of empirically equivalent theories is stronger: adding more of the relevant observational evidence will not separate them in the stipulated way. Whether such pairs can always be constructed is an additional philosophical question.18

Even empirical equivalence does not immediately settle evidential equivalence. A realist may appeal to explanatory considerations or background evidence. An opponent may question whether those considerations support truth rather than convenience. Calling theories empirically equivalent identifies an important relation between their consequences; it does not settle every question about what reasons favor belief in them.19

The background also matters when a construction changes assumptions used in observation. A rival must be assessed as an actual package, not praised for matching predictions while the conditions of that match remain hidden. Equivalence relative to one restricted vocabulary or setting need not establish equivalence in every relevant respect.20

The problem of unconceived alternatives

Stanford’s account of unconceived alternatives draws on a recurring historical pattern: investigators accepted theories despite the existence of alternatives that later became available and could also accommodate substantial earlier evidence. The worry concerns our ability to enumerate serious possibilities, rather than the ingenuity of inventing a contrived rival after a theory is specified.21

This is especially relevant to inference to the best explanation. The best explanation among the candidates considered might be excellent relative to those candidates while failing to approximate the best available account in a wider space. Improving the ranking method does not, by itself, ensure that a good candidate entered the comparison.22

The historical argument is defeasible. It does not demonstrate that every current theory faces an equally promising, unconceived rival. Its force can vary with the domain, the maturity of inquiry, and the particular claims being endorsed. Treating it as a universal proof would replace a historically motivated concern with a much stronger thesis.23

The practical issue is selective confidence. A successful theory can support reliable predictions or particular structural claims without justifying equal confidence in every part of its explanatory picture. Conversely, restraint about an unobservable mechanism need not require abandoning tested regularities. Exactly which commitments deserve confidence remains a question for the relevant evidence.

What Bayesian reasoning can and cannot do

Bayesian comparison makes a distinction between equal likelihood and equal posterior support. If two hypotheses give the same likelihood to the evidence, that evidence does not change their prior odds. Different priors can still produce different posteriors. The theorem does not itself justify those priors or ensure that every serious alternative has been represented.24

For a new discriminating test, the question is whether the alternatives assign different probabilities to its possible results. More data can be valuable when those likelihoods differ. Repeating an observation whose likelihood is identical under the rivals will not resolve their relative standing through likelihood alone.25

This framework helps describe a difficulty precisely. It does not make all controversies disappear. An omitted alternative still has no place in the calculation, and different assumptions about reliability or the background can change the likelihoods assigned to the same recorded event.26

Where the argument leaves science

The positions

Different claims, different burdens

Claim What it says What must be shown
Holist A failed package admits multiple revisions. Which revisions are available and defensible.
Current contrast Rivals fit the evidence now available. Whether further relevant tests separate them.
Empirical equivalence Rivals share all relevant observable consequences. The scope and background of the equivalence.
Unconceived rivals Our candidate set may omit serious alternatives. Why that concern applies to this inquiry.
27. None of these claims, stated alone, says that every theory is equally good.

Recognizing underdetermination does not establish that social or political interests decide every remaining choice. Stanford emphasizes that this is a further positive claim requiring evidence. It cannot be inferred simply from the failure of data to dictate a unique answer.28

The useful endpoint is a map of uncertainty: what is shared by the surviving alternatives, what is disputed, what evidence would matter, and where confidence depends on additional assumptions. That map can guide investigation and responsible action even before every theoretical disagreement is resolved.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the short account simplifies

The phrase same evidence can conceal a difference between a finite record and every possible observation. Likewise, fits can mean avoids contradiction, explains, or deserves the same confidence. Stanford’s distinctions make those ambiguities visible. They also explain why one curve-fitting illustration does not settle the epistemology of an entire scientific field.29

The modern connection to Mill is limited but instructive. His passage anticipates the worry that the imaginable alternatives do not exhaust the possible ones. It should be read as a primary discussion in its own argumentative context, rather than attributed every feature of Stanford’s later historical thesis.30

Connections

The Duhem–Quine problem concentrates on diagnosis after failed tests. Inference to the best explanation examines the ranking and candidate-set problems. Bayes’ theorem supplies the calculation behind equal likelihoods and unchanged odds. Scientific realism asks which commitments successful science warrants.
