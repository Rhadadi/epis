Inference to the best explanation

In short

An explanation can support a hypothesis without proving it. Inference to the best explanation compares candidates by how well they would account for the evidence. Its strength depends both on the comparison and on which candidates entered it. The best of a poor selection can still be wrong. A useful explanation should therefore help identify further evidence, rather than merely make the existing facts feel coherent.

Layer 1Re-learn

Start with alternatives

Imagine finding water under an invented laboratory sink. A leaking pipe explains it. So could a spill or condensation. The pipe hypothesis becomes more persuasive if the pipe is wet at a joint, the water returns after the floor is dried, and an independent pressure test exposes a leak. Those observations do more than increase the amount of unexplained water: they discriminate among candidates.

Douven distinguishes explanatory inference from deductive entailment and ordinary statistical inference, while recognizing disputed boundaries. The conclusion of an abductive argument can be defeated by further information. An account that explains the current observations may lose its advantage when a new cause is discovered.

The word best raises two questions: best according to which criteria, and best among which candidates? Chakravartty describes proposed virtues including simplicity, consistency, scope, and unity. Those virtues can be interpreted differently and need not all favor the same hypothesis.

Ranking is not inclusion

Suppose every investigator in the imaginary laboratory considers only different locations for a pipe leak. An excellent ranking of those locations does not help if the water came from an unrelated spill. The best candidate considered can be the best of a bad selection. Chakravartty discusses van Fraassen’s objection in precisely this connection.

The example does not show that explanation is worthless. It identifies what an inference needs: relevant alternatives and a defensible basis for preferring one. A broad explanatory story gains credibility when its distinctive consequences survive checks that alternatives would not predict as readily.

Sources for this part: Douven 2025, § 1.1; Chakravartty 2025, § 3.2.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Understandable does not automatically mean likely

A hypothesis can be easy to grasp, familiar, or satisfying without being well supported. Even a genuinely simpler explanation needs an argument connecting that simplicity to evidential standing. Chakravartty separates the question of ranking explanatory virtues from the question of whether those virtues indicate truth rather than pragmatic convenience.1

This distinction helps with competing descriptions. A mathematically concise model need not posit fewer entities. A broad unifying account may be less precise on a particular measurement. Assessing explanatory goodness requires saying which virtue is at issue and why it bears on the claim under investigation.

Seek the next discriminator

In the sink example, each explanation suggests different checks. Condensation predicts a relation to temperature and humidity. A leak predicts particular changes in flow or pressure. A spill predicts a different temporal pattern. These invented predictions illustrate how explanatory reasoning can guide evidence collection instead of ending it.

The historical worry about unconceived alternatives asks whether serious candidates remain outside our menu. Bayes’ theorem distinguishes a high likelihood of the evidence under a hypothesis from a high probability of that hypothesis. Neither comparison is strengthened simply by telling a more vivid story.

The larger realism debate asks whether explanatory success warrants belief in unobservable mechanisms. That is a substantive dispute about what success supports, rather than a dispute over whether scientists ever use explanations.
