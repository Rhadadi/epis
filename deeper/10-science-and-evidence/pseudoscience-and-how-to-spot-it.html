Pseudoscience and how to spot it

In short

Scientific pretence can imitate journals, conferences, and technical language. To assess a claim, inspect how its proponents obtain evidence and respond to criticism. Warning signs include selective examples, avoidable refusal to test, and disregard of contrary results. A single sign is not always enough for a verdict. The evaluation needs relevant expertise and a comparison with the reliable knowledge of the subject.

Layer 1Re-learn

Appearance and performance

Imagine an invented organization promoting a cure. It has an elaborate conference, testimonials, and a technical-sounding explanation. These features show that it can present itself persuasively. They do not establish that the cure works.

Hansson discusses pseudoscience’s attempts to mimic the outward institutions of science. He also distinguishes pseudoscience from isolated errors and ordinary bad science: the promotion of a doctrine and the pretence of scientific standing are central to the category he analyzes.

The evaluation should therefore ask what evidence bears on the cure, how it was selected, and what could lead the organization to withdraw or revise the claim. Does the organization compare outcomes with relevant alternatives? Does it publish unfavorable results? Does it address informed objections or merely repeat favorable testimonials?

Testability is a beginning

A claim might make a clear prediction and still be promoted after the prediction fails. Hansson explains why pseudoscience cannot be identified solely with statements that are impossible to test. Some have been tested; their response to that evidence is part of the problem.

Likewise, a hypothesis initially lacking a feasible test is not automatically fraudulent. The appropriate verdict depends on what is claimed for it, what support exists, and whether the limits are communicated honestly. Not yet established is a more informative assessment than a premature label when the evidence is genuinely incomplete.

Sources for this part: Hansson 2025, § 3.2, 3.3; Hansson 2025, § 4.3.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Read warning signs together

Hansson’s review includes lists involving unquestioned authority, handpicked examples, unwillingness to test, neglect of refuting information, and devices that guarantee confirmation regardless of outcome. Multicriterial approaches recognize that inquiry can fail in different ways. They also face questions about relevance and weighting.1

In the imaginary cure case, testimonials alone might reflect selective reporting, spontaneous improvement, or an unrepresentative sample. They could also motivate a legitimate investigation. The decisive question is what proponents claim the testimonials establish and whether they permit an adequate investigation to challenge the claim.

Denial and promotion

Hansson distinguishes promoting a pseudoscientific theory from science denial. The latter may work by manufacturing the appearance of a live controversy around an established result. Some movements combine the two forms. This distinction helps evaluate a message aimed chiefly at discrediting evidence rather than supplying a tested alternative.2

But the distinction does not imply that criticism of a scientific claim is itself denial. Criticism can be essential to inquiry. Assess whether it represents the evidence accurately, engages the relevant expertise, and applies consistent standards to favored and disfavored claims.

The verdict requires knowledge

Hansson emphasizes that assessing conformity with the best available knowledge requires subject expertise. A checklist cannot let an uninformed reader settle every technical dispute alone. It can help identify which questions require an expert answer and which presentations have obscured them.3

See demarcation for the conceptual boundaries, ad hoc hypotheses for repairs, and scientific consensus for evaluating the process behind expert agreement. A label becomes useful when it summarizes reasons already made clear.
