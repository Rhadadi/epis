In short

An argument, in logic, is a set of claims some of which are offered as reasons for another. What makes something an argument is not its grammar but what the speaker is doing: putting reasons forward in support of a conclusion. That is why the same “because” sentence can be an argument in one conversation and an explanation in another. Telling them apart matters, because they are judged by different standards.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Structure is imposed by a reasoner

McKeon puts the definition this way: a collection of propositions is an argument “if and only if there is a reasoner R who puts forward the Pi as reasons in support of C”. The structure “is not a function of the syntactic and semantic features of the propositions that compose it”. It is “imposed on these propositions by the intentions of a reasoner”.

2. What a reasoner commits to

If you put forward premises in support of a conclusion, then plausibly the premises are your reasons and you think them justified; you believe they make the conclusion more probable than not; and you believe they are independent of the conclusion and relevant to it. Each condition rules out a familiar non-argument:

  • A conditional. “If they won’t compromise, the government will shut down” asserts neither part, so neither is offered as a reason.
  • An explanation. A doctor who says the uncle’s paresis is probably due to his old syphilis, while admitting that “few syphilis patients get paresis”, is not arguing that he has paresis.
  • A restatement. “Bill will be at the party, because Bill will be at the party” gives no independent reason.

3. Arguments and explanations have different aims

“Whereas the act of explaining is designed to increase the audience’s comprehension, the act of arguing is aimed at enhancing the acceptability of a standpoint”. An arguer thinks the audience does not yet accept the conclusion; an explainer assumes they already accept the thing explained. “The same line of reasoning can function as an explanation in one dialogical context and as an argument in another”.

Common confusions

  • Indicator words prove there is an argument. Words like “because”, “since”, “therefore” and “so” usually flag premises and conclusions, but “these expressions do not always function in these ways, and so their mere use does not necessitate the presence of an argument”.
  • An argument is just a premise list. The formal logician’s sense counts any list of statements with one marked as the conclusion, “regardless of whether the premises are offered as reasons”. The critical thinker’s sense is narrower.

Sources for this part: McKeon n.d., § 1; McKeon n.d., § 2; McKeon n.d., § Argument.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Arguments, implications and inferences. That frogs are mammals implies that they are not reptiles, but “it is problematic to offer the former as a reason for believing the latter”. An inference is a movement of mind; an argument is a collection of propositions1.
  • Exploratory arguments. Arguments need not aim at persuasion. They “can be constructed for purposes of inquiry”, to see what reasons might support a claim2. They can also aim at other attitudes: getting an audience to withhold assent, or to suspect that something is true3.
  • Arguing without words. Some informal logicians argue for a “multimodal” account that makes room for gestures, facial expressions, sounds and other non-verbal carriers of meaning4. Stories can argue too: in Plato’s Republic, Glaucon uses the story of the Ring of Gyges “to prove that humans are inherently selfish”5.

What the chapter simplifies

  • “Explanations are not arguments.” The chapter’s list treats this as settled. It is the standard view, but some philosophers “think that at least some explanations qualify as arguments”, and Thomas argued “that all explanations are arguments”6.
