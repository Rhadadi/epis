In short

The valid forms in the chapter (modus ponens, modus tollens, disjunctive syllogism and the rest) are not modern inventions. The Stoics listed most of them as the basic, unprovable forms of reasoning more than two thousand years ago. Their power is also their limit: a valid form tells you that premises and conclusion stand or fall together, but not which way to go. When a prediction fails, modus tollens says that something in your premises is false, not which premise.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. The Stoic indemonstrables

For the Stoics, arguments were built from whole propositions joined by connectives, which made their system “one of what we might now call propositional logic, by contrast with Aristotle’s logic of terms”. Their syllogisms are arguments in the form of one of five “indemonstrables”, axiomatic forms, or reducible to them by four rules. The five are: if p then q, p, so q (modus ponens); if p then q, not-q, so not-p (modus tollens); not both p and q, p, so not-q; either p or q, p, so not-q; either p or q, not-p, so q.

2. Validity and soundness, Stoic style

An argument is valid, in Chrysippus’s terms, “if the (Chrysippean) conditional formed from its premises as antecedent and its conclusion as consequent is true”. “An argument is sound (literally “true”) if it is valid and its premises are true”.

3. Modus tollens leaves a choice

A scientific prediction usually follows from a theory plus auxiliary assumptions. If the prediction fails, modus tollens refutes the conjunction. “But logic leaves us with a choice”: we can retain the core theory and give up an auxiliary hypothesis instead. Lakatos built his philosophy of science on this. A research programme’s “negative heuristic” forbids us “to direct the modus tollens at this “hard core”” and tells us to redirect it at a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses.

Common confusions

  • Exclusive “or” is a mistake. The fourth indemonstrable (p or q, p, so not-q) is the chapter’s “affirming a disjunct”. It was valid for the Stoics because they treated disjunction as exclusive. Whether the form is valid depends on which “or” is meant.

Sources for this part: Durand et al. 2023, § 3.4; Musgrave and Pigden 2026, § 2.2; Durand et al. 2023, § 3.3.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Forms recognised in India. Besides the canonical syllogism, classical Indian logicians accepted reductio ad absurdum (prasaṅga), suppositional reasoning (tarka), and a form of reasoning akin to disjunctive syllogism (arthāpatti). They also counted infinite regress and various forms of circularity as bad arguments1.
  • When modus tollens fails for defaults. In some formal systems of defeasible reasoning, “from the fact that birds normally fly and Tweety does not fly, we are not licensed to infer that Tweety is not a bird”2. Modus tollens is valid for strict conditionals, not for “normally” generalizations. Compare Strength and cogency.
  • Lakatos’s planets. When an orbit is not quite the ellipse that Newtonian mechanics predicts, the Newtonian programme “bids us look for another heavenly body whose gravitational force might be distorting the first planet’s orbit” rather than give up Newton3. That is “one person’s modus ponens is another person’s modus tollens” at the scale of a whole science.
