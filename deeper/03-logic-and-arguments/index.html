Logic and the Anatomy of Arguments

For when a section didn't click, or left you wanting more: each page explains the idea again, tells the full story, fills in what the chapter leaves out, and gives the sources.

Rembrandt van Rijn, The Anatomy Lesson of Dr Nicolaes Tulp, 1632

← Back to Chapter 3

  • Short study · 3 minWhat an argument is and is notAn argument, in logic, is a set of claims some of which are offered as reasons for another. What makes something an argument is not its grammar but what the speaker is doing: putting reasons forward in support of a conclusion.
  • Short study · 2 minStandard formStandard form lists an argument’s premises, draws a line, and puts the conclusion underneath. It forces two decisions that ordinary prose lets you dodge: exactly what is being claimed, and how the claims fit together.
  • Full study · 10 minDeduction, induction, and abductionDeduction draws out what premises guarantee; induction generalizes from what has been observed; abduction infers the hypothesis that would best explain the evidence. Peirce showed how the three fit together with a bag of beans: each is the same syllogism with its parts rearranged.
  • Full study · 11 minValidity and soundnessA valid argument is one where the premises cannot all be true while the conclusion is false; a sound argument is a valid one whose premises are true. That much is in every textbook.
  • Short study · 3 minStrength and cogencyAn inductive argument is strong when its premises, if true, would make its conclusion probable, and cogent when it is strong and its premises are in fact acceptable. Strength comes in degrees and depends on things outside the argument: how the sample was drawn, how varied the evidence is, and what we already know about the kind of thing in question.
  • Short study · 3 minPropositional logicPropositional logic studies how the truth of compound sentences depends on the truth of their parts, joined by “not”, “and”, “or” and “if”. Its strength is that whether a conclusion follows should not depend on what the words “man” or “mortal” mean, only on the connectives.
  • Short study · 3 minValid argument formsThe valid forms in the chapter (modus ponens, modus tollens, disjunctive syllogism and the rest) are not modern inventions. The Stoics listed most of them as the basic, unprovable forms of reasoning more than two thousand years ago.
  • Short study · 3 minFormal fallaciesA formal fallacy is an argument whose form is invalid: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, the undistributed middle. Each looks like a valid form, which is what makes it tempting.
  • Short study · 3 minCategorical logic and syllogismsAristotle’s syllogistic was the first formal logic, and for two thousand years it was simply “logic”. It deals with four kinds of sentence (“Every A is B”, “No A is B”, “Some A is B”, “Not every A is B”) and asks which pairs of them force a conclusion.
  • Short study · 3 minPredicate logic and quantifiersPredicate logic looks inside sentences. It splits them into names, predicates and the quantifiers “every” and “some”, and so it can show why “everyone loves someone” does not imply “someone is loved by everyone”.
  • Short study · 3 minModal logic basicsModal logic is the logic of “necessarily” and “possibly”, and, by extension, of “ought”, “will”, “believes” and “knows”. It needs something truth tables cannot give: whether “necessarily A” is true does not depend only on whether A is true.
  • Short study · 3 minParadoxes as stress testsA paradox is a small set of claims, each of which seems true, that cannot all be true together. That makes it a test: something we confidently believe, about truth, knowledge, rational belief or logic itself, has to go.
  • Short study · 3 minReconstructing real argumentsReconstruction turns messy prose into premises and a conclusion you can evaluate. It requires interpretation at every step: deciding what the conclusion is, which unstated premises the arguer relies on, and how charitable to be.
  • Short study · 3 minThe Toulmin modelToulmin’s six-part model describes an argument as a step from data to a claim, licensed by a warrant. The warrant is a general rule that usually holds but has exceptions, so the claim comes with a qualifier (“presumably”) and a list of rebuttals (“unless...”).
  • Short study · 3 minDialectical movesArguments happen between people, and some of the most useful moves are about the exchange rather than the logic of a single argument: the counterexample, the parody that runs an opponent’s reasoning on a different subject, the argument from an opponent’s own concessions. Each has a precise structure and precise limits.
