In short

Philosophers writing in Arabic and Persian inherited Greek philosophy and argued about it for centuries. For most of them the model of knowledge was demonstration: a proof from necessary first principles. Al-Ghazālī asked what guarantees the first principles themselves, and what entitles us to call any link between cause and effect necessary. Ibn Rushd answered him, and Suhrawardī proposed a different kind of knowledge altogether, knowledge by presence. These debates are the background to the chapter’s four portraits, and some of them reached Latin Europe and shaped its philosophy.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. Two ways of reasoning about God and the world

Two intellectual traditions met in the Islamic world. Falsafa, philosophy, was understood as “an intellectual practice developed in Ancient Greece, which emphasizes the use of rational understanding and demonstration in the pursuit of knowledge (ʿilm)”. Kalām, theology, had its own commitments. Its Ashʿarite school, to which al-Ghazālī belonged, appealed to divine omnipotence and seems broadly “to endorse occasionalism, the view that God is the only true cause”.

2. Demonstration was the gold standard

Philosophers sorted arguments by the quality of their premises. Ibn Rushd, following his predecessors, distinguished five types: demonstrative, dialectical, rhetorical, poetical and fallacious. Only demonstration starts from necessary premises, and only it “leads to certainty in philosophy”. Even al-Ghazālī, the great critic of the philosophers, agreed that “A [valid] rational demonstration is never wrong”.

3. But what makes the first principles certain?

A proof is only as good as its starting points. Al-Ghazālī’s crisis, told in his autobiography, turned on exactly this. Once he doubted the first principles, no proof could help him, because “a proof can only be constructed by combining the first [principles of] knowledge”.

4. What do we see when fire burns cotton?

We see fire touching cotton, and then the cotton burning. Al-Ghazālī argued that observation “points towards a concomitant occurrence ... but not to a combined occurrence”: it shows that the burning happens with the contact, not that it happens through it. Whether there is a necessary connection is therefore not something the eye can settle.

5. Some knowledge needs no representation

Peripatetic philosophers held that we know a thing through a form or representation of it in the mind. Suhrawardī criticised and rejected this “representational” epistemology. When you are in pain, you do not form a picture of the pain and then inspect it; the pain is simply present to you. Pain is his paradigm of “a non-discursive, non-conceptual and non-propositional type of knowledge”.

Common confusions

  • Al-Ghazālī rejected philosophy. He condemned a short list of philosophical theses, but he held that each teaching “must be judged by itself, and if found sound and in accordance with revelation, should be adopted”. The result was “a widespread application of Aristotelian teachings” in his own theology and ethics.
  • Al-Ghazālī denied that there are causes. His target was necessity, not causation as such. “The dispute between al-Ghazālī and Avicenna is not about causality as such, rather about the necessary nature of God’s creation”. Secondary causes were “a viable option for al-Ghazālī that he is willing to accept”.
  • The floating man is just the cogito, six centuries early. The resemblance is real, but the aims differ. Ibn Sīnā’s thought experiment was meant to show that the soul is “an independent intelligent substance”, able to know its own existence without the senses. Its target is the nature of the soul, not the refutation of doubt.

Layer 2The full story

How the tradition developed

Timeline

The main figures

  • c. 800–870 · Al-Kindī, “the first self-identified philosopher in the Arabic tradition”, works amid the translations of Greek texts sponsored by the ʿAbbasid caliphs1.
  • c. 870–950 · Al-Fārābī distinguishes demonstrative from dialectical and other kinds of discourse2.
  • 965–1039 · Ibn al-Haytham’s Book of Optics treats many perceptions as rapid, unnoticed inferences3.
  • c. 970–1037 · Ibn Sīnā makes hitting upon the middle term “the cornerstone of his epistemology”4.
  • c. 1058–1111 · Al-Ghazālī’s Incoherence of the Philosophers and his autobiography, Deliverance from Error5.
  • 1126–1198 · Ibn Rushd answers al-Ghazālī in The Incoherence of the Incoherence and defends philosophy in The Decisive Treatise6.
  • 1154–1191 · Suhrawardī founds the Illuminationist school and its theory of knowledge by presence7.
  • Late 12th century · In Toledo, the translation of philosophical texts by al-Kindī, al-Fārābī, al-Ghazālī and Ibn Sīnā into Latin “assumed full pace”8.
  • c. 1571–1636 · Mullā Ṣadrā, “arguably the most significant Islamic philosopher after Avicenna”9.

The starting point was a translation movement. Greek philosophy and science were translated into Arabic, and al-Kindī’s treatises “depended heavily on these translations”10. He defended the borrowing fiercely against contemporaries who objected to Greek ideas:

Read the original

Al-Kindī, On First Philosophy11

We must not be ashamed to admire the truth or to acquire it, from wherever it comes. Even if it should come from far-flung nations and foreign peoples, there is for the student of truth nothing more important than the truth, nor is the truth demeaned or diminished by the one who states or conveys it.

The passage is an epistemic principle as well as a polemic: the source of a claim does not settle its truth. It is the positive side of what the guide calls the genetic fallacy. Al-Kindī was even willing to “recognize shortcomings on the part of Greek scientific thinkers”, for instance in Euclid’s optics12.

Ibn Sīnā: knowledge as hitting the middle term

Ibn Sīnā’s work, in Gutas’s words, “represents the culmination of the Hellenic tradition, defunct in Greek after the sixth century, reborn in Arabic in the 9th”13. His theory of knowledge starts from a technical point in Aristotle’s logic.

To prove that A is C, you need a middle term B: A is B, B is C, so A is C. Finding B is the hard part. Aristotle had mentioned a talent for “hitting upon” the middle term. The Arabic translation rendered it as ḥads, “guessing correctly, hitting correctly upon the answer”, and Ibn Sīnā “made it the cornerstone of his epistemology”14. This explained two things at once: why all certain intellectual knowledge has a syllogistic core, and why people differ in their ability to reach it, since they have a varying talent for it15.

The theory has a striking consequence for history. Knowledge in itself is a closed system, but “on a human level and in history it is evolutionary”. “Each philosopher, through his own syllogistic reasoning and ability to hit correctly upon the middle terms, modifies and completes the work of his predecessors.” Ibn Sīnā “never claimed to have exhausted it all”; in his later works he “bemoaned the limitations of human knowledge” and urged readers to keep improving philosophy16.

It is also, unexpectedly, an empiricism. The human intellect cannot grasp the intelligibles through their causes while it is tied to a body. It must work back from their effects, so Ibn Sīnā held that “the senses are the means by which the human soul acquires different kinds of knowledge”. Even our predisposition for first principles “is itself actualized by the experience of particulars”. The intellect “functions as a processor of the information provided by the external and internal senses”17. Gutas calls the result Ibn Sīnā’s “rationalist empiricism”18.

The floating man

The famous thought experiment imagines “a person suspended in air in total isolation from any physical or sensory experience”. Such a person would still be aware of his own existence. Its purpose is “to show the ability of a person to deduce the soul’s existence intellectually, without assistance from the sensory or imaginative, material faculties of the soul”19. For Ibn Sīnā this proves that the soul is an independent substance, which survives the body20.

The argument travelled. In Latin Europe the “Flying Man” was influential, along with Ibn Sīnā’s definition of the soul as a separate substance21. It also had critics at home. Suhrawardī opened his own epistemology by examining the issues the thought experiment raises, and went on to reject Ibn Sīnā’s “representational” theory of knowledge22. A natural worry is this: imagining yourself aware of your existence without being aware of your body shows, at most, that you can think of the self without thinking of the body. Whether it follows that the self is something other than the body is a further question.

Al-Ghazālī’s crisis

Al-Ghazālī’s Deliverance from Error describes a period of radical doubt. He wanted knowledge “in which what is known is laid bare in such a way as to leave no room for doubt, and is unaccompanied by the possibility of error or illusion”23. Bolyard lists his grounds for doubt: the disagreement of competing theories, sensory illusions such as a shadow that seems to stand still and the apparently small size of the stars, then doubts about reason itself, and finally the dream argument24. Here is the turn from the senses to reason, in Claud Field’s English translation of 1909:

Read the original

Al-Ghazālī, Deliverance from Error (tr. Claud Field, 1909)25

Then I reflected in myself: “Since I cannot trust to the evidence of my senses, I must rely only on intellectual notions based on fundamental principles, such as the following axioms: ‘Ten is more than three. Affirmation and negation cannot coexist together.’” To this the notions I derived from my senses made the following objections: “Who can guarantee you that you can trust to the evidence of reason more than to that of the senses? You believed in our testimony till it was contradicted by the verdict of reason, otherwise you would have continued to believe it to this day. Well, perhaps, there is above reason another judge who, if he appeared, would convict reason of falsehood, just as reason has confuted us. And if such a third arbiter is not yet apparent, it does not follow that he does not exist.”

The senses speak for themselves here, and their point is a sharp one. Before reason corrected them, nothing in their verdicts felt doubtful. So the fact that nothing in reason’s verdicts feels doubtful proves nothing either.

The argument, step by step

The argument from the third judge

  1. My confidence in the senses felt complete until a higher judge, reason, overturned it.
  2. So complete confidence in a faculty is no guarantee that a higher judge will not overturn it.
  3. My confidence in reason is complete, but nothing in that confidence rules out a judge above reason.
  4. Any proof that reason is reliable would have to be built from reason’s own first principles, which are what is in doubt.
  5. So the reliability of reason cannot be established by proof.

Premise 4 is the trap, and al-Ghazālī saw it: “In order to disentangle the knot of this difficulty, a proof was necessary. Now a proof must be based on primary assumptions, and it was precisely these of which I was in doubt”26. This is the same structure as the problem of the criterion and the circularity horn of Agrippa’s trilemma.

He did not argue his way out. The doubt “lasted for almost two months, during which I embraced the [skeptical] creed in actual fact, though not in speech or expression”27. Then the first principles were “once again accepted and trusted”, not “by composing a proof or by an arrangement of words, but rather by a light that God almighty cast into my breast”28. He drew a moral: “Whoever supposes that enlightenment depends upon explicit proofs has narrowed the expanse of God’s mercy”29.

Bolyard finds al-Ghazālī “surprisingly Cartesian” here30. Not everyone agrees how far to press the parallel. Kukkonen situates the discussion within a broader Islamic conversation about skepticism and “downplays the supposed connections between Ghazali and the Early Moderns”31. There is a clear difference in the ending. Descartes tries to rebuild certainty by argument; al-Ghazālī’s certainty returns without one, and he treats that as a lesson about the limits of proof.

Causation: the seventeenth discussion

The Incoherence of the Philosophers attacks twenty philosophical positions. Its seventeenth discussion opens with what Griffel calls “a masterwork of philosophical literature”:

The connection (iqtirān) between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not necessary (ḍarūrī), according to us.32

Any acceptable account of physical processes, he says, must meet four conditions: the connection between cause and effect is not necessary; the effect can exist without that cause; God creates the two events “concomitant, side by side”; and God’s creation follows a prior decision33. His example is fire and cotton. In the Incoherence he denies that the fire alone causes the combustion; the efficient cause is God, “either through the mediation of the angels or without mediation”34.

The argument, step by step

The argument from observation35

  1. All we can learn about a causal connection from observation is what we observe.
  2. Observation shows that the burning occurs together with the contact of fire and cotton, not that it occurs through the contact.
  3. So observation cannot prove that the fire causes the burning, still less that it must.

Griffel adds that, for al-Ghazālī, such connections “cannot be proven through observation (or through any other means)”, but “they may or may not exist”36. The argument is about what we can know, not a proof that there are no causes.

What “not necessary” means

Griffel shows that the dispute with Ibn Sīnā rests on two ideas of necessity. Ibn Sīnā followed Aristotle’s “statistical” model: a sentence like “Fire causes cotton to combust” is necessary if it “was, is, and will always be true”37. Ashʿarite theology instead understood modality in terms of “synchronic alternative states of affairs”, close to the modern idea of possible worlds38. Al-Juwaynī’s example is a building: anyone who sees one knows at once that its height, length and form “are actualized possibilities and could be different”39.

On this model, a causal connection that never fails can still fail to be necessary. “We can imagine a world where fire does not cause cotton to combust”40. Al-Ghazālī, like Ibn Sīnā, “assumes causal connections never were and never will be different from what they are now. Still they are not necessary”41. God simply did not choose to create the alternative world42.

Two approaches, and Ibn Rushd’s reply

The seventeenth discussion then offers two ways to explain the miracles that Ibn Sīnā’s necessitarianism seemed to rule out43. In the first, God creates every event directly, following a custom (ʿāda); “combustion occurs only concomitantly when fire touches cotton and is not connected to this event”, and a miracle is God suspending his habit. In the second, al-Ghazālī “accepts the existence of “natures” (ṭabāʾiʿ) and of unchanging connections between causes and their effects”, and argues only that we are far from knowing all causes, so a miracle may have a natural cause unknown to us44.

The positions

Four views of causal connection45

View Who Core claim Main objection
Occasionalism Al-Ashʿarī and his school God is the only true cause; there are no causal “natures” in things Ibn Rushd: without natures, nothing would have a definition
Necessitarian secondary causes Ibn Sīnā Causes act with necessity, from the natures of things and ultimately from God’s essence Al-Ghazālī: it makes some of the Qur’an’s miracles impossible
Contingent connections Al-Ghazālī Connections are reliable but not necessary; either occasionalism or secondary causes may be true He leaves open which of his two pictures is true
Natures and essences Ibn Rushd Things act from their natures; deny this and nothing has a definition Assumes what al-Ghazālī denied: that natures necessitate

Ibn Rushd “heaps scorn” on occasionalism. His case rests on the link between a thing’s causal role and its nature: “If a thing did not have its specific nature, it would not have a special name nor a definition, and all things would be one”46.

Reason and revelation

A demonstration can seem to conflict with scripture. Al-Ghazālī’s rule was to keep the demonstration and reinterpret the text: “If demonstration proves something that violates the literal meaning of revelation, the scholar must apply “allegorical interpretation” (taʾwīl)”. Where no valid demonstration disproves the literal meaning, interpretation is not allowed47. This rule “was widely accepted by almost all later Muslim theologians”. Ibn Taymiyya objected that it gives reason priority over revelation and denied that the two can really conflict48.

Ibn Rushd “accepted al-Ghazālī’s rule ... but he did not agree with his findings on what can and cannot be demonstrated”49. In The Decisive Treatise he argued that philosophy and religion cannot contradict each other: “For truth does not oppose truth, but rather agrees with and bears witness to it”50. The treatise’s implicit target was al-Ghazālī’s fatwa against metaphysics; to forbid demonstration to those capable of it is, for Ibn Rushd, “an injustice both to God and to these scholars”51. Other paths, rhetorical and dialectical, reach the same truth for other audiences52.

Knowledge by presence

Suhrawardī built his epistemology on two concepts, “self-awareness” and “knowledge by presence” (ʿilm ḥuḍūrī)53. He argued for “a type of knowledge that is self-evident, innate and unmediated through any type of abstraction or representation of forms”54. Self-awareness, like pain, is its model, and he placed it at “a pre-cognitive level of self-consciousness or self-awareness, logically prior to any distinction between subject and object”55.

This had consequences for definition. Suhrawardī argued that “it is impossible for a human being to construct an essential definition in the way the Peripatetics require”56. On his view, “only direct experience guarantees acquisition of true knowledge”57. Mystical intuition (dhawq) then becomes, for some of his readers, “an alternative — albeit more reliable — foundation of certainty”58. The full story of knowledge by presence, including Mullā Ṣadrā’s development of it, belongs to Knowledge by presence in Chapter 7.

How the ideas reached Europe

The Arabic–Latin translations “led to the transformation of almost all philosophical disciplines in the medieval Latin world”59. Ibn Rushd’s commentaries on Aristotle became “the principal secondary literature of Latin university culture” after Paris made Aristotle compulsory reading in 125560. Ibn al-Haytham’s Optics, read in Latin as Perspectiva or De aspectibus, shaped later medieval thinking about perception and illusion61. And Ibn Rushd’s reply to al-Ghazālī was translated into Latin twice, in 1328 and 1526, making al-Ghazālī’s criticism of Aristotelianism “known among the Averroists of the Renaissance”62.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Perception as inference. Ibn al-Haytham held that “many perceptions are inferential”: we grasp things through “sudden, “imperceptible” inferences” so fast that we do not notice them. He even treated “the whole is greater than its [proper] part” as inferential63. He also catalogued illusions, such as the moon looking larger near the horizon64. Compare Perception in Chapter 7.
  • Equivalence of proofs. The Ismāʿīlīs argued that “all rational proofs are mutually equivalent (takāfu’ al-adilla)” and that only the divinely guided Imam conveys certainty. Al-Ghazālī’s answer was demonstration: theologians disagree because they are “largely unfamiliar with the technique of demonstration”65. The Ismāʿīlī claim echoes the Pyrrhonists’ equal force of opposing arguments (see Pyrrhonian skepticism).
  • Later Persian discussions. Rāzī (1149–1210) and Ṭūsī (1201–1274) were not skeptics, but their concern with global skepticism and first principles led to “extended discussions of skeptical arguments”66.

What the chapter simplifies

  • “This anticipates Hume.” Bolyard calls al-Ghazālī’s position “Humean-leaning”67, and the argument from observation does resemble Hume’s. But the surrounding view is different. Al-Ghazālī denied the necessity of causal connections, not their reality, and he assumed they never change68. Hume, by contrast, traced our very idea of necessary connection to habit (see Hume’s problem of induction).
  • Dates. Gutas dates Ibn Sīnā “ca. 970–1037”69, and Rizvi dates Mullā Ṣadrā “ca. 1571–1636”70; the chapter gives 980 and c. 1640.
  • “Ibn Rushd replied.” He did reply, but he also agreed with al-Ghazālī about the rule for reconciling reason with revelation; what he disputed was what can be demonstrated71.
