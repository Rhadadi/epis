In short

Greek philosophy did not just ask early versions of today’s questions; it set the form they still take. Socrates tested claims to knowledge by cross-examination. Plato separated knowledge from true belief and failed, on purpose or not, to define it. Aristotle met the regress of justification with self-evident first principles. The Hellenistic schools then argued over whether anything can be known at all. This page explains the key arguments in their own terms and the main debates about how to read them.

Layer 1Re-learn

Why this section is hard

The Greek words do not map neatly onto ours. Epistēmē is usually translated “knowledge”, but it was understanding of a systematic kind, closely tied to craft (technē): “There is an intimate positive relationship between epistêmê and technê, as well as a fundamental contrast”. Many texts also survive only in fragments or in reports by hostile later writers. Read the claims below as reconstructions.

Step by step

1. Socrates tests claims to know

When the oracle said no one was wiser than Socrates, he questioned people with a reputation for wisdom and concluded that he had an edge only in not mistaking ignorance for knowledge: the other man “knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know”.

2. Plato separates knowledge from true belief

In the Meno, true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus, which “will play truant and run away” unless fastened: “they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause”. In the Theaetetus, Socrates and Theaetetus test three definitions: knowledge is perception, true belief, or true belief with an account. All three fail.

3. Aristotle stops the regress

If all scientific knowledge must be demonstrated from known premises, the demonstrations either go on forever or stop at premises that are not known. Aristotle accepts the dilemma but denies that all knowledge is demonstrative: the first premises are known by “another cognitive state, nous (translated variously as ‘insight’, ‘intuition’, ‘intelligence’)”.

4. The Hellenistic schools argue about whether knowledge is possible

The Stoics said knowledge rests on assent to “cognitive impressions”, which cannot be false. The Academic skeptic Arcesilaus replied that every true impression has a false twin that cannot be told apart from it, “So, there are no cognitive impressions”, and so nothing can be known. Pyrrhonists, the most radical school, suspended judgment on everything.

Common confusions

  • Socrates claimed to know nothing. What Plato’s Apology has him claim is narrower: unlike others, he does not think he knows what he does not know.
  • The Theaetetus defends “justified true belief”. It rejects all three proposals, and it is not even clear Plato was trying to offer a definition: he may be “undermining unacceptable definitions”.
  • Pyrrhonists said nothing can be known. That would itself be a doctrine. Sextus contrasts the Pyrrhonists, who “are still investigating”, with other, dogmatic skeptics.
  • Protagoras was simply a relativist. His “man is the measure” thesis “has been interpreted as a first stance in favour of relativism”, but its relation to his teaching is unclear.

Layer 2The full story

How the ideas developed

Timeline

From the Presocratics to Sextus

  • 5th century BCE · Protagoras: “man is the measure of all things”1.
  • 399 BCE · Trial and death of Socrates (469–399 BCE); Plato’s Apology2.
  • 4th century BCE · Plato’s Meno and Theaetetus3.
  • 4th century BCE · Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics: demonstration and first principles4.
  • 3rd century BCE · Zeno’s Stoics and Arcesilaus’ Academy debate the “cognitive impression”5.
  • 2nd century BCE · Carneades (214–129/8 BCE) heads the Academy and defends a form of probabilism6.
  • 2nd–3rd century CE · Sextus Empiricus, whose Outlines of Pyrrhonism is “the best and fullest account we have of Pyrrhonian skepticism”7.

The story runs from confidence to crisis. Plato thought knowledge possible but hard to define. Aristotle built a theory of scientific knowledge with self-evident foundations. The Stoics then claimed a perceptual foundation, the cognitive impression, and the skeptics attacked it. Their attack is the ancestor of every later debate about foundations: the modes of Agrippa press anyone who makes a claim to choose between an unsupported assumption, a circle and an infinite regress8.

Read the original

Read the original

Plato, Theaetetus 201a–c: the jurymen

“When, therefore, judges are justly persuaded about matters which you can know only by seeing them, and not in any other way, and when thus judging of them from report they attain a true opinion about them, they judge without knowledge, and yet are rightly persuaded, if they have judged well.”9

Orators and lawyers, Socrates says, “persuade men by their art and make them think whatever they like, but they do not teach them”10. So true opinion is not knowledge: “knowledge and true belief are different states”11.

The argument raises puzzles of its own. “The jury argument seems to be a counter-example not only to D2 but also to D3” (that is, to the third definition as well as the second), since the jurors might have an account; and Plato “seems to offer two incompatible explanations of why the jury don’t know: first that they have only a limited time to hear the arguments (201b3, 172e1); and second that their judgement is second-hand (201b9)”12. The second explanation makes the passage relevant to modern debates about testimony.

Read the original

Plato, Meno 97d–98a (Jowett's translation)

“Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding.”13

Two claims are packed in here. Knowledge differs from true opinion by being anchored, by an account of why it is true; and the anchoring makes it stable. The second claim is the ancestor of a modern answer to the question why knowledge is more valuable than true belief. Notice also that Plato identifies the anchoring with “recollection”, his theory that learning is remembering.

The arguments, step by step

The argument, step by step

The agnostics' regress (reported by Aristotle)14

  1. Whatever is scientifically known must be demonstrated.
  2. A demonstration proceeds from premises that must themselves be scientifically known.
  3. Either this goes on forever, or it stops at some point.
  4. If it goes on forever, there are no first premises, and nothing is demonstrated.
  5. If it stops, the first premises are undemonstrated, so not scientifically known, and neither is anything derived from them.
  6. So nothing is scientifically known.

A second group, the “circular demonstrators”, tried to escape by letting principles be deduced from each other. Aristotle rejected circular demonstration because premises must be prior to the conclusion, and a circle would make “every premise prior and posterior to itself”. His own answer denies premise 1 for first principles: “there is another form of knowledge possible for the first premises, and this provides the starting points for demonstrations”15. That is the first version of foundationalism.

The argument, step by step

Arcesilaus against the Stoic cognitive impression16

  1. Knowledge, for Zeno, depends on assent to cognitive impressions: impressions that come from what exists, match it and could not come from what does not exist.
  2. For every true impression there could be a false one indistinguishable from it.
  3. So there are no cognitive impressions.
  4. So nothing can be known.
  5. And since the Stoics hold that it is irrational to assent to what is not cognitive, it is irrational to assent to anything.

The argument turns the Stoics’ own principles against them, and it works only on those principles: “the conclusion doesn’t follow unless it is true that there are impressions, that some are true, that there are no other routes to cognition, that the Stoic definition of cognition gives necessary and sufficient conditions for knowledge”17. Whether Arcesilaus himself held any of this is doubtful.

The positions

The positions

Can anything be known? The Hellenistic answers18

View Who Core claim The main objection
Stoic Zeno and his successors Knowledge rests on cognitive impressions, which cannot be false Indistinguishable false impressions (Arcesilaus)
Academic Arcesilaus, Carneades Nothing can be known; withhold assent Without assent, action seems impossible (the Stoic “inaction” objection)
Pyrrhonist Pyrrho, Sextus Suspend judgment on every question, and keep investigating Can suspension really bring tranquillity? (Burnyeat)

Where the debate stands

  • What the Theaetetus is about. “One crucial question about Theaetetus 201–210 is the question whether the argument is concerned with objectual or propositional knowledge. This is a basic and central division among interpretations of the whole” dialogue19: acquaintance with things, or knowledge that something is so.
  • Can the skeptic be tranquil? Myles Burnyeat “denies that the Skeptic will achieve tranquillity” unless he believes that no answers are forthcoming, which would make him a global, dogmatic skeptic after all20.
  • Aristotle’s nous. His account of how first principles become known is in Posterior Analytics II.19, “long regarded as a difficult text to interpret”21.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • The circular alternative. Aristotle’s opponents included “circular demonstrators” who claimed a regress “both unending ... and finite (because it works its way round the finite circle of premises)”; “some modern interpreters have compared this position to a coherence theory of knowledge”22. See Coherentism.
  • Living without assent. The Stoics objected that skeptics could not act, since “action is impossible without assent”. Arcesilaus answered with a “practical criterion”, a theory “that makes action without assent possible”23; Carneades later defended a form of probabilism24.
  • Knowledge as craft. Plato’s Republic makes knowledge of the Forms “the indispensable basis for the philosophers’ craft of ruling in the city”, and the Stoics later treated virtue as a craft of life “based on an understanding of the universe”25.
  • Protagoras the teacher. His teaching “had a practical and concrete goal” and was “mainly devoted to the development of argumentative techniques”26, which helps explain why Plato set Socrates against him.

What the chapter simplifies

  • Translations differ. The chapter quotes the Meno’s “working out the reason” and the Apology’s “I do not think I know what I do not know”. Jowett’s older translations, quoted above, say “the tie of the cause” and “I neither know nor think that I know”27.
  • The Agrippan modes. Of the five modes, three matter most: hypothesis, circularity and regression to infinity28. The chapter’s list of five is right, but the trilemma does the philosophical work. See Agrippa’s trilemma.
