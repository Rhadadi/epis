In short

Twentieth-century epistemology was shaped by the new logic and by science. The logical positivists tried to draw the line between sense and nonsense with a test of verification, and the test kept failing. Popper drew a different line, between science and non-science, with falsifiability. Moore and Wittgenstein asked what doubt itself presupposes, and Quine proposed handing epistemology over to psychology. Then Gettier’s three pages turned the analysis of knowledge into an industry. Many of the chapter’s later topics begin here.

Layer 1Re-learn

Step by step

1. The positivists’ test of meaning

On the strict verificationist view, “in order to be meaningful a claim must be implied by a finite number of observation sentences”. That had a drawback: “so-called laws of nature would not be meaningful on this criterion”. Schlick bit the bullet and held that laws are not statements at all but principles of inference. Others, the “left” wing of the Vienna Circle (Carnap, Frank, Hahn and Neurath), looked for more liberal formulations.

2. Popper’s line between science and non-science

Popper built on an asymmetry: it is “logically impossible to verify a universal proposition by reference to experience”, but a single counter-instance can refute one. So he made falsifiability the mark of science. Evidence in favour of a theory is easy to find, he argued, so it should count only if it is “the positive result of a genuinely “risky” prediction, which might conceivably have been false”.

3. Moore’s two hands

In “Proof of an External World” (1939) Moore held up his hands: “Here is one hand”, and “here is another”. “How absurd it would be to suggest that I did not know it, but only believed it”.

4. Wittgenstein’s hinges

Wittgenstein’s last notes, On Certainty, are “generally accepted as a work of epistemology”. They tackle skeptical doubts and foundational answers together and, in typical Wittgensteinian fashion, as “a work of therapy which discounts presuppositions common to both”. Instead of answering the skeptic directly, they point to “hinge propositions”: “propositions about which doubt cannot be entertained”.

5. Quine’s naturalism

“Epistemology”, Quine says, “…is contained in natural science, as a chapter of psychology”.

Common confusions

  • Positivism fell to one objection, self-refutation. It also failed on its own terms. “Over the years a great many different formulations of verificationist principles ensued. Most of them came to a bad end rather quickly.” Ayer’s famous formulation in Language, Truth and Logic is “fatally flawed”: “It would declare any sentence whatsoever as meaningful”, since any sentence A, conjoined with the conditional A ⊃ O (if A, then O), implies an observation sentence O. Creath adds a caution: a string of failures may show only that the embedded accounts of confirmation were too simple, not that the link between meaning and confirmation is unsound.
  • Moore was trying to refute skepticism. Baldwin calls this common reading incorrect. “Moore’s avowed aim is to prove the existence of an external world, not to prove his knowledge of the existence of an external world”. Moore himself said he had never implied that “Nobody knows for certain that there are any material things” could be proved false “in any such simple way”.
  • Naturalized epistemology gives up on norms. Kim objected that it does, and that this is a serious defect, but Quine replied that critics “are wrong in protesting that the normative element…goes by the board”. It still tells us to rely on “the evidence of the senses rather than on soothsayers” and to aim at simple theories.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • Gettier cases before Gettier. The fourteenth-century philosopher Peter of Mantua described someone who sees Plato running, takes him for Socrates, and so believes that Socrates is running, while Socrates happens to be running in Rome1. Russell’s stopped clock gives a true belief about the time without knowledge. Such cases “have been discussed throughout the history of Eastern and Western epistemology”, but they came into “far more theoretical salience” after Gettier’s 1963 paper2.
  • Why logic mattered to the positivists. If mathematics and logic were empty of content, as Wittgenstein’s “no-content theory of logic” suggested, then “a thoroughgoing empiricism” was possible without Mill’s view that mathematics is inductive3. Compare the nineteenth century.
  • What hinges are. Whether hinge propositions are beyond doubt “due to their being epistemically foundational, naturally certain, or logically unavailable to doubt is still a matter for philosophical explanation”, and so is the question of which skeptic On Certainty is aimed at4.

What the chapter simplifies

  • Popper and psychoanalysis. Popper’s criterion did not simply sort theories into science and nonsense. On his view “psychoanalysis is a pre-science and astrology and phrenology are pseudo-sciences”5. What struck him about Freud and Adler was that their theories were “amenable only to confirmation”, while Einstein’s had testable implications that, if false, would have falsified it6.
  • Kuhn and irrationality. Kuhn’s claim that scientists do not follow rules in choosing theories “appeared tantamount to the claim that science is irrational”7. But Kuhn named five shared criteria for theory choice, starting with “accuracy” and “consistency”8, and Bird argues that a process that cannot be reduced to rules is not thereby irrational9. See Kuhn and paradigms.
