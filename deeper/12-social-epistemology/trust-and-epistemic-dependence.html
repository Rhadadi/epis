Trust and epistemic dependence

In short

Depending on another person can be a rational way to learn. Trust adds an interpersonal dimension to reliance: another person’s competence and willingness matter, and failure can be experienced as betrayal. Trustworthiness belongs to the source, while trust describes your attitude toward that source. You can therefore trust mistakenly or distrust someone who deserves your confidence. Responsible inquiry examines both risks without demanding personal verification of everything.

Layer 1Re-learn

Reliability and trust are related

Here is an invented example. A neighbor agrees to check your houseplants while you are away and later reports that one needs repotting. You rely on their ability to recognize the problem and their willingness to tell you accurately. A moisture sensor supplies a different kind of information. If it malfunctions, you can be disappointed, but an ordinary sensor does not betray your confidence in the way a person might.

The distinction explains why trust is more than a prediction that a report will be correct. You can have expectations about a machine’s performance, a person’s promises, and an institution’s procedure. Those expectations may overlap in practice while involving different relations of responsibility.

Trust also has an object and a task. Your neighbor may reliably recognize a thirsty plant but lack expertise about a rare infection. You can accept one report while seeking specialist advice about another. Treating trust as all-or-nothing conceals that difference.

Confidence needs room for vulnerability

Trust would be unnecessary if every action were guaranteed and independently inspected. Some vulnerability remains when another person exercises discretion. Monitoring can help, but checking every statement before relying on it would remove much of the point of seeking help.

That does not mean overlooking relevant warning signs. Suppose the neighbor never entered the house but reports detailed observations. The discrepancy warrants inquiry. Asking about it differs from requiring them to prove every ordinary report in advance.

The opposite error also deserves attention. If you reject an accurate report because of an irrelevant prejudice about the speaker, you can lose useful knowledge and wrong them. Responsible distrust requires grounds just as responsible trust does.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Reasons to trust and reasons for a belief

McLeod distinguishes several senses in which trust can be warranted: justified by the trustor’s grounds, well-grounded in actual trustworthiness, or plausible given the relationship. These can come apart. Good apparent reasons might point to an untrustworthy person, while a trustworthy person might be difficult to identify. Distinguish your available evidence from the source’s actual condition.1.

A further distinction concerns whether trusting promotes a valuable end and whether believing a particular report is supported as true. Trust offered to encourage responsibility need not justify confidently believing that responsibility has already been achieved. The existence of therapeutic trust illustrates why a general defense of trusting cannot settle every testimonial belief.2.

The surrounding environment matters

McLeod discusses how a social and political climate can alter appropriate defaults. Accounts disagree about how much initial trust is warranted and how its grounds should be understood. The lesson is to identify the relevant context rather than assume that either universal suspicion or universal confidence is the sole rational stance.3.

The epistemology of testimony likewise distinguishes requiring independent support from allowing an initial entitlement in the absence of doubt. These positions address the grounds for belief, while interpersonal trust views examine norms that govern the exchange. Their questions intersect without being identical.4.
