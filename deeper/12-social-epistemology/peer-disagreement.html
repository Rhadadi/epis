In short

Discovering that a capable person disagrees with you can be evidence about your own fallibility. The puzzle becomes sharp when you have no good reason to regard either person as better placed on the disputed question. Conciliationists think this normally calls for less confidence; steadfast views allow more room to retain your judgment. Intermediate views weigh the original evidence together with what the disagreement reveals. The debate concerns justified confidence, and does not require treating every disagreement as a contest between equals.

Layer 1Re-learn

A second calculation changes your situation

Here is an invented example. You and a colleague independently total a small project budget. You have the same invoices, similar experience, and comparable records of catching mistakes. You obtain £860; your colleague obtains £880. Neither is distracted, and neither has checked twice.

Before speaking, you had a reasonable basis for your answer. After speaking, you learn something new: a comparably reliable calculation conflicts with yours. The invoices have not changed. Your evidence about whether you handled them correctly has changed.

It would be a poor reply to say that your colleague must have erred because your answer is £860. That repeats the judgment under challenge. If you discover that your colleague included a duplicate invoice, you have a different reason: a specific discrepancy that can be checked. The distinction is between assuming your answer wins and finding evidence that explains the disagreement.

Equal respect is not equal competence

An epistemic peer is someone comparably well placed on the particular question. The description concerns relevant evidence and ability, rather than friendship, moral worth, or general intelligence. Two excellent calculators can cease to be peers for this task if only one has received the missing invoice. Conversely, people with different qualifications can be comparably reliable on a straightforward calculation.

Sometimes you cannot establish a comparison. A stranger disputes the budget, but you know nothing about their access or methods. That is uncertainty about relative standing. It should not automatically be converted into either recognized equality or proof of your superiority.

Reduce confidence in an answer, not necessarily change the answer’s number

Equal weight need not mean believing the total is £870. The two reported totals could both be wrong. You can become less confident in £860 and more interested in checking £880 without adopting their numerical midpoint. The compromise concerns your confidence in propositions, rather than a mechanical averaging of whatever numbers those propositions mention.

You can also suspend judgment while acting. The team might delay submitting the budget or use a clearly labeled provisional total. What you should believe and what the situation requires you to do are related questions, but they are not identical.

Layer 2The full story

Fallibility, toleration, and a newer puzzle

Locke’s discussion of degrees of assent connects uncertainty with patience toward disagreement. People commonly hold opinions without demonstrative certainty, and cannot instantly repeat every earlier inquiry. He urges efforts to inform rather than constrain others. This is an earlier treatment of fallible disagreement, not a formulation of contemporary equal-weight conciliationism.1.

Read the original

Locke on disagreement and inquiry

We should do well to commiserate our mutual ignorance, and endeavour to remove it in all the gentle and fair ways of information; and not instantly treat others ill, as obstinate and perverse, because they will not renounce their own, and receive our opinions

2. This passage recommends a manner of inquiry. It does not say that any two opinions deserve identical evidential weight. A practice can be respectful while recognizing substantial differences in competence or evidence.

The contemporary problem asks a more specific question. Suppose you have good grounds, before discovering the dispute, for regarding another person as roughly as likely as you to judge correctly. How should learning their contrary opinion affect your confidence? The restriction to peers isolates the effect of disagreement from the simpler case of encountering a recognized superior or inferior.3.

It also distinguishes a normative question from a psychological one. A person might retain complete confidence, feel embarrassed, or become angry. The philosophical dispute concerns what their evidence warrants, rather than predicting which response they will exhibit. Even a sincere, understandable response can fail to handle new information appropriately.

Peerhood needs careful specification

Frances and Matheson distinguish likelihood peerhood from conditional peerhood. You might initially think two people equally likely to answer correctly, yet also think that, if they disagree, one will probably be right. That distinction matters when the initial assessment already incorporates a very strong commitment to the proposition. Describing someone as generally comparable leaves open how you assess the possibility of a conflict.4.

In the budget example, evidence from earlier disagreements could establish something stronger than similar job titles. Suppose previous checks show that, when the two colleagues differ, neither has a demonstrated advantage. That supports symmetry in the present dispute, provided nothing relevant has changed. By contrast, comparable performance on easy, uncontested tasks might reveal little about who catches the kind of error now at issue.

Equal epistemic position also need not require equality on every factor. One person could have more relevant background knowledge while the other has more time and better access to the records. Depending on the account of peerhood, such advantages might balance out. The literature’s examples often control these variables to isolate the puzzle, while ordinary disputes rarely provide equally clean comparisons.5.

Two further distinctions prevent misleading conclusions. First, peers can both be poorly informed; the central literature generally considers competent peers. Second, being a peer and being recognized as a peer differ. Your rational response depends partly on your evidence about the other person’s position, which may be incomplete or misleading. Recognizing that limitation does not license inventing an advantage for yourself.6.

The equal-weight view has three moving parts

Frances and Matheson reconstruct the equal-weight view using defeat, equal weight, and independence. Discovered disagreement provides a reason to think you have erred; a peer’s opinion supplies a reason comparable in strength to your own; and reasons for discounting the peer must be independent of the disagreement itself. Separating these claims makes the competing views easier to understand.7.

The argument, step by step

A reconstruction of conciliation

  1. Before learning the dispute, I have grounds for treating the other person’s judgment as comparably reliable to mine.
  2. Their contrary judgment gives me a reason to think my own assessment may be mistaken.
  3. I have no independent reason to favor my judgment over theirs in this case.
  4. I should therefore reduce confidence in my original assessment rather than dismiss their judgment merely because it conflicts with mine.
8. This reconstruction supports conciliation. A further equal-weight claim specifies how much weight the opinions receive; conciliation more broadly need not prescribe an exact arithmetic rule.

Christensen’s restaurant-check example supplies the familiar motivation. Friends see the same bill, agree on the tip and division, and independently obtain shares of $43 and $45. Under the stipulated symmetry, finding the disagreement should significantly reduce confidence in the original result. The case excludes easy explanations such as one friend being known to be exhausted or using a different bill.9.

The point is not that a disagreement makes both answers false. One could be correct. It is that correctness and justified retention of confidence can come apart: you can arrive at the correct answer but then acquire strong evidence that calls your confidence into question. A rule for belief must take account of the evidence available to the agent, rather than simply identify the answer that happens to be true.

Independence blocks one easy form of dogmatism

Independence excludes the inference that another person ceased to be a peer merely by disagreeing with your conclusion. Otherwise the rule listen to peers could become empty: agree with anyone who agrees, demote anyone who disagrees. The literature uses this concern to motivate a demand for reasons that do not simply presuppose the disputed verdict.10.

In our budget example, a duplicate invoice offers a potential symmetry breaker. So does learning that your colleague used a different reporting period. Merely feeling certain about £860 does not supply the same kind of explanation. Yet the duplicate itself might be disputed, and determining what counts as sufficiently independent can become difficult. Critics challenge how an independence principle can be formulated without ruling out legitimate uses of the original evidence.11.

The principle also has a scope. It is a constraint on responding to a stipulated peer, not a ban on evaluating reasoning. You can inspect the invoices together, reveal an error, and thereby acquire grounds to change your view of the present calculation. The idealized debate asks which resources remain available at the moment when the dispute is discovered and its explanation is still unsettled.

Why some philosophers defend steadfastness

Steadfast views permit retaining an original judgment in at least some peer disagreements. Their defenses differ. Appeals to private evidence emphasize insights or experiences that cannot be fully shared; appeals to self-trust emphasize the inescapable first-person standpoint from which a person evaluates the dispute. Other arguments question whether another person’s opinion adds evidence about the proposition beyond the shared evidence already considered.12.

Private evidence presents a genuine complication. You can report what a surface looks like without giving another person your visual experience. But conciliationists reply that the other party can have private evidence too. The bare existence of an unshared experience need not break symmetry. The challenge is to explain why your private evidence deserves privileged weight rather than merely noting that it is yours.13.

Self-trust has a related strength and limit. You cannot assess testimony without using your own faculties. It does not follow that those faculties should always outrank the result of another equally reliable inquiry. A reason to trust a colleague can itself be your reason, formed through your own assessment. Critics therefore argue that conciliation need not require abandoning the first-person perspective.14.

Kelly’s earlier argument questions a different step: whether the fact that a competent person formed a belief adds evidence for the proposition or merely evidence about the relationship between the original evidence and the proposition. A concern about double counting motivates this distinction. Replies emphasize that a reliable evaluation may itself provide additional information, rather than simply repeat the data. This dispute helps explain why higher-order evidence matters.15.

Evidence about the evidence

The invoices bear directly on the total. Learning that a comparable calculator got another result bears on your handling of the invoices. The latter is higher-order evidence: evidence about your evidence, reasoning, or epistemic performance. Its subject differs from the budget total, yet it can affect what confidence in that total is justified. The controversy concerns how these levels interact.16.

Kelly’s total-evidence view insists that both the original evidence and the discovered disagreement matter. It resists an approach on which reported opinions entirely determine the final justified confidence. Suppose the underlying evidence supports a low credence, but two people badly misread it and then average their high credences. Their convergence alone should not erase the original evidential failure.17.

Conciliationists can reply by distinguishing a rational response to new information from a fully justified final state. Someone who already mishandled the invoices might respond appropriately to a colleague and still end up with an incorrect or poorly supported total. Alternatively, misleading higher-order evidence may genuinely support a belief from the agent’s position. The dispute consequently requires care about what exactly is being evaluated.18.

That distinction is useful outside philosophy. In the invented case, revising confidence after hearing your colleague can be sensible even if neither of you has noticed the missing invoice. A better update does not guarantee a good final belief. The next step is to investigate the calculation, rather than treat agreement achieved through compromise as independent confirmation.

Intermediate views and extreme cases

Lackey’s justificationist view gives antecedent justification an important role. Her elementary-math example concerns a colleague denying that two plus two equals four. She argues that this need not produce the same conciliation as the restaurant-check case. Strong prior justification, combined with information about oneself, can support reassessing the purported peer’s position. The challenge to independence is deliberate: some original reasons can help establish that a disagreement is extreme.19.

The objection is that high antecedent justification need not immunize a belief against new testimony. Christensen’s lottery example makes this vivid: a ticket is overwhelmingly likely to lose, but the owner’s announcement that it won can still merit investigation. Very high prior confidence and a well-supported reason to reject the new report are not the same thing. The difficult task is distinguishing a credible surprise from a genuine breakdown in another person’s judgment.20.

Does conciliation undermine itself?

People disagree about conciliationism. If its defenders recognize capable opponents, does their own principle require them to weaken or abandon it? This is the self-defeat objection. It challenges the rational maintainability of a view under particular disagreements, rather than straightforwardly demonstrating a logical contradiction in its content.22.

Replies take different routes. Some accept that a true norm might, in certain circumstances, be difficult to justifiably believe. Others compare the situation to modest principles of humility that also face possible disagreement. Another response exempts fundamental principles from their own conciliatory prescription, at the cost of explaining why the exemption is principled. None of these responses should be confused with the assertion that disagreeing people cannot both have reasons.23.

The objection becomes clearer if applied carefully. A budget calculator who recommends checking disagreements could discover that respected colleagues oppose that recommendation. This creates evidence about the recommendation, but does not make checking logically impossible. The issue is what confidence remains warranted, given the specific grounds for regarding those colleagues as reliable on this further question.

The midpoint is a model choice

Formal social epistemology distinguishes several ways of pooling credences. Linear pooling takes a weighted average; geometric approaches combine them differently and preserve different properties. Bayesian updating on others’ opinions requires assumptions about how those opinions were generated. The existence of a convenient arithmetic operation does not establish that it represents the evidence properly.24.

Suppose two colleagues report credences of 0.8 and 0.4 in a proposed explanation of the budget discrepancy. Their unweighted average is 0.6. That calculation alone says nothing about whether both relied on the same overlooked invoice, whether one inspected an additional record, or whether either understood what the reported confidence meant. Treating the result as automatically justified would hide the assumptions that make the opinions comparable.

There is a related distinction between learning another person’s conclusion and learning their reasons. Once the colleague identifies an invoice dated outside the reporting period, you can examine that information directly. Updating on the new record differs from treating their overall confidence as an opaque report. If you then count both the record and an opinion wholly derived from it as independent confirmation, you risk exaggerating the additional evidence.

This does not refute conciliation. It clarifies what information the stipulated case includes. An equal-weight example that fixes comparable access and competence isolates one problem; an actual consultation that reveals new records changes those conditions. A good response states whether confidence changed because of the reported disagreement, the newly disclosed reasons, or a revised assessment of competence. Formal representations can help keep these changes distinct, provided their assumptions remain visible.25.

From ideal peers to actual controversy

Exact peers may be rare. Frances and Matheson explain why the idealization can still matter: controlling other factors helps identify the contribution of disagreement itself, and uncertainty about who is better placed can resemble a peer case. But the resemblance requires argument. Ignorance about a stranger’s competence is not automatically the same evidential state as a long record of parity.26.

Large controversies add dependence and unequal expertise. Ten opinions copied from one report do not carry the same evidential structure as ten separately informed assessments. Nor should the judgment of a specialist and a novice receive equal weight merely because both speak confidently. The literature’s discussion of disagreement by the numbers recognizes both the skeptical pressure of many capable opponents and the importance of how opinions were formed.27.

Deep disagreements complicate matters further. The parties can dispute which sources count as evidence or which principles make an inference reasonable. They may consequently disagree about the very credentials required for peerhood. Some philosophers doubt such disputes can be rationally resolved; others distinguish a reason’s force from its recognition by the opponent. A simple midpoint rule cannot settle that underlying issue.28.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Confidence and cooperation

The disagreement literature separates the appropriate response to a dispute, the justified final belief, and the action to take. You can revise confidence while retaining a working assumption for a project; you can also retain a belief while agreeing that a consequential decision requires another check. Making those commitments explicit prevents a practical compromise from masquerading as settled knowledge.29.

What the chapter leaves open

Equal weight and Bayesian updating are not interchangeable. Formal social epistemology explains that an update on another person’s credence generally requires assumptions about how they formed it and how their information depends on yours. Averaging can be appropriate under particular conditions, but a bare instruction to average leaves these conditions unspecified.30.

For related questions, read experts and novices, why reasonable people disagree, and Bayes’ theorem. The last link supplies a framework for representing evidence, rather than an automatic solution to disputes about its weight.
