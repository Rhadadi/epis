In short

People can be wronged through the practices by which knowledge is shared and understood. Testimonial injustice occurs when prejudice makes someone less credible than they deserve to be. Hermeneutical injustice concerns unfair gaps or exclusions in the resources needed to make important experiences intelligible. These failures can damage the person whose contribution is discounted and the inquiry that loses it. Remedies involve assessing credibility fairly, improving shared understanding, and changing institutions that sustain exclusion.

Layer 1Re-learn

A report that nobody takes seriously

Imagine a maintenance worker reporting that a building’s alarm behaves differently on the night shift. This is an invented case. The manager dismisses the report because they assume workers in this role cannot understand technical equipment. Later, an engineer receives the same report and investigates the fault.

The worker’s report did not become better evidence merely because the engineer heard it. The manager used an irrelevant assumption about the speaker’s social position to reduce its credibility. The loss has two sides: a possible fault was left unexamined, and a person capable of reporting it was treated as an inadequate contributor.

Now change the example. The manager checks the logs and finds that the worker was describing a different building. Doubting the report on that ground is different. People can make mistakes, and fair attention does not require accepting every claim. What matters is whether the judgment tracks relevant evidence or a prejudicial discount.

A problem the available categories cannot capture

Suppose the alarm’s reporting form accepts only working and broken. It has no way to describe a recurring intermittent fault, and the organization excludes night-shift workers from discussions of the reporting system. Their descriptions repeatedly get recoded as user error. Even a worker who understands the pattern may be unable to make it intelligible within the official process.

This second version illustrates a structural possibility. The obstacle is partly the shared vocabulary and partly whose experience shaped it. The worker need not be confused about their own experience. The organization may lack the interpretive resources, or refuse the resources already developed by the workers, that would allow the report to receive appropriate uptake.

A limited form is not automatically an injustice. Any vocabulary leaves something out. The concern arises when exclusion and unequal power make a significant experience systematically harder for some people to communicate or understand. Establishing that diagnosis requires evidence about the process, rather than attaching a label to every misunderstanding.

Correction need not mean automatic belief

A fairer manager could listen without stereotyping, document the circumstances, and invite workers into the redesign of the form. The engineer could test the alleged fault. These steps improve the opportunity for knowledge to enter the inquiry. They do not establish the report’s truth in advance.

Layer 2The full story

What makes the wrong epistemic?

Fricker’s canonical account identifies a wrong to someone in their capacity as a knower. Sinclair’s overview distinguishes testimonial and hermeneutical injustice and connects both to the exercise of social power. Grasswick places the discussion within feminist social epistemology’s attention to the distribution of credibility and participation in inquiry. These accounts concern what people can contribute to knowing, rather than simply whether an interaction is unpleasant.1.

A person can suffer a practical wrong without being disbelieved: a manager may accept the alarm report but refuse to pay the worker. They can also suffer a mistaken credibility judgment that arises without the prejudicial mechanism central to Fricker’s account. The distinctive case combines an epistemic disadvantage with injustice. Keeping the ingredients separate makes the concept more precise and prevents every error in source assessment from becoming the same moral diagnosis.2.

In the maintenance example, the worker possesses relevant observational access. Discounting the report because of a stereotype about their role denies that access its proper weight. The injustice does not require proving that the worker is infallible. A fallible person can deserve more credibility than they receive, and the refusal to consider their contribution can be wrongful even before an investigation settles the underlying claim.

Identity power can operate without an explicit declaration

Fricker’s account of social power includes a socially situated capacity to control others’ actions. Identity power depends on shared understandings of social identities, and can operate actively or passively. A person need not openly announce the stereotype for it to affect who speaks, whose contribution receives attention, or whose interpretation shapes the encounter. Sinclair also distinguishes power exercised by agents from power operating structurally through a social system.3.

In the invented case, the manager actively dismisses the worker. A passive version would arise if the worker had learned that reports from their role never receive attention and consequently stopped submitting them. A structural version could arise through a form and escalation procedure designed around daytime office staff, even when the present manager bears no personal hostility. The mechanisms overlap but suggest different points of intervention.

This distinction prevents an unhelpful choice between deliberate malice and complete innocence of the process. Individual blame, the operation of a stereotype, and the injustice of the resulting arrangement are separate questions. Institutional analysis asks how the arrangement distributes opportunities to be heard. It need not assert that every participant consciously intends the outcome. Anderson’s structural account is particularly important for this reason.4.

Testimonial injustice and relevant doubt

The central testimonial case involves an identity-prejudicial credibility deficit. The hearer attributes less credibility than the speaker deserves because of prejudice concerning a social identity. This formulation includes a comparison: the credibility actually given differs from the credibility warranted by the speaker’s competence, sincerity, evidence, and circumstances. A disagreement by itself does not establish that disparity.5.

Fricker’s literary examples help identify the mechanism. Sinclair discusses Marge Sherwood in The Talented Mr. Ripley: her suspicion concerns the possession of Dickie’s rings, but Herbert Greenleaf dismisses her reasoning as female intuition. O’Connor, Goldberg, and Goldman describe the example of Tom Robinson in To Kill a Mockingbird, whose testimony is prejudicially rejected by an all-white jury. These are illustrations in the philosophical account, not independent empirical studies of credibility.6.

The essential contrast is between an objection to relevant reasons and a discount imposed on the reason-giver through prejudice. If the manager doubts the alarm report because the worker lacks access to the relevant area, that ground can be investigated. If the doubt comes from a blanket assumption that people in the worker’s role cannot observe a technical irregularity, the assessment has substituted a social image for the required judgment.

Not every generalization about a source is treated as objectionable in Fricker’s framework. Ordinary credibility judgments rely on background expectations and cannot require a complete investigation before every exchange. The difficulty lies in prejudicial stereotypes that distort those expectations and resist counterevidence. Sinclair’s account distinguishes useful shortcuts from the negative identity prejudice central to systematic injustice.7.

A person may reject a stereotype and still act through it

Sinclair explains that stereotypes can influence credibility through social imagination without mediation by an explicitly endorsed belief. The example of someone committed to gender equality who nevertheless reacts negatively to female political candidates illustrates the tension. Stating an egalitarian principle does not guarantee that one’s spontaneous assessments reflect it.8.

The practical implication is to compare judgments with reasons. In the invented case, the manager might sincerely believe that all employees deserve respect while routinely dismissing reports from a particular role. A useful question is whether the same description would receive greater weight from another speaker with the same access. Such a comparison can reveal a possible inconsistency, although establishing the source of the inconsistency still requires investigation.

This is also why merely instructing someone to be neutral can be insufficient. A default judgment can carry the imprint of earlier socialization. The philosophical remedy involves reflexive attention to the relation between hearer and speaker, together with willingness to revise the habits governing credibility. It is an account of how judgment should improve, rather than a guarantee that introspection will expose every bias.9.

The harm is more than a missed fact

Fricker distinguishes primary and secondary harms. The primary wrong is to the person’s standing as a giver of knowledge, a capacity connected to human value and identity. Secondary harms can include practical consequences, such as an adverse judgment or lost opportunity, and epistemic consequences, such as weakened confidence in one’s intellectual abilities. The distinction explains why acknowledging the speaker matters even if the same information eventually reaches the inquiry through another source.10.

If the engineer later rediscovers the night-shift pattern, the organization may learn the relevant fact. That does not automatically undo its treatment of the worker. The worker’s participation was refused, and future reports may be affected. Correcting the factual record and correcting a credibility relationship are consequently related tasks with different objects.

The harms also matter to collective inquiry. A system that overlooks contributions from people with distinctive access can lose relevant evidence. Yet the injustice should not be justified solely by arguing that inclusion maximizes useful output. The person’s standing as a contributor is part of the normative concern. On Fricker’s account, ethical and epistemic dimensions meet at this point.11.

The argument, step by step

Why the criticism has two dimensions

  1. The speaker has a contribution whose credibility should be assessed through relevant evidence.
  2. Identity prejudice causes the hearer to give that contribution less credibility than it deserves.
  3. The hearer’s judgment therefore mishandles the epistemic assessment.
  4. It also wrongs the speaker in their capacity as a contributor to knowledge.
12. Premise 2 carries the crucial burden. Evidence of disagreement or disappointment does not establish it. The conclusion identifies both dimensions without assuming that a well-treated speaker must always be correct.

Hermeneutical injustice concerns the tools of understanding

Hermeneutical resources include concepts and interpretive practices through which experiences become understandable. Fricker’s second central case concerns a significant experience obscured by an unfair gap in collective resources. Unequal participation in creating those resources can leave some people’s experiences harder to understand or communicate than others’. The difficulty is structural, rather than merely a failure by one hearer to listen politely.13.

The account uses sexual harassment as a central example. Before a socially recognized concept made the pattern articulable, people could struggle to connect and communicate experiences that prevailing descriptions trivialized. Collective discussion and the development of a concept changed the interpretive possibilities. This example should not be read as saying that no woman previously understood any unwanted conduct or suffered no recognizable harm; the issue is the availability and social uptake of an adequate shared interpretation.14.

Our reporting-form example represents a smaller hypothetical mechanism. A vocabulary shaped without the night shift can fail to register an important pattern. The epistemic disadvantage falls unevenly: those experiencing the intermittent fault bear the burden of its unintelligibility. Others may lack the same understanding without suffering the same practical consequences. Sinclair’s account emphasizes this asymmetry when explaining the wrong.15.

The example requires qualification. A form can be inadequate through an ordinary design mistake. To make it an instance of hermeneutical injustice, the story must include the unfair marginalization that shapes or maintains the gap. Otherwise it illustrates a communication problem without establishing the additional moral diagnosis. A careful use of the concept identifies the relevant social mechanism, the significance of the experience, and the unequal burden.

Understanding oneself and being understood are different

Later discussions challenge an overly unified picture of collective understanding. A marginalized group may already possess the concepts needed to interpret its experiences, while dominant institutions fail or refuse to use them. Grasswick discusses this objection through Mason, Medina, Dotson, and Pohlhaus. Sinclair presents Goetze’s distinction between cognitive and communicative intelligibility. A person can understand perfectly well yet be unable to secure uptake at a crucial moment.16.

In the hypothetical workplace, night-shift staff might have a precise description of the alarm’s pattern. Their disadvantage is then not absence of understanding among themselves. It lies in the office’s refusal to recognize that description. Calling the workers confused would repeat the problem. The appropriate object of scrutiny is the boundary between the group’s resources and the institution’s authorized categories.

Dotson’s contributory injustice names the refusal by a dominant group to employ interpretive resources that marginalized communities have developed. The concept locates a failure of uptake even where those resources already exist. It also connects injustice with willful ignorance: retaining an inadequate interpretation can be convenient for people whose position it protects. This is a proposed mechanism to investigate, not an automatic explanation of every unfamiliar term’s rejection.17.

Silencing has more than one form

Dotson distinguishes testimonial quieting and testimonial smothering. Quieting concerns failure to recognize a speaker as a knower and offer appropriate uptake. Smothering concerns the speaker’s coerced truncation of testimony because the audience lacks or refuses the competence to receive it appropriately. The distinction shifts attention from overt dismissal to what may never be said.18.

Suppose a worker reports only a minor nuisance because a fuller description would be used to portray the night shift as careless. That invented version illustrates the shape of smothering. A short report need not mean there was little to report. Investigators must attend to the circumstances governing what speakers can safely and intelligibly communicate, without pretending they can infer the omitted content without evidence.

Related work discusses epistemic exploitation: imposing on marginalized people the labor of educating dominant groups about their experiences. Improving inquiry can require seeking additional perspectives, but that requirement does not settle who should bear the cost. An organization could pay workers for participating in an investigation, use existing reports, and make participation voluntary rather than demand repeated explanations as a condition of basic recognition.19.

Can excess credibility also wrong someone?

Fricker’s central case emphasizes credibility deficit. Davis and Medina challenge or extend that emphasis by examining credibility excess. Sinclair explains how positive stereotypes can restrict a speaker’s individuality, making them valuable only as representatives of the role a dominant audience assigns them. More credibility in one respect does not necessarily amount to better treatment in every respect.20.

Consider another invented variant. Management assumes every worker from a particular background is naturally good at mechanical diagnosis. It accepts that person’s suggestions uncritically while ignoring their request for training and assigning them all troubleshooting work. The stereotype grants presumed competence, but can also narrow the person’s recognized contribution and impose a burden. Whether this amounts to the specific injustice under discussion depends on the circumstances and account of harm.

This extension also cautions against treating fairness as a universal upward adjustment of credibility. A correction should respond to the actual distortion. Sometimes it involves restoring weight to testimony; sometimes removing an inflated expectation; sometimes changing who can introduce questions or define the agenda. Anderson notes that epistemic exclusion can affect people as inquirers as well as testifiers.21.

A fair inquiry can still reach an unwelcome conclusion

Return to the worker’s report after it receives a proper investigation. Suppose the logged pattern turns out to have a benign explanation. That outcome need not vindicate the manager’s prejudicial dismissal. The earlier judgment and the eventual result have different grounds. Conversely, if the report proves correct, correctness alone does not establish every allegation about how prejudice operated. A careful assessment reconstructs the actual reasoning and institutional process. This distinction preserves both fair participation and the possibility of genuine disagreement. Fricker’s central concept concerns the credibility deserved and the prejudicial deficit, rather than a rule that speakers who experience exclusion cannot be mistaken.23.

Individual virtues and institutional repair

Fricker proposes testimonial justice as a corrective virtue. The hearer develops reflexive critical awareness of how identity relations may distort credibility and adjusts judgment accordingly. Hermeneutical justice adds alertness to the possibility that apparent unintelligibility reflects an inadequate interpretive environment. Sinclair emphasizes patience, attention, further evidence, and sometimes reserving judgment rather than prematurely discounting the speaker.24.

These virtues offer practical starting points in the workplace case: record the report fairly, distinguish unfamiliar expression from incompetence, ask clarifying questions, and avoid assuming that difficulty with the form reflects difficulty observing the fault. They also preserve inquiry. The aim is to remove the unjust obstacle and improve assessment, not certify an untested explanation.

Anderson argues that structural injustice requires structural remedies. Segregated groups can develop shared perspectives that exclude others’ experiences, even when particular interactions involve no consciously prejudicial intention. Integration and inclusive institutions can help alter those conditions. Grasswick similarly explains why transactional remedies between individual knowers may be inadequate for a complex network of exclusion.25.

Changing the reporting form, providing a route to challenge rejected reports, and including night-shift experience in decisions address different parts of the hypothetical mechanism. Each should be assessed for whether it enables relevant contribution and correction. Inviting someone into a meeting without permitting their evidence to affect its outcome leaves the central problem intact. Institutional repair requires uptake as well as nominal access.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Avoid turning a diagnosis into a verdict about truth

An allegation of epistemic injustice deserves assessment like any other significant claim. Ask what contribution was made, what credibility it warranted, how it was received, and what role prejudice or structural exclusion played. Establishing unfair treatment does not settle every underlying factual dispute; failing to establish it does not show that the speaker’s report is false. The concepts clarify a process of knowing and the wrongs it can sustain.26.

Connected questions

The chapter’s two central categories open a larger inquiry into the production of ignorance, unequal authority, and opportunities to ask questions. Standpoint and feminist epistemology examines how social location can supply overlooked evidence. Experts and novices considers the relevant grounds for assigning authority. Epistemic institutions moves the analysis from an encounter to the design of a process.27.
