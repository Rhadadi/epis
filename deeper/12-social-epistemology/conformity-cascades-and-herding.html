Conformity, cascades, and herding

In short

Another person’s choice can convey information, but it can also conceal the evidence they set aside. An information cascade arises when people follow earlier choices and stop expressing their own private information through their actions. Conformity can similarly make agreement look stronger than the underlying evidence warrants. Individually understandable choices may then produce a poor collective result. To assess a crowd’s agreement, examine what information its members actually contribute.

Layer 1Re-learn

Following a choice is not receiving its grounds

Here is an invented example. Several volunteers must choose a route for delivering supplies. The first chooses the eastern road after seeing one favorable report. The second has a small private reason to prefer the western road, but thinks the first probably knows more and follows. The third sees two eastern choices and does the same.

The third volunteer may interpret two choices as two separate favorable reports. In fact, the second choice was partly a response to the first. Information about the western road has not entered the visible sequence. Later volunteers cannot recover that private evidence merely by observing the growing line.

The mistake need not lie in an obviously foolish individual decision. Given uncertainty about what predecessors know, following can appear reasonable. The collective result can still conceal most of the group’s information. Individual rationality and collective success are different objects of assessment.

Social pressure adds another mechanism

Suppose a volunteer follows because dissent would offend the team leader. Their action now reveals even less about their evidence concerning the road. An observer who interprets every eastern choice as a sincere independent judgment will overestimate the support.

This differs from an information cascade driven by inference, although the mechanisms can coexist. In one case the volunteer tries to learn from others; in the other they respond to a social cost. Both can reduce the visible contribution of private information.

A useful change in the invented case is to collect initial assessments and reasons before revealing the leader’s preference. Discussion can then address the reports rather than infer them from public choices. Whether this works in an actual organization needs assessment of its circumstances.

Layer 3Beyond the chapter

Independence concerns information

The social-epistemology entry discusses information cascades through a model of sequential choices and examines conformity in network models. Both highlight why an apparent headcount can exaggerate independent support. Yet independence should not be interpreted as a demand never to communicate. Sharing evidence can improve judgments; copying verdicts without exposing their grounds creates a different informational structure.1.

The useful question is what additional information a new report supplies. If a volunteer has inspected the western road, their contribution can correct the cascade. If they merely repeat the group’s preference, the report may add little. Distinguishing those roles matters more than treating agreement itself as either wise or foolish.

Models show possibilities, not universal prescriptions

Network models described in the entry show how conformity can impede successful belief formation and how premature consensus can end exploration too soon. These results depend on the modeled tasks, information, and update rules. They do not establish that slower communication is always better or that stubbornness should be encouraged in every group.2.

Instead, use the mechanisms to ask whether a procedure preserves useful variation long enough to check it. A team can provide protected opportunities for disagreement while still requiring evidence and a final decision. Dissent is an opportunity for scrutiny, rather than proof that the dissenter is correct.

Read crowds for aggregation conditions and group knowledge for the difference between individual judgments and collective commitments.
