Deeper study · Chapter 12

Social Epistemology: Knowing Together

For when a section didn't click, or left you wanting more: each page explains the idea again, tells the full story, fills in what the chapter leaves out, and gives the sources.

Raphael, The School of Athens, 1509–1511

← Back to Chapter 12

  • Short study · 3 minWhy knowledge is socialAn individual’s knowledge can depend on work done by other people. Those people supply observations, develop methods, preserve records, and check interpretations that the individual cannot reproduce alone.
  • Short study · 3 minTrust and epistemic dependenceDepending on another person can be a rational way to learn. Trust adds an interpersonal dimension to reliance: another person’s competence and willingness matter, and failure can be experienced as betrayal.
  • Full study · 14 minExperts and novicesYou can have good reasons to trust an answer that you cannot work out yourself. The difficulty is identifying the right person to trust, especially when apparent experts disagree.
  • Full study · 14 minPeer disagreementDiscovering that a capable person disagrees with you can be evidence about your own fallibility. The puzzle becomes sharp when you have no good reason to regard either person as better placed on the disputed question.
  • Short study · 3 minWhy reasonable people disagreeDisagreement need not arise from dishonesty or stupidity. People can encounter different evidence, interpret a question differently, or attach different importance to a decision’s consequences.
  • Full study · 14 minEpistemic injusticePeople can be wronged through the practices by which knowledge is shared and understood. Testimonial injustice occurs when prejudice makes someone less credible than they deserve to be.
  • Short study · 3 minStandpoint and feminist epistemologyWhat people can observe depends partly on their position in a social arrangement. Feminist epistemology examines how gender and other relations affect inquiry, authority, and the significance of questions.
  • Short study · 3 minEcho chambers and epistemic bubblesA narrow information network can fail in more than one way. An epistemic bubble omits relevant voices, while an echo chamber actively undermines trust in voices outside it.
  • Short study · 3 minConformity, cascades, and herdingAnother person’s choice can convey information, but it can also conceal the evidence they set aside. An information cascade arises when people follow earlier choices and stop expressing their own private information through their actions.
  • Short study · 3 minThe wisdom and madness of crowdsA crowd can combine information that no one member possesses. It can also multiply a shared error.
  • Short study · 3 minGroup knowledge and beliefSaying that a committee believes something need not mean that every member privately believes it. A group can reach an official position through commitments or procedures.
  • Short study · 3 minMisinformation, disinformation, and propagandaMisleading communication can arise through error, deliberate deception, or selective use of genuine information. Its content and its producer’s intent are different questions.
  • Short study · 3 minConspiracy theoriesA conspiracy explanation attributes an event to people coordinating secretly. That description alone does not establish that the explanation is false.
  • Short study · 3 minEpistemic institutionsAn epistemic institution organizes the production, checking, preservation, or distribution of information. Its reliability depends on procedures and relationships as well as the abilities of its members.
  • Short study · 3 minEpistemic autonomy and artificial intelligenceThinking for yourself can include choosing reliable sources and managing dependence on them. Artificial intelligence makes that dependence harder to classify because a generated report need not involve the same responsibility as a human assertion.
