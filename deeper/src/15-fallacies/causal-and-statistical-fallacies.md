> **In short.** A numerical pattern needs an account of how the cases and measurements were produced. A change after an intervention does not by itself establish the intervention's effect. A dramatic result can reflect selective reporting, extreme-case selection, or a search through many possibilities. Different errors require different repairs: better comparisons, clearer denominators, or new evidence. Statistics becomes useful when the design supports the inference being made.

## Re-learn

### Compare with what would otherwise happen

Imagine a company training the ten teams with the worst monthly error rates. Their rates improve the following month. Training may have helped, but the before-and-after comparison leaves alternatives open: unusual workloads ended, recording changed, or teams were selected partly because of temporary bad luck.

The IEP account of post hoc reasoning warns against jumping from temporal succession to causation. Its common-cause entry explains how another factor can generate both observations. These are warnings about an insufficient inference; a sequence or correlation can still form part of a fuller causal case. [@fallacy-iep, Post Hoc, Common Cause]

A comparison with similarly selected teams that did not receive training would help, particularly if assignment and measurement permit the groups to be compared. Even then, ask about differences in exposure and implementation. See [causal inference](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/causation-and-causal-inference) for designs and their assumptions.

### Selection changes the expected pattern

Choosing the worst recorded month selects both persistent problems and temporary fluctuations. If the fluctuations are not perfectly persistent, a later measurement can be less extreme without any successful intervention. Mistaking that movement for an intervention's effect is the regression fallacy identified by the IEP. It does not establish that every improvement is regression or that the training had no effect. [@fallacy-iep, Regression]

Similarly, a list of successful teams omits the denominator needed to judge success rates. Five successes mean different things among six attempts and among six hundred. Ask what happened to every eligible case, including withdrawals and missing observations. Evidence selection belongs to the inference, rather than to an optional appendix.

## Beyond the chapter

### Discovery and confirmation need different work

Suppose analysts inspect one hundred possible subdivisions of the teams, find a striking association in one, and present it as if that subdivision had been the sole prediction. A pattern worth investigating has become evidence with its search history concealed. The IEP sharpshooter entry describes overemphasised coincidences, while its suppressed-evidence entry explains how omission alters an argument's apparent strength. [@fallacy-iep, Sharpshooter’s, Suppressed Evidence]

Fresh data using the chosen prediction can test whether the pattern persists. The original search can remain useful as exploration. Distinguish finding a candidate explanation from giving it the evidential weight of a narrowly specified, independently tested prediction. See [replication and selection](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-replication-crisis).

### Do not reverse a conditional

In a constructed screening example, ninety of one hundred defective items trigger an alarm. That sensitivity does not tell us what fraction of alarmed items are defective. We also need the number of sound items and their false-alarm rate. The IEP prosecutor's-fallacy example similarly warns about overlooking the pool of possible cases when assessing a match. [@fallacy-iep, Prosecutor’s Fallacy]

If there are 100 defective and 9,900 sound items, sensitivity is 90%, and 1% of sound items trigger alarms, there are 90 true alarms and 99 false alarms. Under these stipulated conditions, 90 of 189 alarms indicate defects: about 47.6%. The arithmetic supplies a checkable example, not an estimate for any real test. See [Bayes' theorem](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/bayes-theorem) for the general relationship.
