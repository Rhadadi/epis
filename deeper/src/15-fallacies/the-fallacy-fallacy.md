> **In short.** Refuting an argument removes one route to its conclusion. It need not show that the conclusion is false. A correct claim can have a poor defence, and a mistaken defence can be improved. Distinguish rejecting support, withholding belief, and establishing the opposite. The next useful step is to ask what independent evidence bears on the claim.

## Re-learn

### What exactly has been defeated?

Consider a fictional claim that a shop closes at six. Someone supports it by saying that all well-run shops close at six and this shop is well run. The generalisation is unsupported. Discovering that defect leaves the actual closing time open: the posted hours could still establish six.

The IEP introduction explicitly notes that reaching truth does not guarantee good reasoning. Groarke's treatment explains why premise acceptability and the strength of an inference must be assessed separately. A criticism should state which of those has failed and what follows from that failure. [@fallacy-iep, § 1; @groarke2026, § 4.1]

The opposite mistake is to treat a true conclusion as vindicating every route used to reach it. If the shop does close at six, that does not establish the generalisation about all well-run shops. Both mistakes collapse the distinction between the quality of support and the truth of the supported claim.

### Withholding is not denying

Imagine the only information available is the poor generalisation. It may be reasonable to withhold judgment about the hours. It would require further support to assert that the shop does not close at six. The failed argument supplies an absence of adequate support in this situation, rather than proof of the contrary.

Sometimes a criticism does establish more. If a timetable directly states that this shop closes at eight today, and its applicability and reliability are established, that is positive evidence against six. The additional evidence is doing the work. Saying that the first argument was bad does not communicate it.

## Beyond the chapter

### A label is a claim needing defence

A charge of fallacy can itself be mistaken. Groarke discusses reasonable instances of patterns traditionally listed as fallacies, including appeals involving emotion, a person's circumstances and predicted chains of consequences. His account also explains objections to treating fallacy catalogues as a complete theory of good argument. [@groarke2026, § 4.2]

Calling an appeal to a specialist *appeal to authority* leaves the central question unanswered: was relying on that person's relevant expertise reasonable here? The IEP entry distinguishes legitimate reliance from defective appeals. Likewise, a conflict of interest can matter to testimony even when an irrelevant insult would not. [@fallacy-iep, Appeal to Authority, Ad Hominem]

An effective criticism supplies the missing account: this speaker's expertise concerns building design, while the disputed premise concerns historical ownership records. That explains why this particular appeal needs more support. It is more informative than reciting a Latin name.

### Repair, then reassess

Suppose the defender replaces the original generalisation with a current official schedule. Assess the new argument on its merits. The earlier mistake remains a reason to be attentive, but insisting that the person can never recover would prevent correction. Check whether the replacement addresses the original gap and whether other consequential assumptions remain.

Repair also has limits. Quietly substituting a weaker conclusion can leave the original claim unsupported. Record the conclusion now being defended and the confidence its evidence warrants. See [fallacies of ambiguity](deeper:15-fallacies/fallacies-of-ambiguity) and [fallacies of presumption](deeper:15-fallacies/fallacies-of-presumption) for how such shifts arise.

The aim is a warranted assessment, which may include suspension, a qualified conclusion, or a supported rejection. Identifying a defect is one part of that assessment. It becomes useful when it tells the reader what remains unresolved and what evidence could resolve it.
