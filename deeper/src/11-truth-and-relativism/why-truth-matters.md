> **In short.** A belief can be sincere, well supported, or comforting while still being false. Truth marks a kind of correctness that those features do not replace. It matters when we rely on a report to understand or act in the world. Calling truth a norm does not mean every speaker actually pursues it. The philosophical question is how that norm belongs to belief and assertion.

## Re-learn

### Three ways a report can go wrong

Imagine an invented train timetable announcing a departure at noon. Its compiler might carelessly guess, carefully rely on an obsolete record, or deliberately print the wrong time. The failures differ. Yet the report is false if the train departs at eleven, regardless of which failure produced it.

This separates accuracy of the report from sincerity and quality of inquiry. A sincere speaker can be mistaken; a careful investigation can encounter misleading evidence. Russell distinguishes what makes a belief true from how we discover that it is true. [@russell1912, ch. XII]

The distinction is useful for responsibility too. One person might deserve criticism for neglecting an available update. Another might have taken reasonable precautions and still been misled. Calling both reports false does not settle those further assessments.

### A norm is not a description of everyone's motive

Glanzberg explains the claim that assertion aims at truth as a proposed account of the practice, rather than the prediction that every speaker wants to be accurate. A lie or an attempt to flatter can still present its content as true. Such an assertion remains liable to a kind of criticism even when truth was not the speaker's purpose. [@glanzberg2025, § 6.6]

The imaginary timetable presents itself as describing a departure. That role gives a reader a reason to check whether it gets the departure right. Its compiler's wish to improve sales would not answer that question. The norm identifies a standard by which the report can fail, even if the compiler was pursuing another aim.

## Beyond the chapter

### Norms can compete or overlap

There is debate about whether truth alone captures the governing norm of assertion. Glanzberg describes Williamson's alternative knowledge norm, under which one should assert only what one knows. That is stronger than simply uttering a true sentence by lucky guess. [@glanzberg2025, § 6.6]

Thus truth can matter without being the whole account of good assertion. Evidence, knowledge, relevance, and responsibility can supply further requirements. [Knowledge and assertion](deeper:05-the-nature-of-knowledge/knowledge-assertion-and-action) explains the particular debate about knowledge.

### A modest theory need not make truth unimportant

Deflationists resist a substantial underlying truth property, but they can still explain the concept's importance in generalizing norms. Armour-Garb and colleagues show how a norm stated for one assertion can be generalized using the truth predicate. They also reconstruct objections that this expressive account leaves something unexplained. [@armourgarb-etal2026, § 4.4]

Disagreement about the nature of truth therefore does not entail indifference to getting claims right. A speaker's attitude and a philosophical account of the concept are different matters. [Theories of truth](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/theories-of-truth) develops that distinction.

### Fallibility and action

In the timetable example, uncertainty about the record gives a reason to seek an update or communicate the uncertainty. It does not make the departure occur at two incompatible times. Likewise, refusing to claim certainty can be part of caring about truth: it avoids presenting an uncertain estimate as established.

The central habit is to keep the questions connected without collapsing them. What happened? What evidence do we have? How confidently should we report it? What precautions should action require? A strong answer states which of those questions it has addressed.
