> **In short.** A false statement can result from error, deliberate deception, or indifference to whether it is true. Frankfurt's account of bullshit focuses on that indifference. The speaker selects what serves another purpose rather than what the evidence supports. A statement can happen to be true and still be produced in this way. Assessing the speech requires both checking the claim and examining its evidential accountability.

## Re-learn

### The falsehood does not identify the motive

Imagine three fictional speakers giving the wrong train departure time. One consulted an obsolete timetable. Another believes the correct time but gives a different one to mislead a passenger. The third chooses a time that makes their presentation sound impressive without checking any timetable.

The statements are equally inaccurate, but their production differs. Hansson summarizes Frankfurt's distinction: a liar deliberately avoids the truth, whereas the bullshitter is indifferent to whether the statement is true or false and interested in its suitability for another purpose. [@hansson2025, § 7.4]

That distinction also separates bullshit from any false claim as such. The first speaker might have been conscientious but mistaken. The third might accidentally select the correct time. Neither the false outcome nor the true outcome alone establishes the relevant attitude.

### Check content and conduct

The passenger needs to know the departure time regardless of the motive. But assessing the source for future use also requires asking how it selects and revises its claims. Does it consult relevant evidence? Does it correct errors? Does contrary information matter to its next statement?

These questions target accountability. They avoid assuming access to another person's mind merely from one inaccurate sentence. An inference about their indifference needs support from the way they handle evidence, rather than dislike of their message.

## Beyond the chapter

### The problem behind the label

The useful concern in talk of post-truth is a breakdown in the practice of making public claims answerable to evidence. That concern should be stated precisely. An isolated error, a disagreement about values, and systematic disregard of checking are different problems; labeling all of them alike obscures the response each needs.

Glanzberg explains why a norm of assertion is not a description of every speaker's actual aim. A statement can present something as true even when the speaker aims to flatter or deceive. Its public role gives others a reason to assess its truth, independently of that private purpose. [@glanzberg2025, § 6.6]

### Confidence can substitute for inquiry

In the imaginary presentation, the speaker can become more emphatic when challenged without becoming better informed. Repetition, polish, and confidence do not supply the missing timetable. The response should identify the actual evidence required and distinguish it from assurances about sincerity or loyalty.

Hansson also discusses Moberger's proposal to treat pseudoscience as culpably unconscientious speech of this kind. This is a philosophical proposal about its character, rather than a verdict that every mistaken scientific statement is bullshit. [@hansson2025, § 7.4]

### Correction and communication

If a public record includes the invented wrong timetable, a correction should clearly state the supported time and the source. An assessment of the speaker's conduct can then be given separately. Keeping these tasks distinct makes the correction useful even to someone unconvinced about the motive.

Checking takes work, but difficulty does not by itself establish a numerical law about the effort required or a theory of everyone's gullibility. The specific claim, available records, and audience determine what correction needs. Care about truth includes avoiding unsupported exaggerations while criticizing unsupported speech.
