> **In short.** The liar combines self-reference with familiar principles about truth. If a sentence says that it is false, apparently reasonable steps lead to conflict. A variant saying that it is not true makes simply adding a third status insufficient. Different approaches restrict truth principles, alter logic, or change the interpretation. The challenge is to retain useful truth talk while explaining exactly which step fails.

## Re-learn

### Follow the dependency

Construct a sentence **L** whose content is: **L is false**. If L is true, what it says holds, so it is false. If it is false, what it says appears to hold, so it is true. With the assumption that L is true or false, the reasoning yields both. Beall and colleagues reconstruct this simple-falsity liar. [@beall-etal2026, § 1.1]

The example is a constructed logical case, not a historical quotation. Its interest is the interaction of ordinary-looking principles. The truth predicate seems to permit moving from a sentence to a claim of its truth and back. The entry calls these directions capture and release. [@beall-etal2026, § 2.2]

### Why a gap is not a complete answer

Now construct **U**, which says that **U is not true**. If U receives a status other than true or false, it is still not true. That appears to make what it says correct. The simple addition of a third label therefore leaves a strengthened difficulty. The logic and the treatment of negation need further explanation. [@beall-etal2026, § 1.2]

The paradox is consequently not resolved by saying the sentence sounds strange. One must identify a principle, an inference, or an expressive assumption that the proposed account changes.

## Beyond the chapter

### Different costs of different responses

Tarski's typed approach defines truth for an object language in a richer metalanguage. The object language lacks the appropriately self-applicable truth predicate. Beall and colleagues explain how this blocks the relevant construction and why extending it to ordinary truth talk raises expressive problems. [@beall-etal2026, § 4.3.1]

Paracomplete approaches alter logic to allow gaps. Kripke's influential framework introduces a partial truth predicate whose extension and anti-extension need not exhaust the sentences. The account is more substantial than simply labeling the liar neither true nor false. [@beall-etal2026, § 4.1.1]

Paraconsistent approaches permit certain contradictions while rejecting the principle that a contradiction entails every statement. Priest's dialetheic treatment is one such response. Accepting a contradiction and accepting triviality are therefore distinct commitments. [@beall-etal2026, § 4.1.2]

Each approach needs to explain how ordinary truth claims continue to work. A restriction may protect consistency while limiting what can be expressed. A revised logic may preserve expressive resources while changing familiar inferences. Those trade-offs are part of the argument, rather than embarrassing details to omit.

### What the paradox does not establish

A problematic self-referential sentence does not show that the report about an ordinary door is unknowable or that everyone may choose its truth. The liar concerns a particular combination of truth principles, expressive capacity, and inference. Its implications must be traced through that combination.

Likewise, endorsing a correspondence or pragmatic theory does not automatically supply a solution. The account still needs rules for its truth talk. [Theories of truth](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/theories-of-truth) explains how the metaphysical and semantic projects relate.

The earlier [paradoxes page](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/paradoxes-as-stress-tests) discusses why a paradox can diagnose pressure among principles. The [relativism page](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/relativism) examines a separate issue about assessment standards; it should not be treated as the same response as revising logic for the liar.
