> **In short.** A theory of truth asks what it is for a claim to be true, rather than merely how we can check it. Correspondence connects truth to the world; coherence connects it to a specified system; pragmatist approaches connect it to inquiry and practice. Semantic theories show how truth can be defined for suitable languages. Deflationists question the need for a substantial underlying property, while pluralists propose different realizations of a shared role. These approaches overlap in some commitments and disagree over what needs explaining.

## Re-learn

### Three questions about one claim

Imagine a report that an invented building's rear door is open. The report might be true because the door is open, well supported because a reliable camera shows it, and useful because it helps someone choose an exit. Truth, evidential support, and usefulness are different questions. A camera can malfunction; a useful warning can be mistaken.

Russell begins his discussion by separating what truth means from how we discover which beliefs are true. That distinction prevents a proposed test from being mistaken for an account of the property tested. [@russell1912, ch. XII]

The competing theories do not all answer the same question in the same way. Glanzberg distinguishes substantial accounts of truth's nature from views that resist a substantial metaphysical explanation. Capps also separates pragmatic projects concerned with definition, justification, and the use of truth in inquiry. [@glanzberg2025, § 1, 5; @capps2023, § 5.1]

### A small schema with a large use

The basic T-pattern says that a sentence is true if and only if what it says is the case. For our invented example: the sentence *The rear door is open* is true if and only if the rear door is open. Mentioning the sentence on the left differs from using it to describe the door on the right.

Tarski's Convention T requires an adequate definition to imply the appropriate instances for the language being defined. It is an adequacy condition, not by itself a complete definition. His recursive technique supplies more: it connects simple sentences, combinations, and quantified sentences through reference and satisfaction. [@glanzberg2025, § 2.2, 2.3, 2.4]

### The important limit

Agreeing with the T-pattern does not settle every dispute about truth's nature. A realist and an anti-realist can disagree over what truth or reference involves while employing Tarskian resources. The schema constrains an account without automatically choosing its metaphysics. [@glanzberg2025, § 4.2]

## The full story

### Begin with the bearer and the explanatory task

Truth is attributed to beliefs, propositions, sentences, and utterances. Philosophers disagree about which are primary. Glanzberg notes that different substantial theories can work with different bearers: the choice does not simply follow from selecting correspondence rather than coherence. The bearer must have meaningful content of the relevant kind. [@glanzberg2025, § 6.1]

A sentence can be an expression used on different occasions. A proposition is a proposed account of what is expressed. In the invented door example, several languages could convey the same report. Conversely, a context-sensitive sentence could convey different reports on different occasions. The [truth-bearers page](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/truth-bearers) explains why those differences matter before any assessment of truth.

Next identify the explanatory task. Are we asking what makes this report true, how to recognize that it is true, or why the word *true* is useful? A theory might illuminate one task while declining another. Calling it incomplete requires explaining why the declined task must be answered, rather than assuming every approach shares the same ambition. [@glanzberg2025, Preamble, § 5; @capps2023, § 5.1]

### A primary text: Russell's distinction

Russell's *The Problems of Philosophy* develops a correspondence account after distinguishing truth from the means of knowing it. He treats truth and falsehood as properties of beliefs whose status depends on something beyond the belief itself. [@russell1912, ch. XII]

::: original Believing does not supply the fact
> What makes a belief true is a _fact_, and this fact does not (except in exceptional cases) in any way involve the mind of the person who has the belief.
:::
[@russell1912, ch. XII]. Russell's exceptions include future matters that the believer can affect. The claim concerns what makes the belief true, not a guarantee that a believer can recognize the fact without error.

This is useful even before accepting Russell's detailed theory. In the invented door case, someone can help make the door open by opening it. That changes the situation the report describes. Merely preferring the report, however, does not establish that the door is open. Causal influence on the world differs from truth by endorsement.

Russell's actual analysis is more elaborate than that example. He treats judgment as a relation involving a mind and several objects. A true judgment has a corresponding complex in which the objects are related in the relevant order; a false judgment lacks that corresponding complex. His treatment is one historical account, not the only version of correspondence. [@russell1912, ch. XII]

### Correspondence: the world enters the explanation

The central intuition is that truth depends on how things are. A substantial correspondence theory specifies the bearers, the facts or other worldly resources, and the relation linking them. A slogan connecting truth and reality leaves those specifications open. [@glanzberg2025, § 1.1.2]

For a simple claim about an object's property, a structured proposition and a structured fact can appear to match neatly. The account becomes more demanding for negative, general, and otherwise complex claims. Must there be negative or general facts, or can more complex correspondence relations connect those claims to simpler facts? Glanzberg identifies this as a substantive issue for the theory. [@glanzberg2025, § 1.1.2]

Our invented report that no rear door is open is not simply a second picture of an open door. An account needs to explain how its truth depends on the arrangement of all relevant doors, without treating grammatical resemblance as a complete solution. Likewise, a general report about every exit has a scope that goes beyond one photographed doorway.

One historical caution matters. Moore and Russell did not hold a single correspondence theory throughout their careers. Glanzberg describes their earlier identity account, on which true propositions were facts, and their subsequent rejection of propositions in developing correspondence views. A modern reconstruction using structured propositions is therefore not exactly the theory Russell presents in 1912. [@glanzberg2025, § 1.1.1, 1.1.2]

### Coherence: which system, and which relation?

A coherence theory associates truth with membership in or a relation to a specified system of beliefs. Coherence is usually stronger than mere consistency. Glanzberg's reconstruction distinguishes modern slogans from Joachim's idealist account of a significant whole and degrees of truth. [@glanzberg2025, § 1.2]

That distinction prevents an easy caricature. A coherentist need not claim that any internally consistent story is true. The theory owes an account of the privileged system and of coherence, rather than simply permitting any collection without contradiction. The objection about rival coherent stories presses this explanatory burden. [@glanzberg2025, § 1.2; @russell1912, ch. XII]

Imagine two fictional histories of the same building. Each explains its architecture, records, and apparent wear. Their internal consistency does not establish that both histories occurred. A coherence theory needs a reason why the relevant system has the truth-conferring relation, rather than merely the features of a well-developed narrative.

Russell raises both the possibility of multiple coherent systems and the dependence of coherence on logical principles. In his account, coherence can be an important test once some truths are available without constituting truth's meaning. These are objections to evaluate, not conclusions every coherentist must accept. [@russell1912, ch. XII]

### Truth and justification in the coherence argument

Glanzberg reconstructs an epistemological motivation: if justification depends on relations among beliefs, and a fully justified belief is true, truth can be understood through the relevant coherent system. Each transition carries a commitment. In particular, the relation between full justification and truth needs more than a remark that ordinary beliefs support one another. [@glanzberg2025, § 1.2]

::: argument A bridge that needs defending
1. A belief is justified by its relation to a suitable system of beliefs.
2. A fully justified belief is true.
3. The system relation can therefore help explain truth.
:::
[@glanzberg2025, § 1.2]. The second premise and the nature of the suitable system are substantive, rather than automatic consequences of the first.

Ordinary justification remains compatible with error. A scientist can reason carefully from misleading evidence. Identifying truth with a kind of ideal justification therefore requires specifying the ideal conditions and defending their connection to truth. The disagreement is deeper than whether checking beliefs for consistency is useful.

This also separates a coherence theory of truth from [coherentism about justification](deeper:06-justification/coherentism). One can endorse a role for coherence in warrant without identifying truth with coherence. The subjects interact, but the theories are not interchangeable.

### Peirce: inquiry as a constraint

Peirce connects truth to the outcome or ideal limit of sustained inquiry. Capps explains his movement from language about eventual agreement toward a counterfactual account of what inquiry would yield if continued indefinitely. Actual unanimity or an actual stopping point is not required. [@capps2023, § 1.1]

The crucial contrast is between persistence and resilience under investigation. A community can obstinately retain a belief without giving it the kind of continued testing the account invokes. Mere survival through censorship or indifference would not establish the relevant relation to inquiry. [@capps2023, § 1.1]

Peirce's criticism of correspondence is also more selective than simple rejection. Capps explains that he regards a merely nominal correspondence definition as insufficiently informative about truth's practical importance. Glanzberg notes that his account can accommodate answerability to an independent world. [@capps2023, § 1.1; @glanzberg2025, § 1.3]

Thus connecting truth to inquiry need not mean replacing reality with a vote. It raises a harder question: how should the counterfactual ideal of inquiry be specified, and why should that ideal connect to truth rather than just a kind of final agreement?

### James and Dewey: verification and practical use

James emphasizes the practical workings of ideas, their verification, and their connection with experience. Capps presents the passages that prompted the charge that he equated truth with usefulness, together with his qualifications and the interpretive difficulties. His reference to usefulness in the longer run should not be reduced to immediate comfort. [@capps2023, § 1.2]

An invented reassuring belief that a broken exit is usable could help someone feel calm while endangering their escape. Its usefulness in one respect does not establish its truth. This is the shape of a classic objection to a straightforward truth-equals-utility definition: useful falsehoods and useless truths can be imagined. [@capps2023, § 5.1]

Dewey's later vocabulary of warranted assertibility highlights what a well-conducted inquiry entitles us to judge. Capps describes both the continuity with Peirce and James and Dewey's changing way of treating truth and knowledge. The pragmatic tradition contains different accounts, not one formula repeated under three names. [@capps2023, § 1.3]

Defenders can respond that pragmatic theories elucidate the role of truth rather than supply a substitute word with the same meaning. Critics then ask whether this leaves the metaphysical question unresolved. Capps's discussion makes the disputed project visible: an objection to a strict definition does not necessarily refute an elucidation of how the concept operates. [@capps2023, § 5.1]

### Tarski: a definition for a language

Tarski's work addresses a precise formal task. Take a suitably interpreted language and define a truth predicate for its sentences. Convention T requires the definition to imply the relevant biconditionals, but writing down the schema does not yet define the predicate. [@glanzberg2025, § 2.1, 2.2]

A recursive account starts with simple sentences and specifies how truth extends through the language's construction rules. A conjunction depends on both conjuncts; a negation reverses the relevant truth condition. Reference connects terms to objects, and satisfaction connects predicates and assignments to their appropriate objects. This machinery can handle infinitely many sentences with finitely stated clauses. [@glanzberg2025, § 2.3, 2.4]

The invented door language could contain a basic sentence about an open door and another about a working alarm. It can then contain indefinitely many negations and combinations of those sentences. A recursive account handles their construction rather than listing every complete sentence separately.

The account's power should not be mistaken for an automatic metaphysical verdict. Glanzberg emphasizes Tarski's contribution to mathematical logic and the use of his tools by different philosophical projects. Their interpretation can matter as much as their formal availability. [@glanzberg2025, § 2, 4.2]

### Why the liar changes the scope

An unrestricted truth predicate combined with appropriate self-reference and ordinary logical principles generates paradox. A definition for a formal language must therefore pay attention to the language and to what its truth predicate can apply. The [liar page](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/the-liar-paradox) explains the competing responses. [@beall-etal2026, § 1.1, 2.2, 4.3.1]

This is a limit on combining principles, not evidence that every ordinary claim lacks a determinate answer. Our report about the door need not be paradoxical just because a language can also generate a troublesome self-referential sentence.

Nor can the liar be handled simply by announcing that truth is correspondence or coherence. A theory must explain how its truth talk, logic, and expressive resources avoid the contradiction or accommodate it without triviality. The issue cuts across the larger metaphysical classifications. [@beall-etal2026, § 2.2, 4.1]

### Deflationism and generalization

Deflationary theories resist a substantial underlying nature of truth. Their variations differ over meaning equivalence, disquotation, and minimal biconditionals. Glanzberg separates redundancy, minimalism, and disquotationalism rather than presenting one uniformly eliminative view. [@glanzberg2025, § 5.1, 5.2, 5.3]

Their expressive explanation is central. Suppose we want to endorse everything in a report we have not memorized. Listing its sentences is impractical. Saying that everything in the report is true generalizes over its contents. Armour-Garb and colleagues explain how the truth predicate supplies resources for such generalizations in ordinary language. [@armourgarb-etal2026, § 1.3]

Consequently, deflationism does not normally say that the word is useless. It offers an account of why the concept is useful without invoking a substantial truth property. Whether that account is adequate remains disputed. [@armourgarb-etal2026, § 1.3, 4.4]

### Can deflationism explain truth's normative role?

Truth appears to constrain assertion and belief: a well-supported but false report remains incorrect in a relevant respect. Critics ask whether an expressive account explains that constraint. Deflationists respond that the truth predicate can generalize norms stated in individual cases. [@armourgarb-etal2026, § 4.4]

For our invented report, one can say that asserting the door is open when it is closed is incorrect. To generalize over every assertion, one uses *true* or *false*. The dispute concerns whether this generalizing function suffices, rather than whether either side must deny the possibility of an incorrect assertion. [@armourgarb-etal2026, § 4.4]

This is also why deflationism should not be described as indifference to truth. An account of the concept's function is distinct from a speaker's willingness to investigate or report accurately.

### Pluralism: one role, several realizations?

Wright and Lynch develop forms of truth pluralism. Glanzberg describes Lynch's functional approach: principles about objectivity and inquiry characterize a common role that different properties could realize in different settings. The view aims to preserve unity without requiring one identical realization across every domain. [@glanzberg2025, § 4.4]

This is not simply a claim that mathematical and physical inquiries use different tests. Everyone can recognize different methods. The stronger pluralist proposal concerns what constitutes truth in the relevant domains. It therefore needs an account of the shared role and of how the proposed realizations satisfy it. [@glanzberg2025, § 4.4]

::: positions What the approaches try to explain
| Approach | Central emphasis | Question to press |
|---|---|---|
| Correspondence | A relation to worldly facts or structures. | Which resources and relation handle complex claims? |
| Coherence | A relation to a specified system. | Why that system and that relation? |
| Pragmatic | Truth's connection with inquiry and practice. | Definition, criterion, or elucidation? |
| Semantic | A formally adequate truth definition. | For which language and interpretation? |
| Deflationary | Equivalence and expressive generalization. | Do they explain the disputed roles? |
| Pluralist | A shared role with different realizations. | What unifies the realizations? |
:::
[@glanzberg2025, § 1.1.2, 1.2, 2.2, 4.4, 5.3; @capps2023, § 5.1; @armourgarb-etal2026, § 4.4]. These approaches can share tools while differing over the explanatory task.

## Beyond the chapter

### Two useful qualifications

The T-schema is not a complete argument for one theory of truth. Glanzberg explicitly notes that Convention T does not discriminate between realist and anti-realist accounts. Contemporary local relativism adds further questions about assessment parameters; the [relativism page](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/relativism) examines them. [@glanzberg2025, § 4.2]

The pragmatists should also be distinguished rather than equated with believing whatever is useful now. Capps reconstructs their different commitments and their critics. A useful objection must identify which project and which version it challenges. [@capps2023, § 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 5.1]

[Realism and anti-realism](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/realism-and-anti-realism) connects these theories to knowability. [Why truth matters](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/why-truth-matters) separates the norm of correctness from a person's desire to investigate. [Scientific realism](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/scientific-realism-and-anti-realism) asks what successful inquiry warrants about unobservables.
