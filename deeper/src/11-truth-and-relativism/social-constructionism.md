> **In short.** Calling something socially constructed leaves several questions unanswered. The target might be a classification, an object, an institution, or a human trait. Social practices might cause it or partly constitute what it is. These dependencies do not all imply unreality or arbitrary truth. A useful constructionist claim identifies the target and the mechanism.

## Re-learn

### Name what was produced

Imagine a fictional town creating a permit system for street vendors. The vocabulary, paperwork, legal status, and vendors' behavior could each be affected. Saying the permit system is constructed does not tell us which of these is under discussion.

Mallon distinguishes construction of representations, construction of facts more generally, and construction concerning human traits. He also distinguishes local claims about particular targets from global claims that every fact is socially constructed. The stronger thesis requires a stronger argument. [@mallon2026, § 1.2]

The town's classification can have a social history without every judgment using it being arbitrary. Once rules and circumstances are specified, a particular vendor either meets the fictional permit requirements or does not. Individual disbelief would not alone erase that status.

### Cause and constitution

Social activity can cause a thing to exist. It can also be necessary to the thing's being a particular kind of thing. Mallon separates causal construction from constitutive construction, while noting grounding as another approach to the noncausal relation. [@mallon2026, § 1.3]

In the example, collective work causes a physical kiosk to be built. Institutional rules constitute its status as an authorized permit office. The kiosk's materials and its authority therefore have different dependencies. An argument about the office's status is not automatically an argument about the unreality of its wood or stone.

## Beyond the chapter

### Classifications can change their subjects

Hacking's looping-effect account concerns ways classifications of people can influence responses to them and their own behavior. Mallon describes the proposed interaction between an epistemic classification and the traits it helps produce. He also notes disputes about the nature and mechanisms of these effects. [@mallon2026, § 3.2.2]

The fictional vendor category might affect who applies for permits, what work they seek, and how officials treat them. Researchers studying the category would then encounter subjects responding to the classification. The example illustrates a possible mechanism; whether such effects occur in any actual category needs evidence.

It also does not follow that every trait is caused solely by classification. A constructionist explanation can interact with other causal factors. The particular claim needs an account of how the social practice contributes, not just an assertion that language matters.

### Construction and criticism are different conclusions

The town's permit system could have been organized differently. That contingency does not alone show that the current rules are bad. Conversely, an apparently natural arrangement could deserve criticism. A historical or constitutive explanation and an evaluation of the arrangement have different premises.

Likewise, explaining how scientific classifications arose does not by itself establish that the objects classified were produced by those classifications. Mallon highlights disputes over inferences from social influences on theory production to construction of the facts represented. [@mallon2026, § 1.2]
