> **In short.** A published result needs more scrutiny than its initial analysis. Repeating calculations, collecting new data with similar methods, and testing a hypothesis in a different way check different things. Failures can expose chance findings, selective reporting, or limits in the original claim. They can also raise questions about whether the repeated procedure addressed the same target. Reform aims to make methods, decisions, and unsuccessful results visible enough for these questions to be answered.

## Re-learn

### Reproduce what, exactly?

Imagine a fictional study reporting a difference between two groups. Another researcher runs the same code on the same data and obtains the same number. That checks computational reproducibility. A new study using new participants checks whether a comparable pattern recurs. A different design addressing the same hypothesis tests its generality in another way.

Fidler and Wilcox explain that terminology varies across disciplines. They distinguish repeating procedures from obtaining similar results, and direct from conceptual replication. A study can be repeatable in procedure without reproducing its original finding. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 1]

Thus a claim that a result has been reproduced should say what was repeated and what outcome counted as success. The headline alone may leave both questions unanswered.

### The published record is selected

Suppose the imaginary research field mainly publishes positive results. Unsuccessful investigations remain unseen. A reader then encounters a record that looks more consistent than the full research history. Fidler and Wilcox discuss both publication preferences for novel work over replications and selection favoring statistically significant findings. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 2.2]

Flexible analysis can contribute too. Decisions about collecting more data, excluding observations, choosing covariates, or reporting outcomes can be influenced by whether they achieve significance. Their review describes how these practices can inflate false-positive rates and distort the published literature. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 2.3]

## Beyond the chapter

### Exploration deserves an accurate label

Looking for patterns after seeing data can be valuable for generating hypotheses. The problem is presenting those patterns as if they were predicted in advance, or assessing a selected analysis as though it were the only analysis attempted. Fidler and Wilcox discuss HARKing and the distinction between exploratory and confirmatory research. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 2.3]

Preregistration makes a date-stamped plan available before the relevant data are examined. It helps reveal which analyses were predicted and which arose later. A preregistration does not make a poor design good; its contribution is greater visibility of the inferential history. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 4.2]

Registered reports add a different safeguard. The publication decision is made from the question, methods, and planned analysis, with publication then depending on carrying out the accepted plan or adequately justifying deviations. This reduces the dependence of publication on whether the eventual result is positive. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 4.3]

### A failure needs interpretation

If the fictional difference does not recur, the assessment should consider the claim's precision, uncertainty, population, and procedure. A different estimate need not contradict a broad and uncertain original claim. Conversely, declaring every failed repetition a changed context can insulate an overly general claim from criticism. The original claim and the new comparison need to be specified.

No single reform removes every problem. Sharing code checks calculations but does not repair the sampling design. A transparent plan exposes decisions but does not guarantee that the measurements capture the target. These are reasons to connect safeguards to specific errors rather than treat openness as one universal cure.
