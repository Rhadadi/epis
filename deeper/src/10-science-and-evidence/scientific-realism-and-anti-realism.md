> **In short.** A successful theory can predict observations while also describing things we cannot directly observe. Scientific realism gives a positive epistemic interpretation to at least some of those descriptions. Anti-realist positions question one or more parts of that commitment. Constructive empiricism accepts literal theoretical language while recommending restraint about unobservables. The dispute concerns what scientific success warrants, not whether successful predictions occur.

## Re-learn

### Separate three commitments

Imagine a theory that explains an invented detector's patterns through invisible entities. One question asks whether the world exists independently of our thinking. Another asks whether the entity claims are literal statements capable of being true or false. A third asks whether the evidence warrants believing them.

Chakravartty distinguishes these metaphysical, semantic, and epistemological dimensions of realism. They can come apart. Someone might accept a mind-independent world and literal descriptions while withholding belief in the unobserved entities. Disagreement about belief does not automatically imply a denial of reality. [@chakravartty2025, § 1.2]

Constructive empiricism illustrates that combination. Van Fraassen's position treats theories literally but aims at empirical adequacy: truth about observable things and events. It permits an agnostic attitude toward claims about unobservables. This differs from traditional instrumentalism's nonliteral interpretation of theoretical descriptions. [@chakravartty2025, § 4.1]

### Why success seems to favor realism

The no-miracles argument asks what explains the striking predictive and practical success of scientific theories. A realist answer invokes their truth or approximate truth. Chakravartty describes both the attraction of this argument and objections to its explanatory and probabilistic framing. [@chakravartty2025, § 2.1]

The imaginary detector theory gains something important when it predicts a new pattern correctly. But the inference to its ontology still needs support. Was the prediction tied to the entities in question? Would an alternative account predict it too? Are the successful parts separable from less well-supported assumptions? Success is evidence to analyze, not a word that settles every commitment.

## Beyond the chapter

### A history of success and replacement

The pessimistic induction points to successful earlier theories later rejected. Laudan's version particularly challenges an automatic connection between success and the truth or reference of central unobservable commitments. Realist replies restrict the relevant theories or identify components retained through change. [@chakravartty2025, § 3.3]

Those replies make realism more selective. A claim that some well-supported aspects of a theory survive is different from a claim that the entire theory is approximately true. The anti-realist challenge also needs a defensible historical comparison: the existence of replacement does not alone establish how much or which kind of knowledge was lost. [@chakravartty2025, § 3.3]

### Observation is a philosophical distinction here

In this debate, observability often concerns what humans can perceive with unaided senses, rather than everything detectable by an instrument. Chakravartty explains that this usage differs from ordinary scientific talk of detecting proteins or particles. The distinction itself is disputed, and its relevance is especially important for positions that limit belief to the observable. [@chakravartty2025, § 1.1]

Thus a new detector does not automatically dissolve the philosophical disagreement by making an entity observable in the relevant sense. It can, however, add evidence whose implications the competing accounts must assess. A vocabulary disagreement should not substitute for that assessment.

See [underdetermination](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/underdetermination) for rivals fitting the same observations and [inference to the best explanation](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/inference-to-the-best-explanation) for the disputed explanatory step.
