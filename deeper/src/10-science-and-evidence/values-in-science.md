> **In short.** Values can affect which questions science investigates and how it handles uncertain conclusions. That influence is not identical to changing results to fit a preference. The debate about value-free science asks where values may legitimately enter and how their influence should be controlled. A key issue is the cost of mistaken acceptance or rejection. Objectivity requires making these judgments open to scrutiny rather than pretending no judgment occurs.

## Re-learn

### Choosing a question and accepting an answer

Imagine an invented environmental study deciding which pollutant to investigate. Concern for public health can guide the choice of question without deciding the answer. Once measurements begin, the investigators still need procedures that allow the favored hypothesis to fail.

Reiss and Sprenger distinguish cognitive values, such as accuracy and explanatory power, from contextual values, including social and ethical commitments. They also distinguish impartiality in assessment, neutrality of theoretical claims, and autonomy of research agendas. These are different versions of a value-free ideal, not one interchangeable demand. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 3.1]

The distinctions prevent a bad inference. If funding priorities reflect social concerns, it does not follow that every measured result is fabricated. Conversely, correct arithmetic does not show that the agenda or assessment is free from problematic influence. One needs to inspect where values affected the process.

### Inductive risk

Suppose the fictional study must recommend whether a substance is safe. Erroneously accepting safety could expose people to harm; erroneously rejecting it could withhold something beneficial. Rudner's argument gives such error consequences a role in deciding how much evidence is enough for acceptance. The literature calls the residual risk in extending evidence to a conclusion *inductive risk*. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 3.2]

Changing the acceptance threshold is different from pretending the observations are stronger than they are. The study can report its evidential uncertainty while explaining the policy judgment. Combining those two tasks silently makes the conclusion harder to assess.

## Beyond the chapter

### A contested argument, with contested boundaries

Rudner's argument faces replies. Jeffrey proposes restricting scientific assessment to evidence and probability rather than pragmatic acceptance. Levi appeals to community standards that can constrain the individual scientist's choices. Douglas emphasizes that values can also enter design, measurement, analysis, and interpretation. Reiss and Sprenger reconstruct these different positions. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 3.2]

There is a further challenge to the cognitive/contextual division itself. Longino argues that apparently cognitive virtues can carry social implications, and the choice between different virtues can shape inquiry. That dispute asks whether the classification of values is as clean as the ideal presupposes. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 3.2]

### Social criticism as a safeguard

Longino's contextual empiricism gives organized criticism a central role in objectivity. It requires avenues for criticism, shared standards, uptake, and appropriate intellectual authority. A community can become more accountable when different participants expose neglected assumptions and their effects. Diversity alone is not a substitute for the mechanisms that make criticism effective. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 5.2]

In the invented pollutant study, publishing the threshold, the measurement definitions, and the competing error costs allows others to challenge each component. That is more informative than announcing either perfect neutrality or unavoidable bias. The aim is an assessable conclusion with an assessable relation to action.
