> **In short.** Feyerabend challenges the claim that one exceptionless method governs scientific progress. Laudan challenges strong conclusions drawn from the mere possibility of alternative theories. Their criticisms concern different targets. Both make a simple story about scientific rationality harder to defend. Neither criticism, considered alone, establishes that evidence cannot distinguish good inquiry from bad inquiry.

## Re-learn

### Two challenges to an overly tidy picture

Imagine a proposed methodological rule: never continue with a theory that faces contrary observations. The rule sounds admirably strict. But a researcher could reasonably investigate an instrument fault or develop a new auxiliary hypothesis. Whether that is progress depends on what the investigation achieves.

Feyerabend's *Against Method* argues against useful, exceptionless rules governing scientific progress. Oberheim and Preston explain his historical treatment of the transition to heliocentric astronomy, including his emphasis on rhetoric, persuasion, and the substantial resources of older theories. His target is a general methodology that would constrain inquiry regardless of setting. [@oberheim-preston2025, § 5.1]

His famous permissive slogan should therefore be read in that argumentative context. Rejecting an exceptionless rule does not logically establish that every local procedure works equally well. A failure of one universal prescription is compatible with stringent standards appropriate to a particular question.

Laudan's critique of underdetermination targets a different inference. If several revisions are logically available after a failed test, it does not follow that they are equally rationally defensible. Stanford describes his insistence on separating different strengths of the thesis before drawing conclusions about science. [@stanford2023, § 2.2]

### The same example, different questions

In an invented laboratory dispute, a researcher keeps a theory despite a discrepant result. A Feyerabend-inspired challenge asks whether a rigid rule would prematurely stop productive work. A Laudan-inspired challenge asks whether the alternative repairs have equal evidential standing. One question concerns the scope of methodological prescriptions; the other concerns the strength of a claim about rational choice.

## Beyond the chapter

### Historical criticism needs historical evidence

Feyerabend argues that scientists celebrated as models of method did not always follow the restrictions philosophers later attributed to them. Oberheim and Preston also discuss his deliberately provocative presentation. To assess the argument, examine both the historical reconstruction and the particular rule it challenges. A vivid anecdote cannot by itself refute every possible account of scientific reasoning. [@oberheim-preston2025, § 5.1]

The productive response is to ask how a method should adapt to its purpose and evidence. Relaxing one restriction might expose a new phenomenon. It might also enable indefinite evasion. The subsequent record matters in distinguishing those possibilities.

### Success and truth need separate support

Laudan also figures centrally in the realism debate. Chakravartty discusses his appeal to successful past theories whose central unobservable commitments were later rejected. This challenges a simple inference from empirical success to the truth or successful reference of a theory's central claims. Realists reply with restrictions on which theories or components support the inference. [@chakravartty2025, § 3.3]

This is a further dispute, not the same argument as the critique of underdetermination. Keeping the targets separate prevents the reader from treating all skepticism about a universal method as one doctrine. It also shows why a theory's status, empirical achievements, and warranted interpretation deserve distinct questions.

For more, see [scientific realism](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/scientific-realism-and-anti-realism), [underdetermination](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/underdetermination), and [Lakatos](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/lakatos-and-research-programmes). They develop the different burdens behind evaluating methods, alternatives, and progress.
