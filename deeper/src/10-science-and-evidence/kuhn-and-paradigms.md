> **In short.** Scientific work needs shared examples of what counts as a good problem and a good solution. Kuhn called attention to how those examples organize research, rather than treating science as a list of independently tested propositions. A revolution can change the questions, the standards, and the meaning of central terms. That makes comparison difficult without making argument impossible. His account explains productive commitment as well as disruptive change, but its sharp division between normal and revolutionary science remains disputed.

## Re-learn

### Learning a practice before questioning it

Imagine two groups studying the same imaginary machine. One learns to explain it by comparing forces on its parts. The other learns to trace flows through the whole system. Their training affects which measurements they take, which breakdowns they consider interesting, and what a satisfying answer looks like.

Neither group needs to repeat all its fundamental arguments every morning. Shared training lets researchers divide work, recognize promising problems, and judge whether a proposed solution meets the discipline's expectations. Kuhn's account of normal science gives this background commitment a central role. Researchers usually try to solve puzzles within an established framework; a stubborn result is initially a problem for the researcher, rather than an immediate refutation of the framework. [@bird2025, § 2, 3]

That response can be reasonable. In the invented machine case, a failed calculation could reflect a faulty gauge, a neglected force, or an arithmetic mistake. Abandoning the entire approach after every difficulty would prevent anyone from learning how far it can go. Yet continued failure can also undermine confidence in the approach itself.

### Two meanings of paradigm

Kuhn eventually distinguished the broad *disciplinary matrix*, including shared commitments and standards, from *exemplars*: concrete problem solutions used to teach people how to proceed. The exemplar is more specific than a worldview. A worked calculation can teach the student to recognize analogous problems without supplying an explicit rule for every future application. [@bird2025, § 3]

An anomaly is a difficulty relative to those expectations. A crisis is a more serious disruption of confidence. A revolution changes central commitments and establishes a new basis for subsequent research. These terms name different situations; a single surprising measurement does not establish that a revolution has begun. [@bird2025, § 2]

### The central confusion

The absence of a decision algorithm does not mean the absence of reasons. Kuhn identified shared values such as accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness. Scientists can agree on these values while disagreeing over their interpretation and relative importance in a particular comparison. That leaves room for reasoned disagreement. [@bird2025, § 4.1]

## The full story

### The historical question behind the theory

Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* appeared in 1962. Its picture of development challenged an account on which science chiefly accumulates confirmed findings or repeatedly replaces theories through decisive refutations. Normal research, on his account, is both highly productive and strongly committed to an established way of doing things. Revolutionary change is distinctive because it can alter the standards by which earlier results were understood. [@bird2025, § 2]

This is a question about the organization of inquiry over time. An abstract account of a single inference can ask whether a hypothesis follows from the evidence. Kuhn also asks how a community comes to share the skills, questions, and examples needed to produce that evidence. Those are related questions, but answering the first does not automatically answer the second.

::: timeline Changes in Kuhn's account
- **1962:** *Structure* presents normal science, crisis, and revolutionary change.
- **The later clarification of paradigm:** Kuhn distinguishes the disciplinary matrix from exemplars.
- **Later work:** his discussion of incommensurability increasingly emphasizes the organization of kinds and the limits of translation.
:::
[@bird2025, § 2, 3, 4.4]. The vocabulary developed; it should not be treated as one unchanging doctrine.

The original book is not among the full texts available in this corpus. The account here follows Alexander Bird's encyclopedia entry, including its discussion of Kuhn's changing views and their critics. That limitation matters most where a slogan seems to settle a complicated interpretive question: the slogan should be checked against the particular period and argument being discussed. [@bird2025, § 4.3, 4.4, 7.2]

### Normal science as concentrated problem solving

In normal science, a shared paradigm gives researchers problems that they expect to have solutions. The achievement is not simply collecting miscellaneous observations. It includes improving precision, extending a theory's applications, and articulating how its resources handle difficult cases. The expectation of success helps sustain demanding work that would otherwise be abandoned too soon. [@bird2025, § 2]

Consider an invented research group whose model predicts a repeating signal. The group first estimates its frequency. Later researchers account for small departures, improve instruments, and connect the signal to other quantities. A commitment to the model makes these tasks intelligible. It tells them which residuals matter and which refinements might be productive.

Calling this work puzzle solving can sound belittling. But a puzzle need not be easy. Its defining feature in Kuhn's account is an expectation that a solution is available within the accepted framework. The difficult calculation or measurement can demand considerable originality while leaving the framework in place. [@bird2025, § 2]

There is a corresponding danger. Commitment can make a community overlook a difficulty that ought to change its assumptions. Kuhn's explanatory problem is how the same commitment can support both fruitful persistence and delayed revision. Describing its benefit does not justify every instance of resistance; describing its risk does not show that researchers should suspend all shared commitments. [@bird2025, § 2, 7.1]

### What an exemplar contributes

The broad use of *paradigm* made it hard to tell whether Kuhn meant a theory, a worldview, a method, or the entire culture of a discipline. His later distinction helps: the disciplinary matrix includes shared theoretical and methodological resources, while exemplars are particular achievements that train researchers in their use. [@bird2025, § 3]

A student's ability to solve a familiar problem is not exhausted by memorizing its conclusion. The student learns to represent the problem, choose relevant quantities, ignore distracting details, and recognize a new case as sufficiently similar. Kuhn emphasized this role for exemplars in acquiring scientific competence. [@bird2025, § 3]

In the imaginary signal project, an exemplar might be a successful analysis of one noisy recording. A student who studies it learns what counts as signal, how to represent background interference, and when a correction is warranted. A later recording will differ in many respects. The shared example supplies guidance without specifying a complete recipe.

This explains why replacing a foundational achievement can require retraining, rather than merely replacing one sentence in a textbook. It also explains why disagreement can concern examples and classifications as much as explicit principles. Two researchers might endorse the same general value of accuracy but disagree over which comparison measures it appropriately. [@bird2025, § 3, 4.1]

### From anomaly to crisis

An anomaly is intelligible only against an expectation. Something is anomalous because the existing framework does not handle it as anticipated. Normal science contains anomalies; their existence alone does not make normal science irrational or automatically bring it to an end. Crisis involves a more substantial loss of confidence, particularly when an important difficulty resists the resources that should have resolved it. [@bird2025, § 2]

It follows that one cannot identify a Kuhnian crisis simply by counting discrepant results. Their importance depends on the expectations of the community, their centrality, and the prospects for resolving them. An apparently small mismatch might concern a foundational commitment. A larger mismatch might be tolerated if the instrument or application is already known to be uncertain. [@bird2025, § 2]

An alternative also matters. In Kuhn's account, scientists do not generally discard the shared basis of their work without another framework capable of taking its place. Crisis opens a period of competing approaches and argument over what future research should become. Revolution changes commitments that normal research had treated as its starting point. [@bird2025, § 2]

Avoid reading this as a timetable. It is an account of relations among commitment, difficulty, and change. The critical question is whether particular histories really divide into these phases as cleanly as the account suggests. That question needs evidence from those histories, rather than an announcement that any recent controversy fits the pattern. [@bird2025, § 7.1]

### Why common values do not make an algorithm

Suppose our two imaginary approaches differ as follows. One predicts the signal's frequency accurately but handles changes in the machine poorly. The other is less precise now but integrates several kinds of behavior and suggests promising new measurements. A preference for accuracy favors the first; a preference for scope or fruitfulness might favor the second.

Kuhn's methodological incommensurability allows differences in how values are applied and weighted. He does not thereby eliminate common values. Scientists can share a commitment to accuracy, consistency, breadth, simplicity, and fruitful research while reaching different judgments about a candidate theory. [@bird2025, § 4.1]

::: argument The limited conclusion from disagreement
1. Shared scientific values can support different assessments of rival theories.
2. Those values need not determine one uniquely required choice by an agreed algorithm.
3. Therefore a shared algorithm need not settle every significant theory choice.
:::
[@bird2025, § 4.1]. The conclusion leaves open whether one choice is better supported and whether later evidence resolves the disagreement.

The argument does not establish that all choices are equally defensible. To reach that conclusion, one would need additional premises about the absence of stronger reasons, reliable judgment, or successful discrimination. A missing algorithm is compatible with excellent and terrible judgment, just as a lack of a universal formula for diagnosing every broken machine does not make every diagnosis equally good.

This helps distinguish a disagreement about rationality from a disagreement about certainty. A scientist might reasonably pursue a promising alternative before it dominates the evidence. Another might reasonably continue work on the established framework. Assessing pursuit and assessing settled belief are different tasks; neither requires that every researcher make the same choice at the same moment.

### Three kinds of incommensurability

The word *incommensurable* can suggest that theories cannot be compared at all. Bird's reconstruction separates methodological, perceptual, and semantic issues. Methodological incommensurability concerns standards and their application. Perceptual incommensurability concerns the influence of training and concepts on what scientists experience. Semantic incommensurability concerns changes in the meanings of terms. Each raises a different obstacle. [@bird2025, § 4.1, 4.2, 4.3]

A methodological obstacle might be disagreement over whether a theory's explanatory reach outweighs its current inaccuracy. A perceptual obstacle might be disagreement over what a display reveals. A semantic obstacle might arise when the same word belongs to different theoretical classifications. These examples are illustrations, not evidence that every scientific dispute has all three features.

Kuhn's early semantic account connected a term's meaning closely to its theoretical setting. If that setting changes substantially, one cannot assume that a sentence using an old term simply carries over unchanged. His later account places greater emphasis on related systems of classification, allowing more local translation failures rather than treating every part of a scientific language as inaccessible. [@bird2025, § 4.3, 4.4]

A failure of word-for-word translation is not identical to a failure of understanding. Someone can learn another system of concepts, explain how it is used, and investigate its successes without finding an exact substitute for each term in their original vocabulary. The relevant difficulty concerns what can be preserved in translation, rather than whether learning is impossible. [@bird2025, § 4.4]

### The realist reply: meaning and reference

Bird describes a major realist reply: terms can change meaning while still referring to the same things or properties. A changed theoretical description therefore need not make truth comparisons impossible. To show that reference changed as well, one needs a further argument about the particular terms and cases. [@bird2025, § 7.2]

Imagine two descriptions of the same imaginary object. One identifies it by an incorrect account of its composition; another gives a better explanation of that composition. The descriptions differ, but they can still concern that object. The example does not prove that every scientific term retains its reference. It shows why a change in description alone does not settle the question.

Even successful continuity of reference leaves an epistemic problem. A realist still needs reasons for thinking the newer theory describes the referent more accurately. Bird discusses externalist and naturalized replies that reject a demand for justification from a perspective wholly outside scientific inquiry. A method can yield knowledge through its reliability, even when assessing that reliability uses scientific resources. [@bird2025, § 7.2]

The disagreement thus reaches beyond vocabulary. It concerns what would count as an adequate basis for judging improvement. Requiring a completely independent standpoint is a strong requirement; rejecting it does not release scientists from testing methods and investigating their failures.

### Progress without a fixed final destination

Kuhn did not simply deny scientific progress. His account recognizes improvement in problem solving, while questioning a story in which the sequence of theories approaches one final, fully correct description of the world. He also allows losses across revolutions: a new framework can achieve major successes while leaving some earlier problems or explanatory achievements behind. [@bird2025, § 5]

An evolutionary analogy helps separate improvement from a predetermined destination. Development can involve selection among competing approaches and increased specialization without a guarantee that every change is movement toward one preassigned endpoint. The analogy is part of Kuhn's account of development, not independent evidence that scientific change obeys a biological mechanism. [@bird2025, § 5]

This raises a question for both sides. The realist must explain how scientific success supports claims about truth rather than only usefulness. Kuhn's account must explain how judgments of improved problem solving remain assessable across changing standards. Calling a transition progressive does not finish the argument about what progress consists in. [@bird2025, § 5, 7.2]

### How well does the historical pattern fit?

Bird discusses objections to the sharp division between normal and revolutionary science. Toulmin argued that revisionary changes are more frequent and less dramatic than Kuhn's picture suggests. A defense would distinguish changes within a paradigm from changes to the paradigm, but that requires a clear account of where the boundary lies. [@bird2025, § 7.1]

Another difficulty is that an achievement can be revolutionary in its effects without arising through the sequence of anomaly and crisis. Bird uses the discovery of DNA's double-helical structure to illustrate the opening of major new opportunities through an unexpected insight. Its importance does not automatically make it a revolution in Kuhn's specific sense. [@bird2025, § 7.1]

::: positions Questions that separate the positions
| View | Emphasis | What needs defending |
|---|---|---|
| Kuhn | Shared exemplars and changing standards organize research. | How normal and revolutionary change are distinguished. |
| Realist reply | Meaning change can coexist with reference and improved truth. | Why the relevant reference and improvement are established. |
| Gradualist criticism | Significant revision can occur within ordinary research. | How the alternative account fits particular histories. |
:::
[@bird2025, § 3, 7.1, 7.2]. These are questions for investigation, not labels that settle a historical case.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the stage picture leaves out

The sequence from normal science to revolution is easier to remember than the role of exemplars. But the exemplar explains how research can proceed without every relevant skill being written as a rule. Keeping that part of the theory in view makes Kuhn useful for thinking about education and scientific competence, rather than only dramatic changes. [@bird2025, § 3]

The chapter's short account also needs a qualification about incommensurability. Changes in standards, perception, and meaning are different claims. Their strongest versions need separate support. A dispute over one term does not demonstrate the absence of common measurements, and a lack of a shared decision algorithm does not demonstrate irrationality. [@bird2025, § 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4]
