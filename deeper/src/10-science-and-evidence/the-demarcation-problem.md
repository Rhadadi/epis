> **In short.** Demarcation asks how science should be distinguished from other activities. Distinguishing science from pseudoscience is one part of that question. An activity can be valuable without being scientific, and an unsuccessful study can still belong to science. A criterion must explain these differences as well as recognize scientific pretence. Applying it often needs knowledge of the disputed subject.

## Re-learn

### Which boundary is being drawn?

Imagine someone asking whether an invented story is scientific. Its purpose might be artistic exploration, with no claim to establish a fact. Calling it non-scientific says little about whether it achieves that purpose. Now imagine its author presenting it as proven medical advice. The evidential pretence introduces a different question.

Hansson distinguishes non-science, unscientific activity, and pseudoscience. Non-science is the broad category. Bad measurements or inadvertent errors can make a study unscientific in a narrower critical sense without turning it into the sustained promotion of a pseudoscientific doctrine. Keeping those categories apart prevents a boundary claim from becoming a universal ranking of human activities. [@hansson2025, § 3.1, 3.3]

There is also more than one historical demarcation project. Verificationist proposals sought to distinguish science from metaphysics. Popper's formulations concerned empirical science, other kinds of statements, and eventually pseudoscience. Hansson explains why these tasks should not be treated as identical. [@hansson2025, § 4.1, 4.2, 4.3]

### A test for a proposed test

A good criterion needs to avoid both false exclusions and false inclusions. If it requires a laboratory experiment, it risks excluding inquiry that cannot manipulate its objects. If it requires only a testable statement, a doctrine might pass that logical test while its proponents continually disregard the results.

Hansson discusses precisely this objection to falsifiability: some pseudoscientific claims have been tested and found wanting. The difficulty is not always that they prohibit no observations. It can be their continued promotion despite unfavorable evidence and their way of evading criticism. [@hansson2025, § 4.3]

## Beyond the chapter

### Why a list does not eliminate judgment

Multicriterial approaches use several characteristics, such as selective use of examples, refusal to test, disregard of contrary results, and appeals to unquestionable authority. They can recognize different ways inquiry goes wrong. But deciding which features are relevant and how much weight they deserve remains a substantive problem. [@hansson2025, § 5.1, 5.2]

Consider an invented claim about an astronomical event that cannot be repeated. Unrepeatability alone would be a poor verdict: researchers might have independent records or different instruments. The useful question is whether the claim has appropriate evidential checks, not whether it possesses every feature associated with a different field.

Hansson also criticizes the impression that demarcation can be applied without subject expertise, like reading a border marker. Deciding whether a doctrine conflicts with reliable knowledge requires understanding the knowledge and the supposed conflict. A philosopher's criterion cannot replace the work of evaluating a particular medical or physical claim. [@hansson2025, § 4, 4.2]

The lesson is to distinguish three tasks: defining a category, deciding whether a case belongs to it, and assessing a particular claim's evidential support. Those tasks inform one another, but a label should not conceal the actual reasons for accepting or rejecting the claim.
