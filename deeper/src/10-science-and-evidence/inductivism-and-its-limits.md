> **In short.** Scientific generalization involves more than collecting repeated observations. Researchers must decide what to observe, how to classify it, and which pattern deserves projection. An observation-first picture can obscure those decisions and the role of imaginative hypotheses. This does not make observation dispensable. It makes the relation between observation and reasoning the central issue.

## Re-learn

### Facts do not select their own description

Imagine recording the growth of seedlings under several conditions. The record could be classified by light, temperature, soil, species, or combinations of them. It will contain many patterns. Some may be accidental; some may reflect the way the sample was selected. A longer list does not by itself identify the explanatory classification.

Hepburn and Andersen describe inductivism as an observation-based ideal of inferring explanations, associated with Baconian and Newtonian methodological traditions. Their historical account also shows why later debates are more complicated than a choice between collecting facts and inventing theories. [@hepburn-andersen2026, § 2]

Mill's approach searches for regularities and causal circumstances. Whewell's emphasizes the contribution of ideas and the creative organization of facts into a theory. Whewell then tests that organization by bringing further phenomena under it. Both approaches give observations and reasoning work to do, even though they differ over how that work is understood. [@hepburn-andersen2026, § 2]

In the invented seedling project, a proposed mechanism might suggest a measurement no one had initially recorded. The hypothesis has helped produce relevant observations. Those observations can in turn expose its failure. It would be misleading to describe that interaction as either facts speaking unaided or imagination operating without constraint.

### The scope of the limit

The philosophical problem is not that every generalization is equally unreliable. It is that a universal conclusion or a claim about unobserved cases goes beyond the observed record. [Hume's problem](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/humes-problem-of-induction) concerns the justification of that step; [Goodman's riddle](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/goodmans-new-riddle-of-induction) asks which classifications support the right projection.

## Beyond the chapter

### Discovery and testing

Popper rejects the claim that induction supplies a uniquely scientific route to theory. His account begins with problems, allows creative conjecture, and emphasizes attempts to test the conjecture. That is a position about scientific method, rather than an observation that scientists never recognize recurring patterns. [@thornton2026, § 3, 5]

A useful distinction is between how a candidate is found and why it is accepted. A hunch can produce a testable idea. An elaborate derivation can produce an incorrect one. Once the candidate is specified, its origin cannot substitute for the evidential comparison. Yet understanding the discovery process can matter when assessing which alternatives were sought and which observations were selected.

Hepburn and Andersen warn against neglecting Whewell's attention to discovery by treating him solely as a predecessor of hypothesis testing. The activity of conceptualizing the phenomena is part of the historical debate, not a disposable preliminary step. [@hepburn-andersen2026, § 2]

### What replacing the caricature achieves

The productive question is how different forms of reasoning cooperate: observation helps constrain a model, a model identifies measurements, and a discriminating test compares its consequences with those of rivals. That picture does not solve the general problem of induction, but it identifies more accurately what particular scientific claims require.
