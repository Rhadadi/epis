> **In short.** A test usually depends on a theory together with assumptions about the instrument, the sample, and the setting. If its prediction fails, at least one part of that package needs reconsideration. Logic alone does not say which part. Duhem and Quine develop this holist difficulty at different scales. The practical task is to diagnose the failure using further evidence and defensible standards of revision.

## Re-learn

### Test the conjunction

Imagine a model predicting that a sensor reads ten units. The prediction uses the model, a calibration rule, and an assumption about the power supply. A reading of seven could reflect a problem in any of those components. Repeating that the model predicted ten does not isolate the culprit.

Stanford's reconstruction of Duhem explains why a physical test cannot simply isolate a theoretical hypothesis from its auxiliary assumptions. The observation conflicts with a package. A researcher may investigate different parts of the package rather than automatically discarding its most prominent theory. [@stanford2023, § 2.1]

::: argument The logical limit of the test
1. If the theory and auxiliaries are correct, the reading is ten.
2. The accepted reading is not ten.
3. The theory and auxiliaries are not all correct.
:::
[@stanford2023, § 2.1]. The conclusion does not identify one particular false component.

Independent calibration could support the instrument assumptions. A different power measurement could test the supply assumption. A new experimental arrangement could challenge the main theory without using the same sensor. The diagnosis draws on these additional relations of support; it is not delivered by the original deduction alone.

### Why two names do not mean one doctrine

Duhem's argument concerns the testing of physical theory. Quine gives holism a broader scope, treating experience as bearing on a network of beliefs that can be revised in different places. Stanford explains the distinction and warns against letting the combined label erase it. [@stanford2023, § 2.1]

## Beyond the chapter

### Available repairs need not be equally reasonable

The imaginary sensor model could be rescued by stipulating an exception for that precise reading. That makes the revised package compatible with the report. It does not give the exception independent support or explain its occurrence. Compatibility, explanatory adequacy, and equal evidential support are different achievements. [@stanford2023, § 2.2]

Laudan's challenge concerns this difference. A claim about what revisions are logically or psychologically possible is weaker than a claim that all such revisions are rationally defensible. His criticism targets inferences from the weaker thesis to dramatic conclusions about scientific irrationality. Stanford also explains why Quine's naturalistic account makes the dispute more complicated than simply invoking an extra principle of good reasoning. [@stanford2023, § 2.2]

The result is a diagnostic problem, not permission to preserve any belief regardless of evidence. One should ask whether the repaired package explains the failure, survives independent tests, and does so without creating larger difficulties elsewhere. These questions make the response accountable to more than its ability to avoid one contradiction.

### What the chapter's formulation can obscure

The problem concerns both background theory and accepted reports. Declaring an instrument reading beyond question would hide one of the possible failure points. Yet saying reports are revisable does not justify discarding an inconvenient report without evidence. Revisability and arbitrary rejection are different things.

[Ad hoc hypotheses](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/ad-hoc-hypotheses) asks when a repair has earned support.
