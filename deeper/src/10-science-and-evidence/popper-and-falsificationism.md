> **In short.** A theory has empirical content when it rules out some possible observations. Popper makes attempts to expose such failures central to science. Surviving demanding tests is corroboration, not a proof of truth. In practice, deciding that a test really conflicts with a theory requires judgments about observations and auxiliaries. Falsifiability is therefore distinct from a rule to discard a theory after any apparent mismatch.

## Re-learn

### A prohibition creates a test

Imagine a proposed law that every sample of an invented material expands when heated under specified conditions. A genuine sample that does not expand under those conditions conflicts with the law. A hundred expanding samples do not logically establish that every possible sample expands. The asymmetry between universal verification and a counter-instance is central to Popper's account. [@thornton2026, § 3]

Now imagine a competing claim saying the material expands unless an invisible, undetectable influence prevents it. If that influence is invoked whenever expansion fails, the claim may accommodate any result. Ask what observation it excludes before calling its repeated success evidentially impressive.

Popper emphasizes risky tests and provisional retention of theories that survive them. His concept of corroboration records how a theory has withstood testing; it should not be read as a posterior probability that the theory is true. [@thornton2026, § 3; @hepburn-andersen2026, § 3.3]

### The practical decision is harder

Did the apparent counterexample involve the specified conditions? Was the sample authentic? Was the instrument accurate? Thornton stresses that Popper distinguished the simple logic from applied methodology and allowed that a single conflicting report is not enough for methodological falsification. [@thornton2026, § 4]

The accepted observation statements used in a test are themselves open to further testing. Inquiry must stop somewhere for practical purposes, but that provisional stopping point is not an incorrigible foundation. Popper's critical attitude applies to the reports as well as the theory. [@thornton2026, § 4]

## Beyond the chapter

### Testability is not the same as good testing

A statement can be logically incompatible with a possible observation even when its proponents avoid every serious attempt to check it. Conversely, some doctrines commonly classed as pseudoscientific have been tested and refuted. Hansson explains why a criterion of logical falsifiability alone does not capture their continued disregard of evidence. [@hansson2025, § 4.3]

This requires distinguishing a statement's content from a community's conduct. Asking what would count against a hypothesis is a useful first question. Asking whether those outcomes are sought, reliably detected, and taken seriously is a second question. A polished answer to the first is not evidence of the second.

### Revising an auxiliary can be progress

Not every response to a mismatch is an evasion. Hepburn and Andersen describe Popper's later recognition that modifications can generate independently testable insights, using the planetary case associated with Uranus and Neptune. What matters is whether the response increases opportunities for testing or merely protects the theory from them. [@hepburn-andersen2026, § 3.3]

The chapter's contrast between falsification and confirmation should therefore avoid treating every repair as forbidden. It also should not imply that probabilistic inference has been eliminated from all scientific assessment. Popper's account is a substantive philosophical proposal, with competitors and objections.

See [the Duhem–Quine problem](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-duhem-quine-problem) for diagnosing a failed package, [ad hoc hypotheses](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/ad-hoc-hypotheses) for evaluating repairs, and [Lakatos](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/lakatos-and-research-programmes) for appraisal across sequences of theories.
