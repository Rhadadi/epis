> **In short.** An observation depends on choices about what to notice, measure, and describe. Scientific concepts and expectations help organize those choices. They can also lead researchers to miss or misinterpret evidence. That influence does not make correction or comparison impossible. The question is whether a particular dependence helps reveal the phenomenon or insulates a theory from criticism.

## Re-learn

### Several kinds of dependence

Imagine viewing an invented detector display. You can see a line without knowing what it measures. To call its movement evidence of a particular particle, you need an account of the apparatus. That conceptual dependence differs from misreading the line because you expect it to be there.

Boyd and Bogen distinguish issues involving perception, assumptions used in testing, and the conceptual organization of evidence. The broad label *theory-laden* should not conceal these different questions. Dependence on an instrument model is not automatically the same as a perceptual illusion or an uncorrectable bias. [@boyd-bogen2026, § 3.1, 3.2]

Their historical example of Worthington's falling drops shows a correction. His early drawings emphasized symmetrical splashes; later photography revealed irregular forms and led him to reconsider what he had recorded. The case illustrates how expectations can affect attention while a changed recording practice exposes the problem. [@boyd-bogen2026, § 3.1]

### Shared observations amid disagreement

Rival theoretical commitments need not prevent agreement on every measurement. Boyd and Bogen describe Priestley and Lavoisier reporting comparable water levels despite interpreting the gases differently. They also describe laboratory disputes resolved through results that the parties could obtain and interpret in common. [@boyd-bogen2026, § 3.1]

The useful distinction is between a shared record and a shared interpretation. Investigators may agree that a level changed while disagreeing about the substance responsible. That common ground can constrain the dispute even when it does not settle it.

## Beyond the chapter

### Circularity must be demonstrated

Using an assumption associated with the theory under test can sound circular. But the relevant question is whether the test outcome is made favorable by that assumption. Boyd and Bogen discuss the Michelson–Morley case to show why the dependence of experimental design on theoretical resources need not make a test incapable of challenging them. [@boyd-bogen2026, § 3.2]

In the invented detector case, calibration might rely on a theory that is also being tested. The assessment should ask which parts of the calibration matter, whether rivals give different relevant calibrations, and whether independent checks support the result. Simply observing that some theory was used gives too little information for a verdict.

### Selection can matter as much as seeing

Training also determines which quantities researchers attend to. Boyd and Bogen discuss Kuhn's pendulum illustration: different approaches could make different measurements salient. They caution that the imagined Aristotelian comparison should be treated as a thought experiment absent the relevant experimental records. [@boyd-bogen2026, § 3.1]

This matters for reading scientific accounts. A discussion of what researchers *might* notice is not a report of what a historical group actually measured. The evidence for those two claims differs. Likewise, an account of perceptual influence does not establish that all scientific communities are separated by unbridgeable perceptual worlds.

The chapter's formulation should therefore preserve the possibility of correction. New instruments, repeated exposure, shared procedures, and explicit comparison can expose the influence of expectations in particular cases. Boyd and Bogen reject the inference that such influence is always inevitable or irremediable. [@boyd-bogen2026, § 3.1]

See [Kuhn](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/kuhn-and-paradigms) for the wider claims about perception and meaning, and [the Duhem–Quine problem](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-duhem-quine-problem) for the package of assumptions involved in testing.
