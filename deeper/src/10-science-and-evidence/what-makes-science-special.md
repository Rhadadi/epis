> **In short.** Science combines ways of asking questions, producing evidence, and correcting mistakes. Its methods vary with the subject and the problem. A controlled experiment, a historical reconstruction, and a mathematical model need different safeguards. What matters is whether those safeguards make the conclusion answerable to evidence and criticism. The name of a method cannot replace an explanation of why it works here.

## Re-learn

### A method has a job to do

Imagine an invented question about why a bridge vibrates. One investigator records its movement. Another models its structure. A third changes a component in a safe miniature. These activities supply different information: a pattern, a proposed explanation, and a test of a change. None is useful merely because it resembles a familiar classroom sequence.

Hepburn and Andersen distinguish scientific methods from aims, products, and the standards used to justify methods. Producing predictions is an aim or product. Measuring, modeling, and testing are activities used to achieve it. Explaining why a particular measurement or test deserves confidence is a further methodological task. [@hepburn-andersen2026, Preamble]

The distinction helps evaluate a claim about a scientific result. First identify the result: perhaps a repeatable pattern rather than an explanation. Then identify how it was obtained. Finally ask which alternatives the procedure excludes and which errors it leaves possible. A clear description of the method makes that assessment possible; a badge of scientific status does not perform it.

### Diversity with standards

The philosophical search for one universal method has increasingly given way to attention to scientific practices: experiments, models, statistics, instruments, collaboration, and communication. This is a shift in emphasis, not an abandonment of questions about reliable inquiry. [@hepburn-andersen2026, § 1]

In the bridge example, no ethical full-scale experiment may be available. Modeling and observation then require validation appropriate to them. A miniature could help, but its relevance depends on which features of the larger structure it preserves. Different methods can compensate for different limitations, provided the connection to the target question is explained.

## Beyond the chapter

### Correction is also an institutional achievement

Individual care is not enough when findings are selected and circulated through institutions. The literature on reproducibility connects publication bias and flexible analysis to misleading bodies of evidence. Those problems can arise even when a published calculation is performed correctly: the record of published results may be a selected subset of the investigations conducted. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 2.2, 2.3]

Longino's account of objectivity gives social criticism a constructive role. Reiss and Sprenger describe requirements including opportunities for criticism, shared standards, uptake, and intellectual authority among qualified participants. The question is whether criticism can actually change inquiry, rather than whether a community merely praises openness. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 5.2]

This adds a useful distinction between having safeguards and using them. A journal, conference, or technical vocabulary can imitate the surface of science. Hansson discusses such mimicry in pseudoscience. To judge reliability, inspect the evidential and critical practices behind the surface. [@hansson2025, § 3.2]

### What the short version should not imply

Scientific knowledge can be fallible without all scientific claims being equally uncertain. Nor does methodological variety imply that any procedure is adequate. The adequacy of a procedure must be argued in relation to its purpose and setting. Hepburn and Andersen's pluralism preserves that attention to practice. [@hepburn-andersen2026, Preamble, § 1]

The wider question of scientific status belongs to [demarcation](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-demarcation-problem). [Causal inference](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/causation-and-causal-inference) shows how methods answer different questions, while [replication](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-replication-crisis) explains why checking a result requires more than repeating its headline.
