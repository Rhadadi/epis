> **In short.** Agreement among relevant experts can be evidence for someone who cannot assess every underlying result. Its force depends on how the agreement formed and what exactly is agreed upon. Repeated reports of one source are not independent confirmations. Critical institutions and diverse investigations can make agreement more informative. A consensus is therefore a reason to examine the evidential process, not a guarantee of truth.

## Re-learn

### Specify the proposition

Imagine a fictional expert panel agreeing that a measurement is reliable, while disagreeing over the mechanism that produces it. A headline saying that the experts agree about the theory would overstate their agreement. The proposition under consensus might be a narrow result, a range of values, or a claim about relative support.

For a reader outside the field, this agreement matters as testimony. The reader can have evidence about the witnesses' expertise and practices without being able to repeat all their specialist work. [Testimony](deeper:07-sources-of-knowledge/testimony) explains that form of epistemic dependence and its limits.

Counting voices is insufficient when the voices share their evidence. Imagine ten reports copying one unverified estimate. They provide ten opportunities to encounter the estimate, not ten independently conducted measurements. The reader needs to identify whether the agreement reflects several methods and investigations or one source repeatedly transmitted.

### Agreement can be produced in different ways

Formal models in social epistemology examine how scientists learn from neighbors and their own evidence. O'Connor and colleagues describe models in which communities converge on the better theory, but also models in which misleading early evidence leads to premature settlement on a poorer one. Communication structure affects those outcomes. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.3]

These are models with specified assumptions, not direct measurements of how reliable every real consensus is. Their value is to identify a possibility and a mechanism: agreement can arise through evidence-sensitive learning while still inheriting misleading evidence from the network.

## Beyond the chapter

### The role of criticism and independence

Longino's account asks whether a community permits transformative criticism through appropriate institutions, shared standards, uptake, and intellectual authority among qualified participants. These features help distinguish an agreement that survives challenges from one protected by excluding challengers. [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 5.2]

The network literature also shows trade-offs. Some modeled structures that slow consensus preserve diversity of investigation and improve the eventual outcome. More communication is not automatically better under every modeled condition. O'Connor and colleagues emphasize how the same networks can spread useful evidence or misleading early results. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.3]

That does not establish a general reason to prefer the minority. Minority status does not guarantee independent evidence, just as majority status does not guarantee adequate criticism. Evaluate the evidence and the process instead of inferring reliability from either headcount alone.

### Incentives are part of the process

Credit economy models examine how rewards for priority can encourage sharing and productive division of work, but also discourage replication or favor rushed results. O'Connor and colleagues survey both sides. A community's incentive structure can therefore affect which findings enter the evidence on which agreement rests. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.2]

In the invented panel case, ask whether the consensus concerns replicated measurements, whether the dissent challenges that result or a different policy claim, and whether the panel communicates its uncertainty. Those distinctions make deference more precise without requiring the reader to become an expert in every technical detail.

See [replication](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-replication-crisis) for evidence that survives repetition and the [experts and novices section](12-social-epistemology.md#experts-and-novices) for assessing expertise. Consensus about evidence should also be distinguished from agreement about the [values](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/values-in-science) governing action.
