> **In short.** A crux is a consequential point of dependence in an assessment. Ask what would change a person's conclusion, in which direction, and why. A disputed point is not a crux merely because it is vivid or easy to test. There may be several necessary changes, or no single shared crux. Locate the dependence before spending effort on another round of evidence.

## Re-learn

### Test the counterfactual

Imagine two managers discussing whether to extend a shop's hours. One supports the change because they expect additional income. The other opposes it because they expect staffing costs to exceed that income. If a credible estimate reversed those expectations and each manager's recommendation changed, the financial comparison would be a shared point of dependence.

Make the counterfactual specific. Would a small surplus suffice, or is a substantial margin needed? Does the supporter also require that staff can choose their shifts? Would the opponent still reject the change because late work disrupts transport? These qualifications determine what a financial estimate could settle.

Mapping implicit premises helps expose such dependence. Groarke explains how an argument's support can fail when a consequential unstated premise is rejected. The crux question applies that insight to deciding which uncertainty merits attention; the managers are an invented example. [@groarke2026, § 3.1]

### A shared question needs shared consequences

Both managers may agree to investigate staffing cost while assigning it different roles. One treats any loss as decisive; the other accepts a modest loss to provide a valued community service. They share a measurement question without sharing the criterion for a recommendation.

Record both the factual proposition and its inferential role. Otherwise, a successful investigation can end in apparently inexplicable continued disagreement. The missing connection may be a value premise, not refusal to acknowledge the figures. See [classifying disagreement](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-2-identify-the-kind-of-disagreement).

## Beyond the chapter

### Some conclusions have several supports

If someone objects on grounds of cost, transport and safety independently, changing cost alone may leave their conclusion intact. A crux can therefore be a combination of propositions rather than one isolated fact. Groarke's distinction between linked and convergent support explains why defeating one route may leave another available. [@groarke2026, § 3.2]

Ask which remaining consideration would still support the conclusion after the proposed change. This is not a demand to abandon it prematurely. It distinguishes a decisive test from a partial contribution, allowing both to be reported accurately.

### Make room for uncertain answers

A person may be unable to name exactly what would change their mind because they do not yet understand the evidence or their own weighting. That difficulty does not by itself prove that the belief is immune to evidence. Help specify plausible alternatives and inspect what each would imply.

Hitchcock describes inquiry as a process that can redefine its question and repeat components. An initially vague crux can become clearer through that process. He also treats maintaining focus and revising unproductive strategies as internal dispositions of critical thinking. [@hitchcock2024, § 5, 8.2]

The practical payoff is a better next question: commission a staffing estimate, inspect transport options, or clarify the value standard. Prefer work whose possible outcomes could alter the assessment. If no available investigation can do that now, report the remaining uncertainty and decide whether to pause or act under it.
