> **In short.** A disagreement can contain several questions needing different kinds of answer. Separate what happened, what caused it, how to describe it, and what to do. Evidence can settle a measurement without settling a value judgment. Definitions can clarify a claim without deciding whether a policy is justified. Classification helps when it identifies the work still needed, rather than forcing the whole exchange into one box.

## Re-learn

### Decompose a practical dispute

Imagine a school considering later opening hours. One person predicts improved attendance; another objects that the change is unfair to families. They might agree about the predicted average and disagree about who bears the cost. Alternatively, their apparent value disagreement may conceal different predictions about transport and care arrangements.

Write the components separately. What are the existing attendance patterns? Would changing hours affect them? Which families would face new difficulties? How should those difficulties be weighed against benefits? What arrangements are feasible? These questions are connected, but an answer to one does not automatically supply the others.

Hitchcock includes dividing a problem into manageable subproblems among inquiry's possible components. Groarke's discussion of implicit premises shows why exposing the bridge between a practical reason and a recommendation matters. The division here is a worked conceptual example, rather than a classification attributed verbatim to either author. [@hitchcock2024, § 5; @groarke2026, § 3.1]

### Remove a disputed word carefully

If the disagreement centres on whether the timetable is *flexible*, ask what arrangements each person means. One may mean variable opening hours; another may mean individual choice. Replacing the word with those descriptions can reveal that both accept the same facts while using a label differently.

It can also reveal a substantive disagreement about which arrangement serves the school's purposes. A definition does not settle that question. See [verbal disputes](deeper:04-language-concepts-and-definitions/verbal-disputes) for cases where clarification dissolves part of a dispute while leaving another part intact.

## Beyond the chapter

### Match the test to the question

A trial can test effects on attendance under specified conditions. It cannot alone establish how much inconvenience a family should be expected to bear. A moral principle can explain a concern about fairness. It cannot alone establish the size of an attendance effect.

Practical arguments therefore need both evidential and evaluative support. If an inference depends on a goal or standard that a participant rejects, more measurements of a different premise may not advance the dispute. Groarke's account illustrates how rejecting an implicit premise can remove an argument's support for its practical conclusion. [@groarke2026, § 3.1]

There may also be disagreement about the accepted methods of inquiry. One person trusts a comparison school; another doubts that its circumstances match. That is an evidential dispute with assumptions to inspect, not automatically a conflict of ultimate worldviews.

### Do not presume a single root

The school proposal may have several independent obstacles: uncertainty about attendance, a cost concern, and a rule governing decision authority. Resolving one can leave the others in place. Record which components are agreed, which remain disputed, and how each affects the recommendation.

Nor must every practical decision wait for perfect factual agreement. Participants can evaluate options under several plausible scenarios and ask whether one remains preferable across them. That is a conditional argument whose assumptions should remain visible.

[Stasis theory](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/stasis-theory) supplies a further organising lens, while [finding the crux](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-6-find-the-crux) asks which unresolved component could materially change a conclusion. Classification earns its place by identifying an appropriate next inquiry.
