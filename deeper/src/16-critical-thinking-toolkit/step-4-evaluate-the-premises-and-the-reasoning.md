> **In short.** Assess the reasons and the inference separately. A valid form cannot rescue unsupported premises, while true premises need not support the proposed conclusion. Non-deductive reasoning should be judged by the strength it claims, rather than by a demand for certainty. Look for the specific bridge between evidence and conclusion. Then ask whether the argument survives its strongest relevant objection.

## Re-learn

### Three questions about reasons

Imagine a firm proposing a maintenance contract because the supplier has a glossy website, two satisfied clients, and a low quoted price. The website may be genuine without bearing much on performance. The clients may offer relevant testimony without representing the full customer population. The price may matter while excluding consequential extra charges.

Groarke explains acceptability, relevance and sufficiency as distinct standards used in informal logic. A premise should be credible or otherwise reasonable to accept, contribute support to this conclusion, and combine with the other reasons to warrant the conclusion's strength. The invented contract illustrates why passing one test does not imply passing the others. [@groarke2026, § 4.1]

Ask what each reason establishes. A quotation establishes an advertised price under stated terms; it does not establish lifetime cost. A client's report supplies evidence about that client's experience; it does not automatically establish every client's likely result. Make the scope match.

### Identify the intended inference

If the contract argument claims deductive certainty, ask whether its premises could be true while its conclusion is false. If it predicts likely performance, examine how the sample, background assumptions and alternatives support that prediction. If it recommends a purchase, make the goals, feasible alternatives and acceptable risks explicit.

Groarke distinguishes deductive standards from broader forms of inductive, abductive and accumulated support. The account does not require every real-life argument to become a proof with an unavoidable conclusion. See [validity and soundness](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/validity-and-soundness) and [strength and cogency](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/strength-and-cogency). [@groarke2026, § 4.1]

## Beyond the chapter

### Use critical questions constructively

Argument schemes pair recurring forms with questions about appropriate use. Groarke presents expert opinion as one example, asking about relevant competence, trustworthiness, the actual assertion, other experts and evidential support. These questions identify where a plausible inference can fail. [@groarke2026, § 4.4]

For the contract, an engineer's approval matters differently according to whether they examined technical specifications, financial terms, or neither. A reasonable objection specifies the missing competence or evidence. It need not dismiss every use of testimony as an authority fallacy.

Similarly, an analogy to another contract needs relevant similarities. If the services, scale and penalty clauses differ, success elsewhere may provide only weak support here. The point is to explain which difference changes the inference, rather than reject analogy as inherently unscientific.

### Distinguish failed support from contrary evidence

Discovering hidden charges may supply positive evidence against the purchase. Discovering that the website is uninformative merely removes one proposed reason. These findings can lead to different conclusions: reject, investigate further, or rely on remaining grounds.

Groarke's fallacy-theory discussion warns that traditional patterns can have reasonable instances and that an error catalogue is incomplete as a theory of good argument. Use a fallacy diagnosis to explain a concrete defect, then reassess the argument as a whole. [@groarke2026, § 4.2]

If a repaired argument survives the original objection, record that change. If its conclusion must become narrower, state the narrower conclusion. See [the fallacy fallacy](deeper:15-fallacies/the-fallacy-fallacy) for why exposing one failure does not settle the underlying claim.
