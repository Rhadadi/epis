> **In short.** A razor is useful when it makes a reasoning task cheaper without concealing the assumption that makes it work. Simplicity can guide the search for explanations while leaving evidential comparisons open. An unsupported assertion can be set aside without proving it false. Asking how a claim could be tested identifies a possible route to correction. Treat each maxim as a question with conditions, rather than as a verdict.

## Re-learn

### Ask what simplicity means

Imagine two accounts of missing inventory. One proposes a recording error; another proposes a coordinated theft. The first may require fewer independent assumptions. But if access logs, altered records and recovered items favour the second, a preference for simplicity cannot simply erase them.

Baker distinguishes syntactic simplicity, concerning principles or formulation, from ontological parsimony, concerning kinds of entities postulated. Reducing one can increase the other. He also distinguishes using simplicity to guide a working theory from treating it as a reason for belief. These distinctions make Ockham-style reasoning more precise than counting the sentences in an explanation. [@baker2022, Preamble, § 1]

In the invented inventory case, specify what makes one explanation simpler and which observations both explain. An account that ignores consequential evidence has not earned a simplicity tie-breaker. A short story can contain a large unexplained coincidence.

### Setting aside is different from refuting

A maxim about dismissing unsupported claims is a reminder that a claimant needs to supply support. It does not turn a missing argument into positive evidence of falsity. The IEP account of misplaced burden identifies the defect in demanding disproof instead of supporting an assertion. [@fallacy-iep, Misplaced Burden of Proof]

For the inventory, refusing to accept an unsupported accusation of theft is different from asserting that theft did not occur. Investigate where there is a consequential reason to do so, and state what the existing evidence supports. See [burden of proof](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/burden-of-proof).

## Beyond the chapter

### A shortcut needs environmental fit

Suppose a shop initially checks for ordinary recording errors because those checks are cheap and often informative. That is a search policy. It becomes defective if staff continue excluding theft after evidence gives them reason to investigate it. The cost-effective first step need not remain the final explanation.

Wheeler's discussion of bounded rationality examines how simplifying procedures depend on the environment and the task's costs. A useful heuristic must be assessed against its job; reducing effort alone does not establish that it reaches adequate conclusions. [@wheeler2024, § 3.1, 5.2]

Likewise, a memorable rule about assuming incompetence before malice should prompt consideration of alternatives, rather than certify anybody's motives. Different possibilities may predict overlapping observations. Compare their support and identify what additional evidence would distinguish them.

### A test is not a complete theory of worth

Asking what would count against an empirical hypothesis is valuable. An answer supplies a possible evidential constraint. It does not establish that the proposed test is reliable, that every auxiliary assumption holds, or that the hypothesis has already survived it.

Nor does lack of an experiment make every question pointless. A mathematical claim needs proof of an appropriate kind; a proposed fairness principle needs reasons about its implications and application. Matching the standard to the question is more informative than applying an empirical slogan to all inquiry.

Baker's distinction between epistemic and methodological uses of simplicity illustrates the wider lesson: the same brief maxim can direct different activities. Identify which activity you intend and what exceptions its justification permits. Then connect it to [evaluating reasoning](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-4-evaluate-the-premises-and-the-reasoning).
