> **In short.** A conclusion should say what the evidence supports and how far that support reaches. Separate confidence in a proposition from a decision about action. Suspension is a legitimate result when alternatives remain unresolved. Numbers can clarify uncertainty when their meaning and resolution conditions are stated. Record what could change the assessment and what still depends on a disputed assumption.

## Re-learn

### State the supported scope

Imagine a committee assessing a supplier after a successful small trial. A careful conclusion might say that the supplier met the specified requirements for this trial, while performance at a larger scale remains uncertain. Claiming general reliability adds a prediction needing further support.

Likewise, mixed evidence need not compel a verdict that both positions are equally likely. Explain which evidence bears on which alternative and where the balance remains unclear. Hitchcock discusses suspension as a missing possibility in overly binary accounts of inquiry and includes it among critical-thinking dispositions. [@hitchcock2024, § 5, 8.1]

The practical endpoint can be a provisional judgment. Include the conditions under which it holds, such as the size of the order and the delivery deadline. That gives a later reader a way to assess whether new circumstances fall within its scope.

### Confidence is not a decision rule by itself

Suppose, in a constructed example, you assign probability 0.7 that a delivery arrives tomorrow. Acting on that forecast still depends on the costs of preparing unnecessarily, being unprepared, or waiting for confirmation. The same probability can support different actions for different stakes.

Hitchcock distinguishes judging from deciding within the process of critical thinking. The distinction helps prevent a need to act from being mistaken for stronger evidence that a proposition is true. See [practical reasons for belief](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/practical-reasons-for-belief). [@hitchcock2024, § 6]

## Beyond the chapter

### Make a probability assessable

A forecast needs an event, a deadline and a resolution rule. Delivery might mean arrival at the depot, receipt at the building, or acceptance after inspection. A probability without that meaning cannot be compared fairly with the eventual outcome.

A single outcome does not establish calibration. If the delivery fails, probability 0.7 had already allowed a 0.3 chance of failure. Collections of predictions and scoring rules provide more informative assessments. [Calibration and scoring](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/calibration-and-scoring-rules) explains the mathematics. Mellers and colleagues' forecasting study describes explicit resolution and Brier scoring in its tournament; that design is an example of a specified assessment, not a guarantee for every numerical opinion. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF pp. 4–5]

Where a rough number is warranted, distinguish it from a precise statistical estimate. Where only a qualitative judgment is supportable, specify what *likely* means well enough for the decision at hand rather than inventing spurious precision.

### Record the route back

Write down the evidence that most affects the judgment and the assumption on which it depends. For the supplier, a failed larger trial might weaken the extrapolation while leaving the original small-trial report true. New evidence can alter different parts of the assessment differently.

Hitchcock's repeatable, potentially spiral account of inquiry makes such revision part of the process. A conclusion need not be a permanent commitment. It is a report of the best supported assessment available under stated conditions. [@hitchcock2024, § 5]

Keep unresolved matters consequential and specific. Saying merely that anything could happen obscures what the evidence has established. Saying that nothing could change the decision obscures the uncertainties it still contains.
