> **In short.** A map shows which claims support which conclusions. Separate the claims from the connections between them. Mark assumptions you supply, and distinguish reasons that work together from independent routes of support. An objection matters according to what it attacks. A clear map can expose a gap without proving that the conclusion is false.

## Re-learn

### Show the support relationship

Imagine an archive deciding whether to move records to a new building. One argument appeals to damp in the existing store and a promise that the new building is dry. A different reason concerns the existing lease expiring. Writing all three facts in a row conceals their different roles.

::: argument One route to a qualified recommendation
1. Damp threatens the records in the current store.
2. The new store would adequately protect them from damp.
3. Moving would be a feasible way to reduce that threat, with acceptable costs and other risks.
4. Therefore, protection from damp gives a reason to move.
:::

This is an invented reconstruction. The third premise exposes assumptions that the first two alone leave open. The conclusion supplies a reason, rather than claiming that moving is automatically best overall.

Groarke's account of standardisation begins with premises, conclusions and inferences, and its treatment of implicit premises explains why unstated bridges need evaluation too. Use different labels for a speaker's actual words and your reconstructed assumptions. [@groarke2026, § 3, 3.1]

### Linked and convergent reasons

The first two damp premises need to work together: a threat at the old store does not establish that this destination improves matters, and a dry destination need not establish a need to move. The lease deadline may provide a separate reason to find another location, even if the damp problem is repaired.

Groarke's mapping discussion distinguishes linked premises that support through a combined inference from convergent premises that provide separate support. A map should show that difference rather than treating every arrow as another independent piece of evidence. [@groarke2026, § 3.2]

## Beyond the chapter

### Place objections accurately

Suppose a critic finds that the new building sometimes floods. That attacks premise 2 and the damp route. It does not answer the lease problem. Conversely, renewing the lease removes that reason without making the existing damp harmless.

An objection to moving costs targets a further evaluative premise. It might weaken an overall recommendation while leaving the fact that moving reduces damp exposure intact. Record the conclusion whose support has changed, rather than treating any defeated premise as a refutation of every claim in the discussion.

Subarguments can clarify these dependencies. A survey supports the claim about damp; a maintenance report supports the claim about the destination; a budget supports feasibility. Each report may require its own evidential assessment. More boxes do not make the premises better, but they can expose where support is missing.

### Logical separation is not informational independence

Two reasons may appear on separate branches while depending on the same report. If the damp survey and the destination assessment were copied from one contractor's unverified advertisement, their presentation as distinct premises would not create two independent investigations.

Keep provenance beside the map: which source supports each claim, and what the source actually measured. Groarke's component-table method includes sources as well as argumentative roles. That makes it easier to inspect whether apparent support comes from distinct observations or repeated reporting. [@groarke2026, § 3.2]

A map is a reconstruction open to revision. Ask whether it captures the conclusion's scope and whether a speaker accepts the implicit premises assigned to them. If more than one reading is plausible, assess the consequential alternatives. See [understanding before evaluation](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-1-understand-before-you-evaluate) and [evaluating evidence](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-5-evaluate-the-evidence).
