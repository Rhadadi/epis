> **In short.** The loop organises an inquiry rather than guaranteeing its answer. Each stage should produce something another person can inspect: a claim, a map, an evidential assessment, or a qualified conclusion. Later discoveries can send you back to an earlier stage. Spend effort where it can change the assessment. A good endpoint records both what is supported and what remains open.

## Re-learn

### Follow a question through the loop

Imagine a museum considering a new booking system. The initial claim is that it will reduce queues. Clarifying it reveals a more specific prediction: shorter peak-hour waits without refusing more visitors. Classifying the disagreement separates that prediction from a practical judgment about cost. Mapping exposes assumptions about staff, arrival patterns and installation.

Evaluation then asks whether the offered reasons support the prediction. Evidence from another museum raises questions about comparability. A consequential uncertainty might be whether the system spreads arrivals or simply moves the queue online. The resulting conclusion can recommend a limited pilot while leaving the larger effectiveness claim unsettled.

These are invented stages of an example, not results from a booking-system study. Their purpose is to show what each step contributes. A stage that produces no clearer question, support relationship or evidential judgment may have become a ritual rather than a useful part of this inquiry.

### Return when the problem changes

Hitchcock's discussion of critical thinking presents inquiry as potentially spiral-shaped, with the problem redefined as obstacles appear. It also discusses treating components as repeatable activities rather than an invariant sequence, and warns about mechanical checklist conceptions. The chapter's loop can be used in that flexible spirit. [@hitchcock2024, § 5]

For the museum, discovering that most delays arise at security checks changes the target. It may send you back to define which queue the proposal can affect. That is progress: evaluating the original claim more meticulously would not repair its mistaken focus.

## Beyond the chapter

### Make the outputs inspectable

A claim needs a scope and a meaning. A map needs identifiable premises and connections. An evidence assessment needs reasons for trusting the measurements and the inference. A conclusion needs a confidence level, conditions and a distinction between what to believe and what to do.

Hitchcock distinguishes observing, imagining, inferring, consulting, judging and deciding within critical thinking. These components need not have the same product: collecting a report is different from accepting it, and judging a prediction is different from choosing a policy. [@hitchcock2024, § 6]

For a short everyday decision, these outputs can be a few sentences. For a consequential public proposal, they may need a document and source trail. The appropriate amount of detail depends on what another person must assess, rather than on whether every step has received equal space.

### Stop with a stated limitation

The loop cannot supply missing domain knowledge or inaccessible evidence. A responsible endpoint can identify the missing measurement and explain why it matters. If action cannot wait, decide under the remaining uncertainty instead of upgrading the evidence to certainty.

Transfer between domains is also a substantive issue. Hitchcock's account distinguishes different subject-specificity claims and stresses the role of background knowledge. Familiarity with an argument checklist does not establish competence in engineering, medicine or historical interpretation. [@hitchcock2024, § 12.1]

Use [finding the crux](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-6-find-the-crux) to decide what deserves further attention, and [weighing and concluding](deeper:16-critical-thinking-toolkit/step-7-weigh-and-conclude) to report the result. A recorded uncertainty is more useful than a confident answer whose missing premise has disappeared from view.
