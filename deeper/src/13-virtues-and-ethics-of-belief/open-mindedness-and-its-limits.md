> **In short.** Open-minded inquiry makes relevant alternatives available for fair consideration. It does not require giving every proposal equal credibility or endless attention. Evidence, competence, and the purpose of inquiry matter. Refusing a challenge can be reasonable, but refusing correction merely to protect a preferred conclusion is a different matter. The difficult cases concern how to distinguish those refusals.

## Re-learn

### Consideration is not agreement

Imagine a fictional council choosing a water-testing method. A member proposes a different sampling procedure and explains why it might detect contamination the current one misses. Being open-minded means engaging with that relevant possibility. It does not mean believing both methods equally reliable before examining their design.

Open-mindedness appears among the character virtues discussed by virtue epistemologists. These virtues concern more than the reliability of a faculty: they involve how the person approaches epistemic tasks. Their place within epistemology, and their relation to knowledge or the regulation of inquiry, remain contested. [@turri-etal2021, § 3, 4]

The council member might reject the alternative after examining its limitations. That outcome can be consistent with openness. Conversely, agreeing immediately to appear flexible can neglect the very comparison that matters. Evaluate the path from proposal to judgment, not just whether the final answer changed.

### Finite attention

The council cannot test every imaginable alternative. Its finite budget makes some selection unavoidable. It can state the question, ask whether a proposal could affect the result, and require an intelligible account of how. Those are proposed standards for the fictional case, rather than a formula guaranteeing good inquiry.

Selection becomes suspect when the same standards are applied unevenly: demanding impossible precision from a critic while allowing the favoured method to proceed on an impression. The asymmetry needs a justification related to the evidence, the methods, or the task.

## Beyond the chapter

### Who gets a hearing?

An objection may be missed because of the speaker’s social standing rather than its content. The discussion of testimonial justice in virtue epistemology addresses prejudice in credibility assessment, while also recording criticisms of remedies that rely too heavily on individual hearers or burden speakers with managing the encounter. Openness therefore has an interpersonal and institutional dimension. [@turri-etal2021, § 10.2]

For the council, accepting written objections and obtaining an independent methods review might make a relevant criticism easier to hear. Whether this helps would need assessment in the actual setting. The example shows how a practice can create an opportunity for fair consideration instead of demanding an unusually brave speaker.

### When caution protects inquiry

Situationist debates ask how consistently intellectual traits operate across contexts and how environments support them. That issue matters when someone behaves openly in private but defensively before an audience. A single agreeable conversation is insufficient evidence for a broad disposition. [@turri-etal2021, § 9]

There is also a distinction between considering an alternative yourself and publicly amplifying it. The latter can mislead an audience about the balance of evidence if presentation suggests equal support. This is a conceptual point about the two activities, not a claim about the measured effects of a particular media policy.

Open-mindedness consequently needs discernment: some propositions deserve investigation; others have already received an adequate answer; still others become worth revisiting when new evidence appears. A good refusal states which of these situations applies and remains answerable to relevant change.

For the competing accounts behind the virtue label, read [virtue epistemology](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/virtue-epistemology). For assessing challenges from other knowers, read [experts and novices](deeper:12-social-epistemology/experts-and-novices). Openness does not erase the need to identify a relevant epistemic contribution.
