> **In short.** Moral considerations can enter the assessment of belief in several different ways. A belief might wrong someone, an inquiry might use wrongful methods, or moral stakes might change what counts as adequate epistemic support. The last proposal is moral encroachment. Showing one of these effects does not automatically establish the others. The dispute needs both an account of the moral issue and an account of its relation to epistemic justification.

## Re-learn

### Three claims to separate

Imagine a fictional interviewer who suspects dishonesty on the basis of a broad group stereotype. One objection concerns the inference: a generalisation may not support this judgment about this person. Another concerns the relationship: forming or acting on the suspicion may wrong the interviewee. A further question asks whether the moral stakes change the epistemic support required, even if the ordinary evidence is held fixed.

Treat the last question as a formulation of the encroachment problem, not as an established result of the example. A bad inference already has an epistemic explanation; a harmful action already has a moral target. Neither needs an encroachment theory to be criticised.

The doxastic-voluntarism literature discusses *doxastic wronging*: the controversial claim that believing in certain ways can itself wrong others. It also distinguishes moral obligations of belief from epistemic obligations and asks how responsibility connects with control. Doxastic wronging therefore opens a moral question without by itself settling a change in the epistemic standard. [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 6.1]

### Evidence and the person

In the fictional case, it matters whether the interviewer has individual evidence, whether the generalisation is accurate, whether its categories are relevant, and how the judgment is held. Those questions cannot be replaced by saying that statistics are always innocent or always forbidden.

The implicit-bias literature describes alleged conflicts between epistemic and ethical aims involving social categorisation and base-rate information. Gendler and Egan develop versions of the dilemma; other authors dispute its inevitability. Brownstein’s overview points readers to moral encroachment as related scholarship. This page uses that overview as a map of the problem, rather than claiming to have checked the original modern encroachment papers. [@brownstein2025, § 3.3]

## Beyond the chapter

### Must fairness require ignorance?

One response surveyed by Brownstein argues that the problem lies in the accessibility of social knowledge in inappropriate circumstances rather than in possessing that knowledge at all. Madva’s proposal concerns regulating stereotype accessibility by situation, with goals and habits relevant to that regulation. It is presented as a response to the dilemma, not as a universal empirical cure. [@brownstein2025, § 3.3]

For the interviewer, a process that foregrounds individual records and job-relevant answers illustrates the difference between discarding information indiscriminately and changing which information drives a particular judgment. Its actual fairness and effectiveness would still require assessment. A process can fail morally, epistemically, or both.

### Responsibility and justification

Whether a belief is blameworthy can depend on available control and opportunities to investigate. That is separate from whether the belief is justified. Discussions of doxastic wronging explicitly connect moral agency with disputed accounts of voluntary belief. A lack of direct choice should neither automatically erase every responsibility nor establish responsibility without further argument. [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 6.1]

Compare [pragmatic encroachment](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/pragmatic-encroachment), whose central question also holds ordinary evidence fixed. For the harm to someone’s standing as a knower, see [epistemic injustice](deeper:12-social-epistemology/epistemic-injustice). These connections locate moral encroachment among several debates; they do not turn one theory into a complete account of injustice.
