> **In short.** Intellectual virtues concern how someone inquires, judges, and responds to reasons. A list of virtues becomes useful when it identifies different difficulties in that work. Attention, courage, humility, and openness address different failures. The appropriate expression of a virtue depends on the situation. A memorable label is not yet a reliable way to diagnose a person.

## Re-learn

### What a catalogue should explain

Consider a fictional investigator examining a disputed measurement. She needs curiosity to ask why the readings differ, attention to inspect the units, courage to challenge a senior colleague, and humility to recognise the limits of her own training. These are different tasks. Calling her intellectually virtuous does not eliminate the need to explain which ability or attitude makes a difference.

Virtue epistemologists distinguish reliable capacities, such as perception and memory, from character traits, such as conscientiousness and open-mindedness. The boundary is debated, and some approaches connect the two. Consequently, a catalogue of character virtues is only one part of virtue epistemology. [@turri-etal2021, § 3]

For character virtues, motivation and judgment matter alongside observable behaviour. Asking many questions can express curiosity, but it can also derail a discussion. Continuing work can express perseverance, but it can also preserve a failed approach indefinitely. These examples show why the relevant description includes the aim, the reasons, and the conditions of inquiry.

::: box A practical grouping
| Difficulty | Useful capacity | What would count against it? |
|---|---|---|
| Starting inquiry | Curiosity and initiative | Following only entertaining questions |
| Sustaining inquiry | Attention and perseverance | Refusing to change a failing method |
| Facing resistance | Intellectual courage | Taking risks without checking competence |
| Handling limitations | Humility and care | Treating uncertainty as permanent helplessness |
| Revising judgments | Openness and discernment | Giving every unsupported proposal equal weight |
:::

This grouping is an explanatory aid for the fictional inquiry, rather than a validated personality scale or an exhaustive taxonomy.

## Beyond the chapter

### Good judgment between failures

Roberts and Wood describe intellectual courage in relation to cowardice and rashness; Baehr connects it to worthwhile epistemic activity involving difficulty or risk. Such accounts make room for refusing an inquiry when its danger or cost is disproportionate. Courage is not measured simply by willingness to court opposition. [@turri-etal2021, § 10.2]

Humility is similarly contested. Some accounts emphasise limitations, others one’s concern with intellectual status or higher-order uncertainty about one’s judgments. These descriptions overlap without being interchangeable. Someone can admit a limitation while still caring intensely about appearing superior; someone else can be secure in a well-supported conclusion without possessing that concern. [@turri-etal2021, § 10.2]

### From personal traits to surroundings

Situationist objections challenge the assumption that people possess broad, stable traits that explain behaviour across contexts. Responses include narrower traits, developmental ideals, and attention to the environments that support good performance. A useful catalogue therefore asks what opportunities, incentives, and safeguards enable a virtue, as well as whether an individual possesses it. [@turri-etal2021, § 9]

A fictional laboratory might protect criticism by allowing junior staff to flag an anomaly without first winning a public confrontation. That arrangement does not manufacture perfect courage. It changes what courage has to overcome and makes an epistemic contribution easier to recognise.

For the competing theories behind these labels, read [virtue epistemology](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/virtue-epistemology). For the relation between limits and justified confidence, read [epistemic humility](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/epistemic-humility-and-confidence). Neither an individual checklist nor an institutional safeguard replaces assessment of actual reasons.
