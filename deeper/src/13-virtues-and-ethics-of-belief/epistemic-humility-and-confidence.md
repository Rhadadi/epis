> **In short.** Epistemic humility concerns how someone handles intellectual limitations. It need not require low confidence in every conclusion. Confidence can be justified within a well-understood competence and misplaced outside it. Admitting a limit becomes useful when it changes inquiry, reliance, or correction. Philosophers disagree about which attitudes best constitute humility.

## Re-learn

### Confidence about what?

Imagine a fictional translator who is confident about a sentence in her specialist language but uncertain about a technical term from medicine. Lowering both judgments to the same cautious level would conceal the difference in her reasons. Humility can instead involve recognising the boundary and asking a medical specialist about the term.

The virtue literature includes several accounts. Hazlett connects humility with appropriate higher-order uncertainty; Roberts and Wood emphasise freedom from excessive concern with intellectual status; Whitcomb and colleagues develop an account centred on owning limitations. These are competing or partially overlapping proposals, not one agreed definition. [@turri-etal2021, § 10.2]

On the limitations approach, acknowledging a weakness privately may be insufficient if it never changes conduct. Acknowledgment can involve attention to consequences, appropriate responses, and a willingness to address the problem. On a status-focused approach, the important defect might persist even in someone who routinely announces uncertainty. [@turri-etal2021, § 10.2]

### Three different judgments

For the translator, distinguish confidence in the sentence, confidence in her competence with the subject, and confidence that she has adequately checked this particular reading. These judgments can diverge. Years of competent work do not show that she checked a newly introduced term; uncertainty about that term does not erase her established linguistic ability.

This is an explanatory distinction, not a proposed psychological measure. It gives another person something specific to inspect rather than the vague assurance that the translator is usually careful.

## Beyond the chapter

### Humility is not automatic deference

Suppose a second translator objects. Lowering confidence makes sense only after considering what the objection provides: relevant expertise, a different text, a plausible ambiguity, or merely unsupported insistence. The disagreement literature distinguishes cases involving epistemic peers from those involving unequal evidence or competence. Identifying the comparison is part of the work. [@frances-matheson2026, § 2, 3]

Nor does humility require preferring someone else’s answer in every case. An indiscriminate policy of deference could abandon a better-supported judgment. The pertinent question is whether one is responding appropriately to reasons about one’s limitations, the disputed matter, and the other person’s position.

### Supporting the attitude

Situationist criticism challenges broad accounts of stable intellectual character, while some virtue theorists stress environmental support. A translator who keeps terminology notes, seeks review where needed, and marks unresolved ambiguities has arranged a practice in which recognising limitations can make a difference. That example illustrates a mechanism; it does not establish the success rate of a humility intervention. [@turri-etal2021, § 9]

For confidence expressed numerically, [calibration and scoring rules](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/calibration-and-scoring-rules) add a different test: how probabilities perform across appropriate forecasts. Calibration is not identical with humility, and one accurate prediction is not proof of either a virtue or a well-calibrated record. The concepts ask different questions about a knower.

Read [virtue epistemology](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/virtue-epistemology) for the broader debate about faculties and character. Its distinction between good outcomes and competent achievement helps explain why confidently guessing correctly is not an ideal of confidence.
