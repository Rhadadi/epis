> **In short.** Pragmatic encroachment says practical circumstances can partly determine whether someone knows. Its controversial claim holds even when the truth-relevant evidence is unchanged. Higher stakes causing someone to gather more evidence is a different, ordinary effect. Defenders appeal to cases and connections between knowledge and action. Critics argue that changes in caution or speech can explain the cases without changing knowledge itself.

## Re-learn

### Hold the evidence fixed

Imagine two fictional travellers checking a departure time. Each consults the same recent timetable and has the same background information. Missing the departure would mildly inconvenience one and jeopardise the other’s important appointment. Both can sensibly face different practical decisions. The further question is whether that difference changes who *knows* the time.

Pragmatic encroachment makes a constitutive claim: a difference in practical circumstances can make a difference to knowledge. It is not merely the claim that importance motivates investigation or makes someone more attentive. Those causal effects can change the evidence itself and are compatible with traditional approaches. [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12]

Stanley’s bank cases contrast low and high stakes while preserving the remembered evidence about opening hours. They are used to motivate encroachment. But a case description and an intuition about what someone should say are not yet a proof of its metaphysical explanation. [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12]

### Knowledge and reliance

Some arguments connect knowledge with properly using a proposition in action. Fantl and McGrath combine fallibilism with principles concerning knowledge and action; Weatherson appeals to decision theory. The literature contains different proposals, so pragmatic encroachment should not be identified with one complete analysis of knowledge. [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12]

For the travellers, the pressure can be stated without assuming a solution. If knowing the departure time always permits relying on it for a relevant action, and one traveller should check again while the other need not, what explains the difference? Perhaps the first does not know. Perhaps knowledge permits some reliance but does not settle every costly decision. The disputed link needs to be specified.

## Beyond the chapter

### Alternatives to the encroachment explanation

Critics including Rysiew, Brown, and Gerken argue that traditional approaches can account for the cases. Further objections concern apparently awkward consequences: someone might know until the question becomes important, despite receiving no new evidence. Defenders offer responses, and the debate remains contested. [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12]

One way to examine the fictional case is to distinguish the traveller’s belief, the action warranted by the available risks, and what a cautious speaker is willing to assert. A change in any one need not entail a change in all three. Encroachment is a substantive thesis about the epistemic state, rather than a general acknowledgement that decisions have stakes.

### Three nearby claims

This differs from [practical reasons for belief](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/practical-reasons-for-belief). A reward for believing the departure is early concerns the benefit of possessing that belief; the cost of missing an actually early departure concerns reliance on its content.

It also differs from contextualism about knowledge attributions. Encroachment concerns the subject’s practical situation; contextualist proposals concern the context in which knowledge is attributed. Finally, [moral encroachment](deeper:13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief/moral-encroachment) raises a related question about moral circumstances. Keeping these targets separate prevents a change in one kind of norm from being mistaken for every other change. [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12, 13]
