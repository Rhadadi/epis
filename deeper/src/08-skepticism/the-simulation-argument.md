> **In short.** Bostrom's simulation argument connects assumptions about conscious simulations with the proportions of different kinds of observers. It concludes a disjunction, not simply that we are simulated. A further indifference principle connects a population fraction with personal credence. The argument depends on assumptions about consciousness, computational capacity, and the relevant observers. It does not make every ordinary observation worthless.

## Re-learn

### A conditional population argument

Suppose advanced civilizations could create vast numbers of conscious simulations of people with experiences like ours. If many civilizations reached that stage and many chose to run such simulations, simulated observers could greatly outnumber biological observers. Bostrom's argument asks what follows from that population picture [@bostrom2003, §§ I, IV].

Its conclusion is disjunctive. Roughly, very few human-level civilizations reach the posthuman stage, very few posthuman civilizations run the relevant ancestor simulations, or almost all observers of the relevant kind are simulated. The argument does not select the third alternative without further grounds [@bostrom2003, §§ IV, VII].

The word relevant does real work. The argument concerns simulations with conscious observers and human-type experiences, not every computer model of a person. Increasing the number of weather simulations would not increase the population being counted.

### Consciousness is an assumption

Bostrom assumes that suitable computational implementation can produce conscious experience. He explicitly distinguishes this from merely behaving like a human or passing a conversational test. The required physical detail is part of the substrate-independence assumption, which he acknowledges as not entirely uncontroversial [@bostrom2003, § II].

The thought experiment therefore does not establish by itself that current computer systems are conscious. A counted observer must satisfy the assumed consciousness condition. Confusing a visual representation of a person with a conscious simulated person changes the argument's denominator.

### From proportions to your probability

Even if a large fraction of the relevant observers were simulated, a further step connects that fraction with your credence about yourself. Bostrom proposes an indifference principle for a case lacking information that distinguishes your membership in the simulated or biological group. This is a specified observer-selection principle, not general permission to assign equal probabilities whenever information is scarce [@bostrom2003, § V].

If you had evidence that distinguished the groups, its role would need assessment. The argument's conditions should not be discarded just because the numerical conclusion is striking.

## Beyond the chapter

### What must be checked in the reasoning

Separate four questions: whether the proposed simulations could contain conscious minds, whether advanced civilizations could run enough of them, whether enough civilizations would reach and use that capacity, and whether the sampling principle applies to us. Rejecting one assumption differs from denying the arithmetic under the assumptions.

Bostrom's discussion also distinguishes failure to reach posthumanity from imminent extinction. The first alternative does not by itself date a catastrophe; long survival without reaching the stipulated stage remains compatible with it [@bostrom2003, § VI].

This is why a slogan about the argument proving near-term doom would overstate the conclusion. Likewise, a claim that technological progress automatically proves simulation would omit both motivations and observer-selection assumptions.

### A simulation need not abolish inquiry

Bostrom says that, conditional on simulation, ordinary empirical study remains the best guide to how the world has been set up. He does not recommend abandoning practical plans or every observed regularity. The simulation conclusion is not automatically a global denial of knowledge [@bostrom2003, § VI].

The comparison with [the brain in a vat](deeper:08-skepticism/the-brain-in-a-vat) therefore needs care. A skeptical hypothesis is often introduced as a possible matching situation designed to undermine a target belief. Bostrom offers a probabilistic and disjunctive argument about observer populations. Their questions and conclusions differ.
