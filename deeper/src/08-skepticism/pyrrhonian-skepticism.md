> **In short.** Pyrrhonian skepticism is a practice of setting opposing considerations against one another until judgment is suspended. Sextus presents it as an ability, rather than the doctrine that knowledge is impossible. Its relation to ordinary belief is disputed: some interpreters allow everyday beliefs, while others understand its appearances without belief. The skeptical modes are tools for producing opposition, not a proof that every disagreement is irresolvable. The connection between suspension and tranquillity also raises questions about the skeptic's aims.

## Re-learn

### A third response to a disputed question

Two people disagree about the nature of a thing. One claims to have established the answer. Another claims that no answer can ever be established. A Pyrrhonist describes a third position: the inquiry has not yet yielded a convincing resolution, so the search continues. Sextus distinguishes the searching skeptic from both the discoverer and the person who declares discovery impossible [@morison2026, § 3.1].

Withholding judgment is not the same as believing that both answers are false. Nor is it merely never having considered the question. Someone who has investigated and found opposing considerations balanced has a different relation to the issue from someone who has never heard of it [@morison2026, § 3.2].

Imagine two equally convincing explanations for an unfamiliar noise. You cannot yet choose between them, but you can still hear the noise, record when it occurs, and continue investigating. This ordinary example helps identify suspension; it does not reproduce the full Pyrrhonian practice or prove that the explanations really are equally strong.

### A skill rather than a negative doctrine

The Pyrrhonist's distinctive ability is to find and set out opposing considerations. Sextus describes suspension as arising through their equipollence, their equal convincingness. The skeptic does not simply begin by asserting that every question must end in a tie [@morison2026, §§ 3.1, 3.2].

The difference matters when considering self-refutation. Someone who asserts that no one knows anything invites a question about whether that assertion is known. A practitioner who reports being unable to settle an issue has not yet made the same universal assertion. Whether the practice introduces other commitments remains a further question.

### Life continues, but how?

Sextus says the skeptic follows appearances, including hunger, customary conduct, and learned skills. This is his answer to the charge that suspension makes action impossible. The difficult question is whether acting in these ways already involves beliefs, and if so which kinds [@patrick1899, Outlines I.21–24; @morison2026, § 3.4].

That interpretive dispute is essential. Saying simply that the skeptic has no beliefs hides disagreement over the central texts. Saying that the skeptic only avoids philosophical theories also needs defense. The full story explains why scholars read the same passages differently.

## The full story

### Reading Sextus as an investigator

The opening of the *Outlines* distinguishes three possible outcomes of seeking: discovery, a declaration that discovery is impossible, and continued searching. Sextus associates Pyrrhonists with the last. Morison emphasizes that membership in this school is not constituted by a set of doctrines comparable to the characteristic claims of Aristotelians or Platonists [@patrick1899, Outlines I.1–4; @morison2026, § 3.1].

This does not make Pyrrhonism a formless refusal to think. Its ability has an organized activity: assembling and assessing opposing considerations. The skeptic examines what would support an affirmative answer and what would support an incompatible answer. Merely finding one bad argument on one side does not accomplish that opposition [@morison2026, § 3.2].

A useful reading therefore asks what the skeptic does with arguments. Presenting an argument for a conclusion is not necessarily endorsing that conclusion. Sextus can offer an argument against a dogmatist's claim as a counterweight, rather than as a replacement doctrine. This distinction helps explain why negative-looking arguments need not express negative beliefs [@morison2026, § 3.4].

It also makes the practice demanding. An investigator cannot assume that one weak reply balances a strong argument just because the reply points in the opposite direction. The relevant relation is convincingness, not the number of slogans available to each side. A modern debate with two speakers is not automatically an instance of equipollence.

### A primary distinction between appearance and assertion

::: original Sextus on honey
> For example, it appears to us that honey is sweet.
:::
[@patrick1899, Outlines I.19–20]. This passage comes from Patrick's public-domain translation of the first book of the *Outlines*, printed after her commentary. It is Sextus's translated statement, rather than Patrick's own assessment of the skeptic.

The passage continues by questioning whether honey is sweet by its essence. That question differs from whether sweetness appears in the current experience. The contrast does not say that every appearance is an infallible scientific measurement. It marks the relation between undergoing an appearance and endorsing an account of the object's nature [@patrick1899, Outlines I.19–20].

Modern readers can easily turn this into a two-level doctrine: certainty about private experiences and uncertainty about external reality. Yet the scholarly dispute over belief shows why that translation is too quick. Some interpreters allow the skeptic beliefs about appearances, others allow ordinary undogmatic beliefs, and others understand apparent reports as avowals without belief [@morison2026, §§ 3.4.1, 3.4.2, 3.4.3].

The wording of a report alone does not settle its philosophical role. An exclamation of pain, an assertion that one is in pain, and a medical explanation of the pain can all occur in the same situation. Their commitments differ. The interpretive problem asks which speech acts and attitudes Sextus allows, rather than simply whether he can utter sentences.

### What the modes contribute

The skeptical modes supply recurring ways to generate opposition. Morison distinguishes the Ten, Five, Two, and Eight Modes. The Ten exploit variation in appearances; the Five include disagreement, regress, relativity, hypothesis, and reciprocity. They are techniques within the skeptical ability, rather than necessarily an exhaustive list of all possible skeptical methods [@morison2026, §§ 3.5, 3.5.1, 3.5.2].

Variation can occur between observers, senses, circumstances, positions, or customary judgments. A tower can look round from a distance and square nearby. What is immediately available is a difference in appearance. The further question concerns how a person is entitled to move from one appearance to a claim about what the tower is [@morison2026, § 3.5.1].

Consider an explanatory reconstruction of the pressure such variation creates:

::: argument A challenge generated by conflicting appearances
1. A thing appears one way under one set of conditions.
2. It appears an incompatible way under another set of conditions.
3. Selecting one appearance as revealing its nature requires an account of why those conditions are preferable.
4. Therefore the variation puts pressure on an unqualified inference from appearance to nature.
:::
[@morison2026, § 3.5.1]. This reconstruction identifies a demand for an account. It does not conclude that no account can ever be given, and it does not represent suspension as the deductive conclusion of these premises.

An ordinary investigator might answer premise 3 by calibrating instruments, varying the distance, or using an independently supported theory of observation. The skeptical practice can then challenge the proposed criterion or its support. The point is to expose the dependence in the answer, rather than to infer from any variation whatever that inquiry must fail.

Morison criticizes a reconstruction that makes the skeptic endorse the proposition that neither situation can be preferred. That endorsement risks becoming a forbidden dogmatic claim. He instead develops the idea that the modes generate equal and opposing arguments, with suspension occurring in response to their balance [@morison2026, § 3.5.1].

The distinction is subtle but consequential. A logically valid argument can conclude that there is no preferred criterion. A psychological or practical account can instead describe a person unable to give preference while continuing to investigate. Sextus's presentation must be interpreted with that difference in view.

The Five Modes focus more directly on justification. A proposed reason may need another reason, loop back to what it was supporting, or stop at an unsupported assumption. Their detailed structure belongs on [Agrippa's trilemma](deeper:06-justification/agrippas-trilemma); here their role is to continue the opposition when an observer proposes a criterion for settling a dispute [@morison2026, § 3.5.2].

### Does the skeptic believe anything?

The central passage is *Outlines* I.13. Sextus distinguishes a broader sense of dogmatizing, which includes acquiescing in feelings forced by appearances, from a sense concerning unclear objects of scientific investigation. Whether this licenses belief, and what kind, has generated sustained disagreement [@morison2026, § 3.4].

Frede's interpretation permits ordinary undogmatic beliefs. On this reading, the difference need not be the subject matter alone. A belief can be dogmatic because it is held on claimed reasoned grounds, while a response to the immediate pull of an appearance can be undogmatic. Morison explains and defends a version of this interpretation [@morison2026, §§ 3.4.1, 3.4.4].

Barnes and Burnyeat instead interpret the skeptic as rejecting all beliefs. Barnes understands some appearance utterances as avowals: expressions of a condition rather than assertions about it. The analogy with saying ouch explains the proposed category, though it does not by itself demonstrate that every skeptical appearance utterance functions that way [@morison2026, § 3.4.2].

Fine and Perin provide another interpretation. The skeptic can believe that things appear a certain way, while withholding belief about how the things themselves are. This differs from Frede's permission for ordinary beliefs about the world. It also differs from Barnes's account of avowals without belief [@morison2026, § 3.4.3].

::: positions Three interpretations of ordinary skeptical life
| Interpretation | Figures discussed by Morison | What the skeptic may retain | Main pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Undogmatic belief | Frede; Morison | Ordinary beliefs responsive to appearances without theoretical endorsement | How the skeptical modes affect these beliefs |
| Avowal without belief | Barnes; Burnyeat | Expressions of appearances and patterns of action | Whether assent and ordinary action can be understood without belief |
| Belief about appearance | Fine; Perin | Beliefs about how things appear to the subject | Whether Sextus's terminology supports this restriction |
:::
[@morison2026, §§ 3.4.1, 3.4.2, 3.4.3, 3.4.4]. This is an interpretive map, not three schools Sextus explicitly lists. Morison's assessment argues that the terminology and context favour the Frede reading, while acknowledging the continuing dispute.

The lesson for reading is to preserve the difference between a text and an interpretation of the text. Sextus's willingness to follow appearances is evidence all these interpreters must explain. The short claim that appearances permit life does not decide whether the resulting life contains beliefs in the contemporary sense.

### Action without a completed theory

Sextus's fourfold observance includes nature's guidance, the necessity of feelings, laws and customs, and instruction in arts. Hunger leads to eating, thirst to drinking, and training supports skilled activity. The account directly addresses the allegation that a skeptic must be inactive [@patrick1899, Outlines I.21–24].

It leaves further questions open. Does following a custom imply belief that the custom should be followed? Does performing a skill require propositional knowledge? Does avoiding danger express a belief about the danger? An answer can distinguish habit, feeling, practical acceptance, and assertion, but the distinctions need explanation rather than merely different names.

The interpretive options produce different answers. Barnes attempts to understand the four observances without belief; the undogmatic-belief reading permits more of the attitudes ordinarily attributed to an acting person. The inaction objection is therefore connected with the belief dispute rather than a separate problem settled by listing four sources of conduct [@morison2026, §§ 3.4.1, 3.4.2].

This also prevents an easy modern dismissal. A person can act while unsure of a theoretical explanation. A physician can use a practical technique without endorsing every disputed theory about its mechanism. That ordinary distinction gives the skeptic room to respond, even though it does not establish the possibility of life without any belief whatsoever.

### Why tranquillity follows, and why that is disputed

Sextus describes people initially seeking peace through settling conflicting appearances and judgments. Instead they encounter balanced opposition and suspend judgment; tranquillity follows unexpectedly. The story of Apelles, whose thrown sponge accidentally achieves an effect he could not paint deliberately, illustrates that unanticipated route [@patrick1899, Outlines I.25–30; @morison2026, § 3.3].

The tranquillity concerns matters of opinion. Sextus does not claim that suspension removes hunger, cold, or every unavoidable discomfort. His account therefore differs from a universal promise that a skeptical person will never suffer [@patrick1899, Outlines I.25–30].

But even intellectual tranquillity presents tensions. If the skeptic keeps investigating, can they be satisfied that no answer is forthcoming? If they believe suspension invariably produces peace, does that become a doctrine? And if inquiry aims chiefly at tranquillity, is it still an ordinary truth-directed investigation [@morison2026, § 3.3]?

Morison surveys different responses concerning the internal aim of inquiry and the investigator's personal motivation. An activity can be directed by truth-sensitive methods while being undertaken for another benefit. That distinction offers a way to understand the combination of inquiry and tranquillity, without erasing objections about the skeptic's commitments [@morison2026, § 3.3].

Sextus's account is a philosophical description of a practice and its reported outcome. It is not clinical evidence that suspension treats anxiety in every person. Using the ancient idea responsibly requires keeping those kinds of claim separate.

## Beyond the chapter

### What a modern label can obscure

The contemporary skepticism survey uses Pyrrhonian as a label for universal skepticism while explicitly warning that its taxonomy is not historically exact. Its argument about all justified attitudes is therefore not a transparent paraphrase of Sextus's life and practice. Read the modern regress problem and the historical interpretation as related but distinct inquiries [@comesana-klein2026, § 2].

Likewise, ancient descriptions of Academic skepticism are part of Sextus's contrastive presentation. Repeating that contrast need not settle every historical question about Academic philosophers. For this page, the important feature is the role Sextus gives continued inquiry in distinguishing his own practice [@patrick1899, Outlines I.1–4; @morison2026, § 3.1].

### Suspension does not require artificial balance

An investigator can suspend where considerations are genuinely unsettled while accepting a conclusion elsewhere. Nothing in the concept of suspension requires assigning identical strength to every two positions. The Pyrrhonian ambition of producing opposition on any investigated issue is a stronger practical claim, and it needs assessment in its own right [@morison2026, § 3.2].

This matters for public argument. Fabricating a counterweight to strong evidence would be manipulation, not patient investigation. A productive use of the modes asks which assumptions make an argument seem compelling and whether proposed alternatives deserve their apparent force.
