> **In short.** Skeptical arguments reveal requirements we may be assuming when we claim knowledge. Their value does not depend on accepting every skeptical conclusion. They make us distinguish a possibility of error from evidence of error, and knowledge from certainty or persuasion. A response must identify which premise it rejects. That discipline also improves ordinary evaluation of doubts.

## Re-learn

### A disagreement about standards

Someone says that you cannot know a photograph is authentic because a perfect fabrication is possible. What assumption links that possibility to the conclusion? Perhaps knowledge is being treated as requiring independent exclusion of every alternative. Perhaps the speaker has particular evidence that this photograph was fabricated. Those are different grounds for doubt.

Philosophical skepticism targets propositions ordinarily counted as known. Ordinary uncertainty about an obscure fact does not raise the same puzzle. Comesaña and Klein distinguish these cases using both the content of the disputed belief and the attitudes a person can rationally take toward it [@comesana-klein2026, § 1].

The photographic example can therefore be evaluated in stages. First establish whether a specific alternative has evidential support. Then ask whether a general requirement to eliminate every possibility would apply to all photographs. Finally ask what account of knowledge licenses that requirement. The last question is philosophical even when the example is ordinary.

### Why a valid argument can still be resisted

The closure argument connects ordinary knowledge with knowledge that an incompatible skeptical hypothesis is false. If the latter knowledge is denied, the ordinary claim is threatened. Rejecting the conclusion requires identifying a premise, an ambiguity, or a qualification that fails [@comesana-klein2026, § 4].

A disturbing conclusion alone does not demonstrate invalidity. Conversely, a valid inference does not certify its premises. Studying skepticism makes this difference consequential: plausible premises can combine into a conclusion that forces us to reconsider one of them.

The process also reveals that reasonable theories can prioritize different commitments. Someone may defend ordinary knowledge, another may defend closure, and another may defend an error requirement. Their disagreement becomes clearer when each says why their preferred commitment deserves priority.

## Beyond the chapter

### A thought experiment is not a report of discovered deception

The brain-in-a-vat hypothesis specifies an experience-matching possibility. Its function is to test an epistemic principle. Constructing it does not provide empirical evidence that the actual person has been envatted. The skeptical argument still needs an account connecting the possibility with a lack of knowledge [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.2].

This distinction prevents the example from being used as a rhetorical substitute for investigation. A forged photograph should be investigated through its provenance and features. A general possibility of forgery does not identify which particular image is false. The philosophical question can be legitimate while that practical discrimination remains necessary.

### Knowing and persuading

An ordinary fact can be known even when a person cannot produce an argument convincing someone who rejects all ordinary evidence. Whether a particular argument supplies new warrant is also distinct from whether its premises and conclusion have epistemic support. The literature separates closure and transmission partly to capture this difference [@comesana-klein2026, §§ 4.1, 4.2].

Studying the distinction improves the questions we ask of an answer. Is it explaining existing knowledge, creating independent support, or trying to persuade a particular interlocutor? A response should be assessed against its actual aim.
