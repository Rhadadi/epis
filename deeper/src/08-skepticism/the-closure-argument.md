> **In short.** Closure says that appropriate deduction can extend knowledge to an implication of what is already known. It does not say that a person automatically knows every logical consequence. Skeptical arguments combine closure with the claim that a person cannot know a skeptical hypothesis is false. The argument's force depends on both premises, and on specifying the hypothesis carefully. Closure must also be distinguished from a particular argument's capacity to supply new warrant.

## Re-learn

### From one known proposition to another

Suppose you know that the locked cabinet contains a violin. You work out that it therefore contains a musical instrument and accept that conclusion on the basis of the deduction. A closure principle explains why this can extend knowledge. It does not require a separate inspection of the instrument for each new description.

The qualifications matter. You might never consider the implication. You might misunderstand the category musical instrument. You might acquire evidence that undermines the original belief while reasoning. A principle stating that anyone who knows a proposition already knows every consequence ignores these possibilities [@luper2020, § 1].

A careful version concerns competently deducing a conclusion while retaining knowledge of the premise and coming to believe the conclusion through that deduction. It connects an epistemic achievement with a suitable transition, rather than treating minds as repositories of infinitely many consequences [@luper2020, § 1].

### How the skeptic uses the same idea

Let the ordinary proposition be that you have hands. Let the skeptical hypothesis describe your being a handless brain receiving perfectly convincing simulated hand experiences. Having hands excludes that particular hypothesis. The skeptical argument then says: if you know you have hands, appropriate closure would permit knowledge that you are not that handless brain. But you cannot know that the hypothesis is false. Therefore you do not know you have hands [@comesana-klein2026, § 4].

The reasoning is familiar modus tollens. Its disturbing conclusion does not identify a logical error by itself. The challenge is to defend or reject the claim about ruling out the hypothesis, the closure principle, or another assumption in the formulation.

### Possible error is only the beginning

The hypothesis is designed to make experience match while the target belief fails. But the possibility of a matching experience does not alone establish that knowledge is absent in the actual case. One must connect that possibility with a requirement on knowing. The closure argument supplies part of that connection; the claim about lacking knowledge of the hypothesis's falsity supplies another part [@comesana-klein2026, §§ 4, 4.2].

## The full story

### Why deduction makes closure attractive

Knowledge would be strangely limited if recognizing what follows from something known could never extend it. Deduction ordinarily helps combine and unpack information. It can identify an implication not previously noticed, guide an investigation, or reveal inconsistency among what a person claims to know [@luper2020, preamble].

The last role is easy to overlook. If accepting a consequence produces a result that is clearly false, the rational response may be to withdraw the original knowledge claim. Closure does not require retaining the claim whatever follows. Its conditional applies when the premise really is known, rather than when a person confidently calls it knowledge [@luper2020, preamble].

::: original Russell on truth-preserving consequence
> Thus our principle states that if this implies that, and this is true, then that is true.
:::
[@russell1912, ch. VII]. Russell states a logical principle about truth. It helps explain the attraction of deduction, but it is not by itself a complete epistemic closure principle. A truth-preserving relation between propositions and a knowledge-preserving transition by a person are different claims.

To move from the first to the second, an account must address the person's belief, competence, and relation to the premises. A true implication does not create a belief in someone who has never considered it. Nor does its truth make an unreliable guess at the implication epistemically successful. This is why contemporary principles include conditions absent from a bare statement about truth [@luper2020, § 1].

### Straight closure and competent deduction

Luper begins with the straight principle: if a person knows *p* and *p* entails *q*, the person knows *q*. The principle fails because a person may not believe the consequence or even recognize it. Some consequences are too complex for the person to understand [@luper2020, § 1].

A repaired principle introduces knowing the entailment and believing the conclusion because of it. Another version emphasizes competent deduction from the known premise. Luper discusses how these formulations relate, including questions about additional logical knowledge involved in a deduction [@luper2020, § 1].

The repairs should not be dismissed as evasions. They identify the transition closure was meant to describe. Compare a library having a book with a reader understanding what the book implies. The implications exist independently of whether anyone has carried out the reasoning. The epistemic principle concerns the reader's achievement.

Knowledge must also survive the transition. Suppose a person learns that the supposed violin is a painted model while considering whether violins are instruments. The deduction from an asserted premise remains valid, but the premise is no longer known. The closure principle does not authorize ignoring the new information.

### One premise and several premises

Some deductions combine separate known propositions. Knowing that a person is tall and knowing that the person is left-handed can support knowing the conjunction. Luper distinguishes generalized closure from formulations focused on a single starting proposition [@luper2020, § 1].

This introduces another reason to separate knowledge from probabilistic justification. Many individually probable beliefs can form a conjunction that is much less probable. In a large lottery, each ticket is highly likely to lose, but the conjunction that all tickets will lose conflicts with the information that there will be a winner [@luper2020, § 6].

The lottery case creates pressure for closure of justified belief under aggregation. It is not a straightforward proof that knowledge cannot survive a competent deduction from known premises. Knowledge requires truth; the set of propositions that every ticket loses cannot all be known in a lottery guaranteed to produce a winner.

An argument about closure therefore needs to specify the epistemic property. Truth, belief, propositional justification, doxastic justification, and knowledge differ. Comesaña and Klein explicitly distinguish being justified in believing a proposition from having an actually held justified belief when repairing their justification principle [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.1].

### The skeptical argument reconstructed

Let *H* be the proposition that I have hands. Let *V* specify that I am a handless brain receiving the matching experiences. The incompatibility is built into this choice of *V*. A simplified knowledge argument is:

::: argument Closure and a handless vat hypothesis
1. I do not know that V is false.
2. If I know H, competent deduction from H would permit me to know that V is false.
3. Therefore I do not know H.
:::
[@comesana-klein2026, § 4; @luper2020, § 1]. The formulation suppresses some transition details for readability. Premise 2 uses a qualified closure principle; a fully explicit version includes recognition of the implication, the appropriate belief, and retention of knowledge of H.

Premise 1 is not equivalent to the claim that *V* is possible. Possibility alone does not entail absence of knowledge. Nor is it equivalent to the claim that someone in *V* would fail to know that *V* is false. That proposition would be false in the vat case, so knowledge would fail there through the truth condition. Additional reasoning is required to transfer the failure to the actual case [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.2].

Premise 2 also does not arise merely from the appearance of hands. It concerns the epistemic implication of knowing the ordinary proposition. A defender can therefore dispute the skeptic's account of why matching appearance prevents knowledge while preserving deduction as a way of extending knowledge.

The structure makes the disagreement tractable. A Moorean gives priority to ordinary knowledge and rejects premise 1. A tracking theorist can retain the ordinary knowledge claim while rejecting the relevant closure principle. A skeptic accepts both and draws the conclusion. Contextualist responses ask what proposition a knowledge attribution expresses in the relevant conversational setting [@luper2020, §§ 1, 2.1, 5.4].

### Why the hypothesis must contradict the target

An ordinary dream about having hands does not entail that the sleeper lacks hands. In that case the dreamed proposition may be true, though the dream does not appropriately disclose it. Comesaña and Klein note that hypotheses not entailing the falsity of external-world propositions complicate the closure argument [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.2].

This distinction prevents a common shortcut. From knowing that one has hands, one cannot infer that one is not dreaming anything whatever. Having hands and dreaming are compatible. The conclusion of a closure-based deduction must really follow from the premise.

A skeptical scenario can instead challenge the connection between belief and truth. A dream might produce a true belief accidentally or through an inappropriate route. Such a challenge matters for knowledge, but its logical structure differs from inferring the negation of an incompatible hypothesis. The broader problem should not be reduced to one formula [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.2].

### Dretske's zoo case

Dretske considers an observer seeing apparent zebras in an ordinary zoo. The observer knows they are zebras, he proposes, but does not know they are not mules cleverly disguised by the zoo authorities. Being a zebra excludes being that disguised mule, so the case is intended to challenge closure [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.1].

The attraction comes from separating ordinary identification from an unusual fraud hypothesis. The observer has not conducted special tests for painted mules. It feels natural to allow the former knowledge without granting the latter. But that initial reaction is not the end of the analysis.

One reply denies the asymmetry. If there is a serious reason to suspect the fraud, perhaps the ordinary knowledge claim fails too. Another reply distinguishes closure from the claim that precisely the same evidence must independently establish both propositions. Closure need not specify identical evidential routes for each [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.1].

The context is therefore part of the case. A normal identification problem and an investigation after evidence of fraud are different situations. A counterexample must preserve the premise's knowledge while denying knowledge of the consequence, rather than quietly undermine the premise halfway through.

### Tracking and the cost of denying closure

Tracking accounts introduce a condition concerning what the person would believe if the proposition were false. A person can pass this test for an ordinary proposition while failing it for the negation of a skeptical scenario. In the scenario they would still believe the scenario false [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.1; @luper2020, § 2.1].

This explains how such an account permits knowledge of zebras without knowledge that there are no disguised mules. The counterfactual associated with the latter proposition invokes precisely the deception that would preserve the misleading appearance. The two contents therefore yield different tests [@luper2020, § 2.1].

The result can seem uncomfortable: a person knows a proposition but does not know an implication they competently derive from it. Luper also discusses the red-barn case and the importance of specifying a method of belief formation, rather than treating every tracking account as identical [@luper2020, § 2.1].

An account's ability to block skepticism is not sufficient reason to accept it. The theorist must assess how much ordinary deductive knowledge the restriction sacrifices, whether the counterfactual condition is independently attractive, and how the method is individuated. The [responses to Gettier](deeper:05-the-nature-of-knowledge/responses-to-gettier) page develops those conditions more fully.

### Closure and transmission

Closure states a relationship between epistemic standing with respect to an implication and its premise. Transmission asks whether a particular route through that premise supplies new warrant for the implication. An implication can already be supported by something on which the premise's support depends [@comesana-klein2026, §§ 4.1, 4.2].

Suppose interpreting a medical display presupposes that the display is functioning. Reading a reassuring number may then support a health judgment only against that background. Using the health judgment to establish the display's reliability would repeat an assumption in a circle. This invented example illustrates why deductive implication and independent certification differ.

The point is not that every inference from an instrument's output is circular. Independent calibration, cross-checking, and external evidence can change the evidential structure. The problem concerns a proposed argument using a conclusion whose own support already depends on the condition it purports to establish.

Comesaña and Klein explain that the zebra challenge can assimilate closure to a transmission requirement. They distinguish possibilities in which the premise itself supports the consequence from ones in which the consequence's support is antecedently needed for the premise [@comesana-klein2026, §§ 4.1, 4.2].

This distinction is particularly important for Moorean arguments. An argument can be valid and its premises known while failing to supply a skeptic with an independently acceptable starting point. Whether that is a defect in knowledge, new warrant, or persuasion depends on which success condition is being assessed.

### Contextualist closure

Speaker-centered contextualists understand knowledge attributions as depending on the attributor's conversational context. They can preserve closure within a context while explaining why statements made under ordinary and demanding standards behave differently. Luper distinguishes this semantic proposal from dependence on the subject's circumstances [@luper2020, § 5.4].

On this account, one must avoid carrying a knowledge attribution from an ordinary conversation into a skeptical conversation as if its content were unchanged. Preserving closure does not mean that every sentence containing knows has identical truth conditions in every setting.

The response also leaves a substantive question: why are the ordinary knowledge attributions warranted or true? Comesaña and Klein argue that contextualism needs an independent account of ordinary justification, rather than answering that question merely by announcing context sensitivity [@comesana-klein2026, § 5].

## Beyond the chapter

### A logical puzzle with several epistemic layers

The compact argument can hide its assumptions about evidence. Are good and bad cases assumed to supply identical evidence? Does knowledge require the ability to discriminate them? Does the evidence include facts known about the world, or only shared experiences? Disputes over those assumptions can change premise 1 without touching the validity of modus tollens [@comesana-klein2026, § 4.2].

It also matters whether the conclusion concerns knowledge or justification. A knowledge skeptical claim does not by definition establish that a belief lacks every kind of rational support. A further connection between recognizing the lack of knowledge and the attitude one should take requires argument [@comesana-klein2026, § 1].
