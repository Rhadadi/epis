> **In short.** Skeptical positions differ in target, strength, and attitude. Doubting knowledge in one domain differs from denying it everywhere. Suspending judgment differs from believing a negative conclusion. Historical labels can also refer to modern argumentative patterns rather than faithful descriptions of the named philosophers. Identify what is being denied and why before classifying a skeptical view.

## Re-learn

### Scope and strength are separate

Consider three claims: we do not know tomorrow's weather; we do not know any facts about the future; we do not know any facts at all. Their scopes differ. A response that establishes one future fact would challenge the second without resolving the third's general argument about justification.

Now compare lacking knowledge with lacking justified belief. A belief might be rationally supported while falling short of knowledge. A skeptical argument targeting knowledge does not establish the absence of justification merely through the definitions. Further premises may connect recognizing one's lack of knowledge with the attitude one should take [@comesana-klein2026, § 1].

This makes a useful map: specify the domain first, then specify the epistemic status at issue. External-world knowledge, moral justification, and certainty about future events are three different targets. The generic word skepticism cannot replace that specification.

### Suspension is an attitude

Suspension is neither accepting a proposition nor accepting its negation. It also differs from never considering the proposition. A person who investigates an issue and withholds assent can have a reasoned relation to it. The suspension itself can be rationally appropriate or inappropriate [@comesana-klein2026, § 1].

If you cannot establish whether a jar contains an even number of beans, you can suspend rather than assert that the number is odd. The inability to support one answer does not automatically support the other. This simple difference matters whenever an argument from uncertainty is turned into a positive alternative.

### Historical practice and modern taxonomy

Comesaña and Klein distinguish Humean, Cartesian, and Pyrrhonian patterns through increasing scope. They explicitly use these labels without a claim to historical accuracy. Their Humean pattern challenges non-deductive inference; their Cartesian pattern challenges external-world propositions; their Pyrrhonian pattern is universal [@comesana-klein2026, § 2].

Morison's account of Sextus instead emphasizes a skeptical ability, continued investigation, and disputed limits on belief. Treating the modern taxonomy as a complete description of ancient Pyrrhonism would obscure those differences [@morison2026, §§ 3.1, 3.4].

## Beyond the chapter

### Method is not always a final conclusion

Descartes uses doubt in pursuit of an indubitable starting point. In the *Discourse*, he contrasts uncertain opinions used in practice with the procedure adopted in searching for secure truth. He then treats the thinking subject's existence as a first principle. Doubt is part of a constructive project, not simply a final declaration that knowledge is impossible [@descartes1637, Part IV].

That project can generate arguments later philosophers assess independently of Descartes's proposed solution. A critic may accept the force of the doubt but reject the attempted reconstruction, or reject the standards used to motivate the doubt. The historical thinker and the argument pattern therefore need not stand or fall together.

### Applying the map

Suppose someone questions whether a particular news source is reliable. This is a local challenge concerning a source and its outputs. If they move to saying no testimony can ever justify belief, they have changed the scope. If they then assert that the opposite of every report is true, they have also changed the attitude. Neither transition is automatic.

The map helps expose such changes without determining the answer in advance. A local challenge can be well founded; a broader challenge requires broader support. Classifying a claim is preparation for evaluating it, not a verdict about its merits.
