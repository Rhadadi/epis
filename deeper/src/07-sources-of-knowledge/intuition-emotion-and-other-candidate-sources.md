> **In short.** A judgment's arriving without conscious reasoning does not identify a single faculty. Philosophical intuitions, practiced recognitions, emotional responses, and guesses may all feel immediate while having different origins. Emotion can direct inquiry without guaranteeing the truth of what it suggests. A candidate source needs an account of its target, its contribution, and its limits. The useful question concerns how a response helps us know, rather than whether it feels compelling.

## Re-learn

### Several things called intuition

A chess player instantly spots a promising move. A person finds a proposition about a square obvious. A parent feels sure that a child has been treated unfairly. Ordinary language can call all three intuition, but that does not make their epistemic bases identical.

Pust distinguishes the philosophical topic of propositions seeming true in a characteristic way from broader everyday uses. His survey also discusses research on expert judgments in nursing, firefighting, and chess, where experience can yield a decision without conscious comparison of options. The survey's focus is armchair inquiry rather than a claim that every immediate response forms one psychological kind [@pust2024, § 1].

The distinction helps explain why immediate does not mean a priori. An expert's response may depend on extensive experience. A proposition about a concept may instead be considered through understanding and reflection. The absence of an explicit argument at the moment of judgment leaves this difference open.

### A seeming need not become a belief

Pust surveys accounts that identify intuitions with beliefs and objections to them. A proposition may continue to seem true after a person has rejected it through reasoning. Paradoxes supply a familiar illustration: several claims can each appear plausible even when they cannot all be true [@pust2024, § 1.1].

You therefore need not accept everything that strikes you as right. Recording that a conclusion seems attractive can be useful evidence about your response without making it decisive evidence for the conclusion. Reflection can ask which assumptions produced the attraction and whether they survive closer analysis.

### Emotion has more than one epistemic role

Anxiety can prompt you to check a bridge before crossing. Surprise can prompt you to revisit a prediction. Curiosity can sustain the investigation. These are contributions to inquiry even before you decide whether the initial emotion accurately represented its object.

The virtue-epistemology literature discusses epistemic emotions including curiosity, surprise, doubt, wonder, and confusion. One role highlighted in the survey is motivating confirmation or disconfirmation and further inquiry. This role differs from treating an emotional response as an independently sufficient reason for a specific factual conclusion [@turri-etal2021, § 10.4].

## Beyond the chapter

### Separate a signal from a verdict

Suppose a report makes you uneasy. The uneasiness may help you notice an inconsistency, but it does not specify whether the author is lying, mistaken, or simply unclear. The response becomes more useful when you identify what prompted it and check that feature. An inquiry can benefit from the signal even if the first interpretation was wrong.

This division avoids two oversimplifications. Treating every emotion as an obstacle ignores its possible contribution to attention and motivation. Treating every strong emotion as a reliable verdict ignores the distance between being moved to investigate and establishing what is true. The virtue survey supports examining emotional dispositions as parts of intellectual character, with questions still open about individual virtues and their relation to particular emotions [@turri-etal2021, § 10.4].

### Other candidates need the same distinctions

Claims about dreams, revelation, or unusual experiences require identifying the intended contribution. Does the experience disclose a fact, supply a hypothesis, provide personal assurance, or motivate a practice? These are different claims. A novel hypothesis still needs assessment; a profound experience does not automatically establish a public prediction.

Likewise, finding an experience difficult to describe does not by itself show that it is unintelligible or authoritative. The question is what relation to truth the proposed route supplies and what would count as a mistaken interpretation. The discussion of [knowledge by presence](deeper:07-sources-of-knowledge/knowledge-by-presence) offers a specific historical account, rather than a general endorsement of every experience labelled immediate.
