> **In short.** Calling a source basic identifies a kind of epistemic dependence, not a ranking of its importance. A belief can depend on learned skills without deriving its justification from another belief. Conversely, a judgment that arrives instantly may rely on earlier information. The classification of testimony and memory remains disputed. Ask what supplies support in the particular case before deciding which source label applies.

## Re-learn

### What supports this belief?

Suppose you see a red warning light and believe that the light is on. You then infer that the machine needs attention because its manual says that this light signals a fault. The first judgment appears to rest on perception; the second depends on the first judgment and the manual's information. Yet both involve learned concepts and a functioning visual system. Psychological complexity alone does not make the first belief inferential.

One foundationalist definition makes a justified belief basic when its justification does not come from other beliefs. An experience might supply support instead. This definition states a relation of dependence: it does not say that the belief needs no support, that everyone has it, or that it cannot be mistaken [@steup-neta2025, § 4.1].

The word source can also name a faculty or practice rather than an individual belief. That creates another question: does testimony supply support in its own right, or must its support always derive from independent evidence about the speaker? Reductionists and non-reductionists disagree about that question. A hearer may sometimes infer a speaker's reliability even if testimony need not always operate through such an inference [@leonard2023, § 1].

### Three distinctions that prevent confusion

First, distinguish acquiring a skill from using it as evidence. Learning to count through experience need not make every later mathematical justification depend on observations. The a priori debate distinguishes enabling experience from experience that supplies the reason for accepting a proposition [@russell-bruce2024, § 4.1].

Second, distinguish the absence of a conscious inference from the absence of dependence. A practiced reader need not silently recite an argument about the author's competence each time a sentence is understood. Whether this makes the resulting justification basic requires an account of testimonial support, not a stopwatch [@leonard2023, §§ 1.1, 1.2].

Third, distinguish generating information from generating knowledge. A recollection can reconstruct details while still depending on an earlier experience. Conversely, whether memory can generate knowledge that was not originally possessed is a further epistemological dispute. Reconstruction and epistemic generation are related questions rather than interchangeable descriptions [@michaelian-sutton2026, §§ 4.2, 9].

## Beyond the chapter

### A source map is not yet a theory of justification

The chapter lists familiar routes into knowing. That map is compatible with rival accounts of how beliefs receive support. Foundationalism privileges some non-inferential beliefs; coherentism instead understands justification through relations within a system. Naming perception, memory, and testimony does not by itself settle this structural disagreement [@steup-neta2025, §§ 4.1, 4.2].

In the warning-light example, separate the evidence into stages: seeing the light, retaining the manual's information, interpreting its meaning, and drawing the conclusion. If the manual is unreliable, the inference can be valid while the final belief lacks adequate support. If the light is obscured, the problem arises earlier. This decomposition identifies a failure more precisely than saying that reason or perception failed.

The basic/derived division also leaves open a question about preservation. Memory may sustain a belief after its original evidential details have been forgotten. Whether the retained belief remains justified, and what happens when a forgotten defeater once undermined it, requires more than classifying its initial source [@michaelian-sutton2026, § 9].
