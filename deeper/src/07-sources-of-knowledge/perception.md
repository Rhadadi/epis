> **In short.** Perception seems to put us in contact with things, yet appearances can mislead. The philosophical problem is not merely that our senses sometimes fail. It asks what a perceptual experience is and how it supports a belief when a matching misleading experience is possible. An account of neural processing does not by itself decide whether we perceive objects directly. An account of direct perception also needs a separate explanation of justification.

## Re-learn

### A door seen through patterned glass

You look through patterned glass and see a door whose edges appear wavy. There is a door, but it is not wavy. Contrast this with a vivid experience as of a door when no door is present. In the first case you misperceive something; in the second the supposed object is absent. Philosophers distinguish veridical perception, illusion, and hallucination when asking what experiences have in common [@crane-french2021, § 1.6].

The distinction prevents an overly quick argument. From the fact that the door looks wavy, it does not follow without a further premise that you see a wavy mental object instead of the door. Perhaps you see the door under conditions that distort its appearance. The disputed step connects an appearance of a quality with an actually instantiated quality in an immediate object of awareness [@crane-french2021, § 2.1].

Likewise, a vivid door-like experience without a door shows something about that bad case. Moving from it to a conclusion about every successful perception requires another premise: successful and hallucinatory experiences must have the same fundamental nature. Disjunctivists reject that common-kind requirement [@crane-french2021, §§ 1.6, 2.2].

### Direct does not mean physically instantaneous

Light, sensory organs, and neural processes can mediate the causal production of an experience while the person perceives a door rather than those processes. Causal mediation and an object of awareness that stands between you and the door are different notions. Contemporary intentionalist accounts can even defend direct perception while explaining experience through representation [@crane-french2021, §§ 3.3.5, 3.3.6].

The epistemic question remains: does this experience justify believing a door is there? A theory can answer by appealing to fitting evidence, a reliably functioning process, or a direct world-involving relation. These approaches disagree about which facts confer support. They are not simply three answers to a question about the brain's physical machinery [@lyons2023, § 3].

## The full story

### Two problems that are often combined

The first problem concerns perceptual experience's nature. Does seeing consist fundamentally in a relation to an ordinary object, in an experience representing such an object, or in awareness of an intermediary? The second concerns epistemic standing. What gives a belief formed through that experience adequate support? The two problems interact, but a solution to one does not automatically settle the other [@lyons2023, preamble; @lyons2023, § 2.4].

For example, direct realism about experience can say that successful seeing relates a person to a door. It does not yet say whether she must know that her experience is successful before relying on it. A theory could impose a demanding higher-level requirement and become vulnerable to skepticism despite accepting direct contact. Conversely, an account distinguishing inner appearances from external objects can propose a permissive rule for justified reliance on appearances [@lyons2023, § 2.4].

This distinction also clarifies what scientific explanations contribute. Describing how input is processed can reveal conditions of success, error, and discrimination. But whether an internally matching illusion has the same fundamental experiential nature as perception is a philosophical question about the account's categories. Whether such a state justifies a belief is a normative question about support. Neither answer simply follows from naming a processing stage.

### Russell's table: variation without a stable appearance

::: original A table's color varies with its circumstances
> This colour is not something which is inherent in the table, but something depending upon the table and the spectator and the way the light falls on the table.
:::
[@russell1912, ch. I]. Russell considers changes of lighting and viewpoint, then contrasts what appears with the physical table. His conclusion is a philosophical interpretation of variation. Observing the variation does not require accepting every part of his interpretation. A contemporary view might instead explain an object's color and its appearances through different roles for surface properties, illumination, and the observer.

The example is valuable because it separates a constant object from changing experience. You can view the same door from two angles and obtain different visible shapes. A circular object can present an elliptical aspect. The question is how the appearance relates to the object. Treating one familiar viewpoint as simply revealing the object's intrinsic appearance already involves a choice of conditions and a theory of what is being revealed.

One philosophical route introduces sense-data, immediate objects bearing the qualities that appear. Another route treats the experience as representing the ordinary object a certain way, correctly or incorrectly. A third gives the ordinary object and the perceptual relation a constitutive role in successful experience. The problem is to explain the character of experience, not merely to insist that the object exists [@crane-french2021, §§ 2.1, 3.3.3, 3.4.1].

### Reconstruct the argument from illusion

::: argument From a misleading quality to an intermediary
1. An object appears to have a quality it does not actually have.
2. Whenever a quality appears, something immediately presented to the subject actually has that quality.
3. Therefore the presented thing bearing the quality differs from the ordinary object.
4. If such a different thing is presented, the ordinary object is not also directly presented.
5. The same fundamental account applies to successful experiences.
6. Therefore successful experience also fails to present ordinary objects directly.
:::
[@crane-french2021, § 2.1]. Premise 2 is the phenomenal principle. Premise 4 is an exclusion assumption. Premise 5 is the spreading step. Stating them separately prevents the argument's conclusion from seeming to follow from the uncontroversial fact that illusions occur.

The phenomenal principle is controversial because seeming and being differ. A stage can look empty without containing an empty-looking intermediary. Likewise, a straight door can appear wavy without any wavy object being the direct target of awareness. A theory rejecting the principle still owes an explanation of the appearance, but the demand for an explanation does not establish which ontology must provide it [@crane-french2021, § 2.1].

The exclusion assumption matters even if one grants an intermediary. Showing awareness of something that is not the door does not show absence of awareness of the door. A person can be aware of more than one thing. Crane and French discuss the gap between those conclusions and the added assumption needed to bridge it. An argument can fail there even before the generalization to successful perception is considered [@crane-french2021, § 2.1].

This is a useful logical lesson. *You directly encounter a non-door thing* and *you do not directly encounter the door* are different claims. A familiar story about sense-data can encourage a reader to substitute the second for the first. The explicit argument reveals what additional premise is doing that work.

### The argument from hallucination changes the burden

In the stipulated hallucination, no door is presented. That base-case claim does not require the phenomenal principle about a misleading quality. The pressure comes from saying that a successful door experience and an indistinguishable hallucination share one fundamental experiential kind. If the same account must fit both, a relation requiring the door seems unable to define that kind [@crane-french2021, § 2.2].

Subjective indistinguishability is not obviously identity of nature. Two experiences can be alike from the subject's reflective standpoint while differing in a relation to an object. An intentionalist can locate their shared aspect in representational content. A naive realist can instead deny that this shared aspect exhausts what successful perception is. The apparent sameness generates a problem; it does not settle the account [@crane-french2021, §§ 2.2, 3.3.3, 3.4.1].

The philosophical case is normally about a possible matching hallucination. It need not claim that every clinical hallucination is impossible to distinguish from ordinary perception. Real episodes can have features enabling recognition. Confusing the possibility claim with a universal empirical generalization makes the argument either stronger or easier to dismiss than its intended form [@crane-french2021, § 2.2].

The relation between illusion and hallucination also remains significant. Illusion can involve the ordinary object despite a mistaken appearance. Hallucination lacks the presented ordinary object in the stipulated case. A view can treat successful seeing and illusion as sharing a world relation while differentiating hallucination. Classifying every failure as the same kind of bad experience would erase a distinction the theory may need [@crane-french2021, §§ 1.6, 2.2].

### Intentionalism: representation without a picture being watched

Intentionalism appeals to what experience represents. An experience can represent a door as present and wavy whether or not its content is accurate. Shared content can help explain similar phenomenal character across success and failure. Representation here does not require an inner picture that the person watches as a separate object [@crane-french2021, § 3.3.3].

This point prevents a frequent conflation. Saying that perception represents the world need not mean that we perceive only a mental representation and infer the world behind it. An intentionalist can say that in a good case the experience directly represents the object and is appropriately caused by it, so the person directly perceives the object. Crane and French discuss such causal direct realism [@crane-french2021, §§ 3.3.5, 3.3.6].

The account nevertheless differs from naive realism. If a matching experience could occur without the door, the experience's common nature is not fundamentally constituted by that door. The ordinary object can be necessary for a successful instance of seeing while not constituting the experience's shared phenomenal kind. Distinguishing successful perception from the experience it includes helps articulate that position.

An intentionalist still needs a theory of content. What is represented: a particular object, a general arrangement, properties, relations? How does that content explain experience's felt character? Does every aspect of character come from content? The survey notes variations within the view. It should not be reduced to one universal claim that perceptual consciousness is nothing but a belief-like description [@crane-french2021, § 3.3.3].

### Naive realism is not blanket infallibility

In contemporary debate, naive realism assigns an important constitutive role to the ordinary world in successful experience. It need not say that everything always looks exactly as it is. The survey discusses variation with the perceiver, viewpoint, modality, and manner of relation. The same presented object can contribute to different experiential characters [@crane-french2021, § 3.4.1].

Thus naive is a technical label, not a dismissal of sensory complexity. A view can recognize optical conditions and failures while holding that genuinely seeing a door is fundamentally a relation involving the door. The challenge is to extend or supplement that explanation for illusion and hallucination without losing what makes the successful case distinctive [@crane-french2021, §§ 3.4.1, 3.4.2].

Disjunctivist approaches reject a single fundamental kind covering successful perception and matching hallucination. They can describe the subject's situation as either genuine perception or a misleading episode without treating the alternatives as sharing an exhaustive underlying nature. Their explanatory burden includes the apparent sameness and the character of the bad case. Rejecting the common-kind premise opens a route; it does not finish the theory [@crane-french2021, §§ 2.2, 3.4.1].

### Epistemic approaches ask a further question

Lyons distinguishes evidential, etiological, and world-involving approaches. Evidential accounts look for an appropriate relation to good grounds. Etiological accounts look at how the belief is produced. World-involving accounts appeal to the perceiver's relation to the fact itself, such as seeing that a door is present. These categories need not be exclusive [@lyons2023, § 3].

::: positions What confers perceptual support?
| Approach | Central candidate | Main question |
|---|---|---|
| Evidential | A fitting experience or other good reason | What makes the ground support this belief? |
| Etiological | An appropriate belief-forming process | Which process and conditions make it epistemically good? |
| World-involving | Seeing the relevant fact | How does the successful relation bear on justification and skeptical error? |
:::
[@lyons2023, § 3]. The categories identify explanatory strategies. A world-involving account can construe seeing as evidence; a process account can give an experience an important causal role. Their overlap does not eliminate disagreements about deceptive counterparts or accessible grounds.

For an evidential view, an internally matching bad case can supply similar grounds even when the conclusion is false. For a reliability view, the process's actual or relevant counterfactual performance matters. For a factive view, the good-case reason can require that the fact obtains. A matching hallucination then lacks that very reason. These are different explanations of support, not just different descriptions of sensory information [@lyons2023, §§ 3, 3.3].

### Seeing that and merely seeming

Seeing that a door is present is factive: if the attribution is correct, the door is present. Merely seeming to see a door is not. World-involving accounts use this difference to resist the assumption that the best possible perceptual reason must be neutral between success and a matching hallucination. A genuine good-case relation can offer support absent in the bad case [@lyons2023, § 3.3].

But a factive relation does not automatically explain the subject's access to it. Can she recognize from her standpoint that she has this reason when a hallucination would seem alike? Some epistemological disjunctivists defend a form of access; other world-involving accounts are more explicitly externalist. The survey presents these differences instead of treating disjunctivism as one unanimous answer [@lyons2023, § 3.3].

This distinction also separates metaphysical and epistemological disjunctivism. An account of experience's fundamental kind need not entail a particular rule about which beliefs are justified. An epistemic account invoking factive seeing needs to explain the relation between seeing, justification, and knowledge. It cannot settle every case merely by attaching the good-case label [@lyons2023, §§ 2.4, 3.3].

### Where the skeptical pressure remains

A skeptic can demand justification that distinguishes genuine seeing from a deceptive alternative. Whether that demand must be met before ordinary perception justifies anything is itself disputed. An account granting immediate support can reject the preliminary certification requirement; a different account can argue that good-case factive grounds already distinguish the cases in the required way [@lyons2023, §§ 2.4, 3.3].

Local checks remain useful even if global certification is difficult. Changing the viewpoint can test an apparent bend. Touch can check an apparent boundary. Another person can inspect the object. These procedures do not provide a perspective outside all perception and memory. They can nevertheless identify particular defects within ordinary inquiry. A demand for universal independence differs from an ordinary request for a relevant check.

The philosophical lesson is proportionate. Failures show that appearances and reality can diverge. They do not alone show that perception never presents reality, that experience never supplies support, or that every perceptual belief is knowledge. Reaching any of those conclusions requires the additional premises made visible by the competing accounts.

## Beyond the chapter

### Neural representation does not settle the philosophical label

An account can describe representational processing while defending direct perception of ordinary objects. A direct realist can allow physical mediation without making the intervening machinery the object seen. The important questions concern the kind of awareness and its epistemic role, not whether any causal stages occur between an object and a belief [@crane-french2021, §§ 3.3.5, 3.3.6; @lyons2023, § 2.4].
