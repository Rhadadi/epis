> **In short.** Knowledge by presence is an account of awareness in which what is known is present rather than known through a representing form. Suhrawardi develops it through self-awareness and awareness of pain, within his philosophy of illumination. The proposal is more specific than the everyday claim that an experience feels immediate. A later judgment about an experience can introduce concepts and mistakes even if awareness itself is unmediated. Comparisons with acquaintance or contemporary self-knowledge are useful when they preserve those differences.

## Re-learn

### Feeling a pain and reading a report of it

You can read a note saying that someone was in pain, interpret a scan, or hear a person's description. These routes represent a state to you. Feeling your own pain seems different: you are not first reading a description and then identifying the painful state it describes. Suhrawardi's account uses pain and self-awareness as paradigms of an unmediated, non-discursive mode of awareness [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

This distinction does not imply that every judgment about the pain is correct. You might misdescribe it as burning rather than stabbing, mistake its cause, or misremember when it began. Awareness of the state, classification of the state, and explanation of its cause are different tasks. A theory that secures the first need not secure every proposition generated in the other two.

The phrase presence also requires care. Being physically near a medical chart does not turn reading it into presential knowledge of the patient. Presence in this theory concerns a relation of knowing, not simply spatial distance. Similarly, something's seeming immediately obvious does not establish that it is known without representation.

### More than a claim about ordinary introspection

Suhrawardi's proposal belongs to a metaphysics of lights. Self-awareness is connected with the nature of incorporeal light, and the account extends beyond a person's reports of feelings. Mullā Ṣadrā develops related ideas within an ontology emphasizing existence. Treating both as just an early version of checking your mood would lose their explanatory ambitions [@marcotte2025, §§ 3.2, 4.2; @rizvi2021, § 4.4].

At the same time, understanding the ordinary example does not require accepting the whole metaphysics. You can examine the distinction between awareness and representation first, then ask which further claims about the self, the world, or divine knowledge follow. Separating those steps makes both the theory and objections more precise.

## The full story

### The contrast with acquired representation

In a representational account, a form or concept stands in a relation to what is known. An appropriate correspondence gives the representation its truth or accuracy. The possibility of a gap becomes epistemically important: an appearance can occur without the external object, and a concept can fail to match its target. Suhrawardi questions whether that model explains basic self-awareness [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

The challenge is especially sharp for the first-person relation. A representation of a person does not identify that person as you merely by looking familiar. If you recognize it as representing yourself, the recognition seems to presuppose some grasp of what makes it yours. The chapter's regress formulation draws attention to that dependence. It asks whether representational identification can be the ultimate source of self-awareness, rather than whether representations ever contribute to knowing oneself.

Marcotte describes Suhrawardi's positive alternative as self-evident, unmediated awareness rather than abstraction through an image, form, notion, or attribute. Her survey characterizes the pain paradigm as non-discursive, non-conceptual, and non-propositional. Those qualifications matter: it is not simply an extraordinarily certain belief about the self stated in a sentence [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

### An explanatory reconstruction of the dependence problem

The following argument makes one concern behind the rejection of representation explicit. It is an explanatory reconstruction, not a quotation or a claim that Suhrawardi wrote these numbered premises. It isolates the role of recognizing something as representing oneself [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

::: argument Why representation may not provide the first self-awareness
1. A representation can depict a person without by itself identifying that person as the subject who is aware of it.
2. Recognizing it as a representation of oneself requires an appropriate first-person relation to its target.
3. If that relation requires another representation recognized as one's own, the identification problem recurs.
4. Therefore an account must explain a first-person relation that does not depend only on another act of representational identification.
:::
[@marcotte2025, § 3.2]. The conclusion identifies an explanatory need. It does not yet prove an immaterial soul, an ontology of lights, or a universal principle of infallibility. Those further conclusions require further premises.

An objector can question premise 2's demand. Perhaps a representation can be self-referential through how it functions, without a separate conscious recognition of its target. Or perhaps the first-person relation is built into a cognitive architecture rather than established through an inferential identification. Such replies concern what representation includes. A dispute can turn on whether the term is defined narrowly enough to require a separate identifying act.

The distinction also keeps a regress from becoming too easy. If every cognitive state is redescribed as a representation and every representation must be validated by another explicit judgment, an infinite demand follows almost automatically. A useful argument needs to explain why the relevant identifying condition is genuinely required. Otherwise the conclusion depends on imposing the very model under dispute.

### What Suhrawardi's alternative tries to explain

Marcotte connects self-awareness with the soul's existence, identity, and individuation. The self is not first discovered as one object among others and then recognized as the observer. The account instead introduces a pre-cognitive awareness logically prior to a subject–object distinction of the ordinary representational kind. This gives the knowing subject a different status from an external object depicted to it [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

That priority is explanatory, not necessarily a claim about an earlier moment in childhood. Saying that one relation is presupposed by another need not mean that a person first had the relation at a distinct time. The issue is which feature explains the possibility of self-directed knowing. It can remain even when the person has never formulated any theory of self-awareness.

The pain example gives the proposal an accessible starting point. A pain is not normally known only after comparison with a public representation. Yet making a universal claim from that example requires caution. Awareness of a sensation, awareness of a belief, a person's knowledge of motives, and a metaphysical account of the persisting self are different targets. Similar use of the word self does not establish one account for them all.

### A primary passage through an open secondary source

::: original Intellection as presence
> the presence (hudur) of a thing for a self (dhat)
:::
[@marcotte2025, § 3.2]. Marcotte quotes this expression from Suhrawardi's *Intimations*, in her discussion of its dream-vision dialogue. The original work and translation were not read independently for this page; this brief primary passage is checked in the open encyclopedia text. Its context presents a relation of knowing, rather than a claim that the word presence itself solves every epistemological problem.

The passage directs attention to the subject for whom something is present. A mere object existing somewhere is not thereby known. Nor is a subject's having an arbitrary picture sufficient. The proposal tries to explain an awareness relation that is intrinsically connected with its target. Whether that relation is coherent, and how it differs from representation, remains a legitimate subject of analysis.

The dialogue's literary setting also matters. Marcotte describes an Aristotle figure within Suhrawardi's dream-vision. The quoted words should not be reassigned to a surviving treatise by the historical Aristotle. Primary material can appear within a secondary survey while retaining its correct author and context. Keeping that chain visible is part of responsible citation [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

### Presence within the philosophy of illumination

Light in Suhrawardi's account is not merely a decorative metaphor for clarity. Self-awareness, self-manifestation, intensity, and relations among lights form part of the ontology. Marcotte explains how degrees of light relate to degrees of self-awareness and how the account addresses divine knowledge of itself and other things [@marcotte2025, § 4.2].

This setting changes the scope of the theory. A modern reader who extracts only the sentence that pain is directly felt might leave behind what made the view a complete metaphysical account. Conversely, a critic who rejects the metaphysics immediately may fail to examine the narrower self-awareness argument. Evaluating the two levels separately permits a more discriminating verdict.

The theory also links intuitive knowing with contemplation and spiritual practice. Marcotte describes access to metaphysical lights as a central ambition rather than a peripheral application. Such claims are historical descriptions of the view. They should not be presented as empirical findings that everyone who undergoes a compelling experience thereby perceives a metaphysical reality [@marcotte2025, § 3.2].

An immediate experience and an interpretation of what it reveals therefore need different treatment. The person may accurately report that something occurred or seemed significant. The claim that it reveals a particular ontology is a further judgment. Preserving that distinction avoids granting an unexamined metaphysical conclusion the certainty attributed to the experience itself.

### Mullā Ṣadrā: a related idea with a different ontology

Rizvi distinguishes several models in Mullā Ṣadrā's treatment of knowledge. The representational model seeks correspondence between an internal concept and an external object. Presential knowledge instead connects knowledge with existence and a more immediate relation to what is known. The survey stresses the difference between Ṣadrā's emphasis on existence and Suhrawardi's emphasis on light [@rizvi2021, § 4.4].

This is development rather than simple repetition. A theory can borrow a relation of presence while changing its ontological explanation. Calling both thinkers defenders of presence is useful, but saying they held exactly the same theory would miss a substantive contrast. The relation to divine knowledge of particulars also becomes important in the later account [@rizvi2021, § 4.4].

Rizvi also explains that the representational model retains a role despite not furnishing the preferred route to certainty. That prevents an overstatement that Ṣadrā simply denied all ordinary conceptual cognition. The philosophical question concerns the adequacy and ultimate epistemic status of different models, not whether people ever use concepts or representations [@rizvi2021, § 4.4].

The broader ambition makes critical assessment harder than asking whether pain feels direct. How could presential awareness extend to entities beyond ordinary inner states? What makes an asserted unitive relation epistemically available? How are different interpretive claims distinguished? The examples motivate questions; they do not supply every answer without additional explanation.

### Comparison with Russell's acquaintance

Russell distinguishes knowledge by acquaintance from knowledge involving description. His account permits direct awareness of particular thoughts and feelings. But he explicitly hesitates about acquaintance with the bare self, as distinct from its particular contents. That qualification is crucial when comparing his theory with an account centered on self-awareness [@russell1912, ch. V].

The shared idea is a relation of direct awareness that is not simply inferring from a description. The differences concern what is directly known, what ontology explains the relation, and whether the self itself is among the targets. A resemblance at one level should not be converted into identity of theories.

Nor does acquaintance automatically explain every proposition formed from it. Being aware of a sensation can be different from knowing that it belongs to a certain category. The relation and the judgment can have different conditions. This is part of why foundationalist acquaintance theories face questions about how a fact, a thought, and their correspondence enter justification [@hasan-fumerton2022, §§ 2.2, 3.2].

::: positions Distinguish the target of an immediacy claim
| Claim | What it concerns | What does not automatically follow |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness of a present state | A relation to the state as experienced | Correct classification or causal explanation |
| Awareness of oneself | A first-person relation | A complete theory of the persisting self |
| Acquaintance with contents | Direct awareness of thoughts or feelings | Acquaintance with a bare self |
| Presential metaphysical knowing | A proposed relation to reality beyond representation | Validation of every reported mystical interpretation |
:::
[@marcotte2025, § 3.2; @russell1912, ch. V; @gertler2024, preamble]. These distinctions compare explanatory targets. They are not a verdict that every listed relation exists or that every philosopher accepts the same boundaries.

### Awareness, a belief about awareness, and a public report

Consider three stages: feeling pain, believing that you are in pain, and telling someone that you are in pain. A theory can assign different epistemic conditions to them. The first can be an occurrence or awareness relation. The second uses a concept. The third adds language, context, and communication. Describing the first as non-propositional does not prevent the later stages from being assessed as true or false.

This is why a criticism based on mistaken introspective reports needs a precise target. If somebody misidentifies a feeling, that challenges the report or classification. It does not alone show that no state was present to the subject. Conversely, saying that the state was present does not vindicate the classification. Both sides can overreach by transferring a property between levels.

Gertler's survey distinguishes epistemic security, an exclusively first-person method, cognitive agency, and first-person authority. These are different claims about self-knowledge. A special social presumption in favor of someone's report does not establish infallibility; a special method does not establish omniscience about every mental state [@gertler2024, §§ 1, 1.1].

### Infallibility and omniscience differ

Infallibility says that a certain kind of judgment cannot be false. Omniscience about one's states says that whenever a state occurs, the person knows it occurs. One can deny either without denying every form of privileged access. Gertler notes that few contemporary philosophers endorse both for all mental states [@gertler2024, § 1.1].

This matters when interpreting the claim that presence leaves no gap for error. The claim might concern a particular awareness relation rather than every belief the subject forms. It might concern the object's existence in that relation rather than complete recognition of every aspect of it. Without a specification, the slogan seems to grant more than the example can establish.

Likewise, a subject can be certain that she experiences something without being certain of its description. A vague unease can be felt while the person struggles to classify it as fear, guilt, or fatigue. The distinction gives a theory room to preserve a direct relation while acknowledging conceptual uncertainty. Whether that is the correct account remains a separate question.

### What would count as a good criticism?

A critic should identify the relation the account posits, then ask how it explains its target. Does the self-awareness argument require a separate act of identification for every representation? Can a representational theory avoid that demand? Does the proposed presence relation distinguish awareness from an unexplained metaphysical label? These questions engage the explanatory structure.

A further criticism can concern reach. Even if direct awareness of pain is accepted, why extend it to metaphysical lights or divine knowledge? The extension requires premises about the nature of the self and reality. A narrow example does not license the entire ontology. A defender should explain those premises rather than appeal to the felt immediacy of an experience as if it answered every question.

Finally, a reader can ask whether knowledge should be restricted to propositional knowledge. If so, the non-propositional awareness may be a ground or precondition rather than knowledge in that restricted sense. If knowledge is used more broadly, that terminological choice can be defensible. The important point is to make the target clear, so that a disagreement about words does not substitute for evaluating the proposed awareness relation [@marcotte2025, § 3.2; @gertler2024, preamble].

## Beyond the chapter

### Comparison without assimilation

Presence, acquaintance, first-person authority, and rational insight identify different candidate relations. Their shared vocabulary of immediacy can conceal differences about objects, concepts, and ontology. Use comparisons to ask sharper questions, not to conclude that one tradition merely anticipated another or that every account confirms the same doctrine [@marcotte2025, § 3.2; @rizvi2021, § 4.4; @russell1912, ch. V].
