> **In short.** An inference draws a conclusion from premises or information already available. A valid form preserves truth if its premises are true; it does not certify the premises. Epistemic success also requires an appropriate relation between the person's reasons and their belief. Reliable reasoning can still inherit a problem from unreliable inputs. Inference therefore needs assessment at both the input stage and the step connecting inputs to conclusion.

## Re-learn

### A good connection can carry a bad starting point

Suppose the library closes whenever the building alarm sounds, and the alarm is sounding. The conclusion that the library closes follows deductively. But perhaps you mistook a nearby phone for the alarm. The relation between the stated premises and conclusion remains valid; your route to accepting a premise is defective.

Now suppose you have excellent evidence for both premises, but believe the conclusion because a fortune-teller predicted it. Your available evidence and the actual basis of your belief come apart. The basing relation concerns the connection between a belief and the reasons on which it is held, rather than simply whether suitable reasons exist somewhere in the person's evidential situation [@korcz2026, § 1].

Assessing an inference thus involves separate questions. Are the premises supported? Does the transition warrant the conclusion? Is the belief appropriately based on that support? Different answers can identify different failures. Merely writing a valid argument beside a belief does not establish that the argument explains its justification.

### Beyond deduction

Inference need not guarantee its conclusion. A repairer may infer the likely source of a fault from several symptoms, while allowing alternative explanations. Foundationalists commonly permit non-deductive support between basic and further beliefs because ordinary justified beliefs cannot all be straightforwardly deduced from a small foundation [@steup-neta2025, § 4.1].

An additional symptom may undermine the repairer's first conclusion without proving the earlier reasoning irrational. A careful judgment can be defeasible: warranted relative to the available information but subject to revision. That is why assessing a non-deductive inference requires attention to alternatives and background conditions, as well as to its stated premises.

### Reasoning as a process

Reliabilist accounts also ask how the belief-forming process performs. Their treatment of belief-dependent processes distinguishes the quality of inferential transitions from the epistemic status of the input beliefs. A process can be good at drawing true conclusions from true inputs while performing badly when supplied with false inputs [@goldman-beddor2021, § 2.3].

Consider a calculator supplied with the wrong price list. Correct arithmetic does not repair the inaccurate data. Conversely, accurate prices do not repair an arithmetic mistake. Human inference involves richer problems than calculation, but the separation of input and transformation remains helpful.

## Beyond the chapter

### No automatic conversion into knowledge

The analysis of knowledge also shows that avoiding false premises does not eliminate every kind of epistemic luck. The familiar barn-facade case concerns a person looking at the one genuine barn in a deceptive environment. The belief can be true without deriving from a false lemma, yet still seem accidentally correct [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 4].

This case does not show that ordinary inference never supplies knowledge. It shows that one proposed safeguard, the absence of false lemmas, is insufficient as a general account of knowledge. Questions about luck and the environment remain alongside questions about premises and validity.

### Showing an argument and making an inference

A public argument may reconstruct reasons more explicitly than the thinker originally did. That reconstruction can clarify a transition and reveal an overlooked assumption. It can also misrepresent the actual basis if it substitutes a better reason after the fact. The distinction matters whenever a person is asked both what supports a claim and why they believe it [@korcz2026, § 1].

Read [validity and soundness](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/validity-and-soundness) for the logical distinction, and [epistemic luck](deeper:05-the-nature-of-knowledge/epistemic-luck) for the environmental problem. The later chapter on [non-deductive inference](09-induction-probability-bayes.md#kinds-of-non-deductive-inference) develops probability and explanatory support.

### Where to go next

Korcz introduces the basing relation; Goldman and Beddor explain the input-sensitive structure of inferential reliability. Steup and Neta connect inference with theories of justification, while Ichikawa and Steup explain why a no-false-lemmas condition does not settle the knowledge question. These are distinct issues, so read them with the same example in mind while changing the question being asked [@korcz2026, § 1; @goldman-beddor2021, § 2.3; @steup-neta2025, § 4.1; @ichikawa-steup2026, § 4].
