> **In short.** Misleading communication can arise through error, deliberate deception, or selective use of genuine information. Its content and its producer's intent are different questions. Propaganda concerns organized influence on beliefs and actions, whose epistemic defects require examination of both messages and their circulation. A true sentence can mislead when a context or comparison is withheld. Checking information therefore includes tracing its source and asking what the presentation leaves out.

## Re-learn

### Separate the message from the motive

Imagine a notice claiming that a reservoir is full. This is an invented example. One resident repeats an old report without noticing the date; another knowingly removes the date to conceal a current shortage. The repeated sentence is the same, while the second communication has a deceptive intention.

For this page, *misinformation* covers false or misleading material without settling intent; *disinformation* identifies deliberate deceptive communication. Terminology in the literature varies, so a precise analysis should state what is wrong with the content and what evidence supports an attribution of intention.

You can establish that the report is outdated without knowing whether the resident intended deception. Conversely, evidence of a manipulative motive does not make every sentence that person utters false. Avoid turning a judgment about the communicator into a substitute for checking the claim.

### True information can produce a false impression

Suppose a campaign publishes the reservoir's capacity during the wettest month while presenting it as representative of the year. The figure may be accurate. The selection and framing can still encourage an unwarranted conclusion about ordinary supply.

The important questions concern the relevant time period, comparison, and missing evidence. Replacing the false impression requires more than affirming that the published number is authentic. Explain what the number measures and what it cannot establish.

Several notices copying the campaign's graph do not necessarily add new evidence. Trace whether they rely on separate measurements or share a single source. A message's visibility and its evidential support are different properties.

Sources for this layer: [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.3, 5.2].

## Beyond the chapter

### Definitions remain contested

O'Connor, Goldberg, and Goldman describe a common distinction between unintended misinformation and intended disinformation, while noting that misleading content can be true and that other accounts emphasize ignorance or obstruction of action. Their discussion of malinformation and competing definitions shows why a label alone does not supply a diagnosis. Specify the epistemic failure in the case being assessed. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 5.2].

In the reservoir example, the decisive issue might be an omitted date, selective sampling, an unsupported causal claim, or false attribution to a specialist. Those require different checks. A conclusion about intent needs further evidence, such as a documented decision to suppress the date, rather than an inference from error alone.

### Propaganda can operate through selection

Network models reviewed in the entry examine propagandists distributing biased samples of genuine research results. Other models consider fraud or industry influence on which investigators receive support. They illustrate how misleading influence can arise without every communicated finding being fabricated. These are model-based mechanisms, whose relevance to an actual campaign must be established separately. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.3].

A useful response in the hypothetical case is to locate the underlying reservoir records, compare the campaign's selection with the whole relevant period, and publish a correction that identifies the missing context. This advice follows the identified defect; it is not a claim that a particular correction technique has a demonstrated universal effect.

The social setting matters too. A correction can be visible yet rejected because its source has already been discredited. [Echo chambers](deeper:12-social-epistemology/echo-chambers-and-epistemic-bubbles) explains that distinction. [Post-truth and bullshit](deeper:11-truth-and-relativism/post-truth-and-bullshit) considers indifference to truth, while [Chapter 14](14-psychology-of-reasoning.md) examines psychological mechanisms.
