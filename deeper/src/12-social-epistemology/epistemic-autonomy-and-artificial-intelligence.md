> **In short.** Thinking for yourself can include choosing reliable sources and managing dependence on them. Artificial intelligence makes that dependence harder to classify because a generated report need not involve the same responsibility as a human assertion. A useful answer, its apparent confidence, and its evidential basis should be assessed separately. Asking for a source helps only if the source exists and supports the claim. Autonomy involves retaining responsibility for how an answer enters your inquiry.

## Re-learn

### Assistance changes the route to a belief

Imagine using an AI assistant to summarize a building's maintenance requirements. This is an invented example. It produces a fluent paragraph and names a technical standard. You can use that output to decide what to investigate next. Believing the requirement is established needs a further check: does the standard exist, apply to this building, and actually say what the summary attributes to it?

The same distinction would matter if a person supplied the summary. Assistance can point toward evidence without becoming that evidence. A report's wording does not reveal all the grounds supporting it.

There are also different sources behind an output. It could reproduce an official notice, combine several documents, or offer a proposed interpretation. Each route calls for a different assessment. Treating all of them as one undifferentiated answer conceals where uncertainty enters.

### A source is not verified by being named

Suppose the named standard turns out to discuss ventilation rather than electrical inspection. Its existence does not support the claimed maintenance requirement. Conversely, failure to find a supporting document does not prove that the requirement is false; it shows that this route has not established it.

For a consequential question, locate the authoritative record and consider qualified advice. Keep track of which part you checked and which part remains a suggested interpretation. This preserves a usable distinction between a lead, a supported proposition, and a decision.

These are practical requirements for any assisted inquiry. They do not depend on attributing intentions to an automated system or assuming that every automated answer is unreliable.

## Beyond the chapter

### Does a machine testify?

The social-epistemology entry identifies a dispute about AI testimony. Some accounts understand testimony as a speech act for which a speaker bears responsibility and deny that instrument-produced sentences qualify in the same way. Other accounts extend testimonial epistemology to AI reports. The disagreement concerns the kind of epistemic relation involved, rather than the simple question of whether an output can be correct. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 3.1].

This difference matters when seeking accountability. A human specialist can be asked what observations and methods support a judgment and can assume responsibilities associated with communicating it. An automated report raises further questions about who produced the relevant grounds and who can answer for them. A product's ability to generate another explanation does not by itself establish that explanation as the original basis.

### Deference and independent judgment

Accounts of authoritative testimony disagree about whether an authority's report replaces the hearer's other reasons or adds a strong reason to them. Before applying either account to an AI system, establish what makes the system an authority on the specific task and whether the relevant responsibility and reliability conditions are met. General fluency cannot settle that question. [@leonard2023, § 5].

Autonomy need not require personally repeating all specialist work. You can manage dependence by selecting sources, understanding their scope, and maintaining a route to correction. McLeod's distinction between trust and mere reliance also cautions against assuming that an interpersonal trust relation automatically applies to every tool. [@mcleod2023, § 1].
