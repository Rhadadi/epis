> **In short.** A crowd can combine information that no one member possesses. It can also multiply a shared error. Whether aggregation helps depends on competence, informational independence, diversity, and the procedure used. A theorem about an idealized majority does not guarantee that an actual crowd meets its assumptions. Before treating agreement as strong evidence, ask what new information each judgment brings.

## Re-learn

### A small mathematical example

Imagine three independent inspectors making a yes-or-no judgment. This is a constructed model. Each has a probability of 0.6 of being correct, and the group follows the majority. The majority succeeds if exactly two are correct or all three are correct:

\[
3(0.6)^2(0.4)+(0.6)^3=0.648.
\]

The majority is more reliable than one inspector under these assumptions. Now give each inspector a probability of only 0.4 of being correct. The same calculation gives 0.352. Aggregation makes the poorly performing majority worse.

The numbers are stipulated, not observations about real inspectors. They show why a competence assumption matters. Increasing the number of judges cannot compensate automatically for a shared tendency to answer incorrectly.

### Three voices may contain one report

Suppose the second and third inspectors simply copy the first. The probability that the majority is correct is then the first inspector's probability, rather than the value calculated for three independent judgments. A unanimous result would add no independent check.

Between complete independence and complete copying lie many arrangements. Inspectors may share training, consult the same measurement, or review one another's reasoning. Those connections can be useful, but the aggregation rule should account for them. The mere number of voices leaves their dependence unspecified.

### Diversity needs a task

Different inspectors may notice different defects. Combining those observations can improve coverage. But diversity of unsupported guesses about a measurement does not necessarily improve its accuracy. Specify which differences supply relevant information or methods and how the group will use them.

A good procedure can preserve initial judgments, invite reasons, and distinguish repeated evidence from additional checks. Its aim is to make contributions available for assessment, rather than force a consensus before the information is examined.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the jury theorem establishes

The Condorcet jury theorem connects increasing majority reliability with independent judgments of adequate competence. In the standard idealization with a common probability above one half, reliability approaches one as the group grows. The threshold and independence assumptions do substantial work. The theorem is a conditional mathematical result, rather than empirical proof of the reliability of electorates, committees, or online votes. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.1].

It also concerns binary correctness. Aggregating estimates, combining probability distributions, and choosing policies with competing values require additional models. A successful majority rule for one task should not be exported without argument to a different task. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.1].

### Why group design matters

The social-epistemology entry surveys models of cognitive diversity, including the debated diversity-trumps-ability result. It describes both proposed benefits and criticisms of the representations and assumptions. Social diversity can supply cognitive diversity through different experiences, but the relationship and its epistemic value require an account of the inquiry. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.4].

Network models add another warning: rapid consensus can stop exploration before a better approach receives adequate testing. This is one possible mechanism of collective failure, rather than evidence that every consensus is suspect. The appropriate question is whether the agreement remains responsive to additional evidence and criticism. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.3].
