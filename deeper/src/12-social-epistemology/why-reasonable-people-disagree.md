> **In short.** Disagreement need not arise from dishonesty or stupidity. People can encounter different evidence, interpret a question differently, or attach different importance to a decision's consequences. Even a shared body of evidence raises a further debate about whether it permits more than one rational response. Explaining a disagreement does not automatically justify both sides. A useful investigation identifies what differs before judging whether that difference is defensible.

## Re-learn

### Locate the disagreement

Consider an invented debate about whether a library should extend its evening hours. One resident says attendance will be high; another says it will be low. They might rely on different neighborhoods' needs, different survey samples, or different meanings of *high*. Before deciding whose reasoning is better, establish which prediction they are making and which evidence each has seen.

Now suppose both predict the same attendance. One favors the extension because access for shift workers matters; the other opposes it because the cost would reduce children's services. Their disagreement concerns priorities as well as facts. More attendance data may help, but does not by itself settle how the competing benefits should be weighed.

Finally, suppose the residents share the survey, prediction, and priorities, yet assess the policy's promise differently. The puzzle becomes more like disagreement over the interpretation of shared evidence. Neither an information gap nor a difference in aims provides an easy explanation.

### Explanation is not vindication

Different experiences can explain why one resident noticed a need the other missed. They can also explain why someone acquired a mistaken generalization. A social history of a belief does not tell you whether the belief survives scrutiny. Ask what access the difference provides and whether the conclusion follows from it.

The same caution applies to sincere values. A disagreement about which service matters most can be meaningful without implying that every priority is equally defensible. Clarifying the dispute identifies the next argument needed; it does not replace that argument.

When the issue is mixed, separate its components. You might agree that evening attendance is uncertain, acknowledge a real access problem, and still disagree about allocating a limited budget. That account is more informative than treating the entire conversation as a single clash of opinions.

## Beyond the chapter

### Uniqueness and permissiveness

The uniqueness thesis says that a body of evidence justifies at most one competing doxastic attitude toward a proposition. Permissivist approaches allow more than one justified response in at least some cases. The issue concerns evidential support, rather than the claim that contradictory propositions become true for different people. Frances and Matheson explain how this debate bears on steadfast responses to disagreement. [@frances-matheson2026, § 5.2.1].

If evidence is permissive, a different response need not immediately show that one reasoner mishandled it. But permissiveness still requires limits: allowing some range of confidence does not establish that every confidence is justified. Nor does it show that new testimony from a capable person never supplies a reason to reconsider.

### A deeper dispute concerns standards

Some disagreements reach the principles governing evidence: which surveys deserve trust, which experiences count, or what makes an institution reliable. Philosophers call some such disputes deep disagreements. Their rational resolvability is contested, and disagreement about epistemic standards can also obstruct recognition of peerhood. [@frances-matheson2026, § 5.5.4].

This does not make investigation pointless. Identify a narrower proposition, compare methods, and seek a check that both parties can understand. Success at one point can improve the conversation without resolving every background dispute. The proposal is a practical strategy, rather than a theorem that all deep disagreements yield to it.
