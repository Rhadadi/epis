> **In short.** You can have good reasons to trust an answer that you cannot work out yourself. The difficulty is identifying the right person to trust, especially when apparent experts disagree. Expertise concerns a particular question, and knowing a great deal about one subject does not confer authority over everything. Arguments, independent agreement, credentials, interests, and records of performance provide different kinds of evidence about a source. Responsible dependence combines these clues while leaving room for correction.

## Re-learn

### Two questions about one answer

Imagine that a housing association must repair a retaining wall. This is an invented example. One engineer says the foundations require replacement; another recommends improving drainage. The residents cannot calculate the loads themselves. They face two questions: *What is wrong with the wall?* and *Who is well placed to answer that question?*

Answering the second need not require answering the first. Residents can check whether each engineer works on this kind of structure, whether each inspected the site, and whether a proposal addresses the same observed damage. They can establish that one estimate was made before a new survey arrived. None of those discoveries makes the residents structural engineers. Each changes their reasons for relying on a particular judgment.

The distinction also sets a limit. Being able to see that an explanation is clear does not establish that its calculations are sound. A compelling presentation and a correct diagnosis are different achievements. Conversely, an expert's inability to explain every step to a beginner does not establish that the diagnosis is false.

### Competence, access, and communication

Three questions often become tangled. Does the person possess the relevant skill? Did they have access to the evidence in this case? Are they communicating their judgment accurately? A skilled engineer who has not seen the survey may be less well placed than a colleague who has. An honest generalist can report what a specialist found without acquiring the specialist's competence.

You can therefore trust someone for a limited task. One resident might accurately report when cracks appeared while having no expertise about foundations. Their testimony supplies evidence to the engineers. The engineers' eventual interpretation supplies evidence to the residents. Authority moves in more than one direction.

### Disagreement changes the investigation

If the engineers disagree, first establish whether their recommendations actually conflict. Replacing the foundations and improving drainage could address different risks. They might also agree about physical probabilities but disagree about how much risk the association should accept. A technical disagreement, a difference in available information, and a disagreement about priorities call for different responses.

When the disagreement is genuine, investigate the sources of their judgments. A second opinion is most informative when it offers another assessment rather than a repetition of the first. Residents should also ask what observation could distinguish the diagnoses. They may be able to commission the relevant inspection even if they cannot interpret it unaided.

Sources for this layer: [@frances-matheson2026, § 4; @leonard2023, § 5; @anderson2024, § 1].

## The full story

### The problem is older than modern specialization

Locke distinguished seeing a demonstration from accepting the report of someone who had seen it. His example concerns a mathematical result: the person who follows the proof and the person who trusts a mathematician have different grounds for assent. Locke reserved *knowledge* for a demanding kind of evident connection, treating the latter person's assent under probability. Contemporary epistemologists often allow testimony to yield knowledge, but his comparison identifies the enduring difference between checking a conclusion and assessing its source. [@locke1690, book IV, ch. XV, §§ 1–4; @oconnor-etal2026, § 3.1].

::: original Locke on the assessment of witnesses
> In the testimony of others is to be considered: 1. The number. 2. The integrity. 3. The skill of the witnesses. 4. The design of the author, where it is a testimony out of a book cited. 5. The consistency of the parts, and circumstances of the relation. 6. Contrary testimonies.
:::
[@locke1690, book IV, ch. XV, § 4]. The list separates features of a report from features of its producer. Integrity and skill perform different roles: sincerity cannot compensate for inability, and ability cannot guarantee honest reporting. Contrary testimony matters because assessing one witness in isolation can conceal a conflict.

The modern novice/expert problem sharpens the difficulty. Goldman asks how people without expertise in a domain can reasonably identify those who possess it. As Frances and Matheson explain, the challenge is particularly pressing when alleged experts disagree. Hardwig emphasizes the dependence that this situation creates; Goldman explores evidence that can make a novice's choice less blind. These are questions about warranted reliance, rather than a promise that dependence can be eliminated. [@frances-matheson2026, § 4].

The topic belongs to a larger debate about testimony. Reductionists require adequate independent reasons to regard testimony as trustworthy; anti-reductionists allow an initial entitlement in the absence of relevant doubt. Neither description by itself settles which of two competing specialists a novice should believe. A dispute can supply the very reason for scrutiny that a default entitlement leaves open. The general permissibility of learning from others and the comparative credibility of particular sources remain different issues. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 3.1].

### Why expertise is relative to a question

Frances and Matheson distinguish several factors affecting an epistemic position: evidence, background knowledge, cognitive ability, attention, time, distractions, biases, and intellectual virtues. Someone can be better placed on one factor and worse placed on another. Relative standing consequently depends on the proposition being judged and the circumstances of its evaluation. A general description such as *intelligent* cannot settle all these comparisons. [@frances-matheson2026, § 4].

Return to the invented wall case. An engineer may understand the calculations exceptionally well but rely on an outdated drawing. Another may know the site well but lack the relevant design experience. If an updated measurement becomes available, the ranking of their present judgments can change without any change in their underlying qualifications. That is why a useful request for advice identifies both the kind of question and the evidence available.

Ballantyne's term *epistemic trespassing* concerns experts assuming authority beyond their expertise. Social epistemology treats this as a possible dysfunction in the distribution of credibility. The concern does not imply that experts must never cross disciplinary boundaries; it asks whether the authority claimed travels with adequate grounds. A contribution outside someone's specialty can be sound, but their standing in another specialty does not establish its soundness. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 5.3].

The reverse mistake is to recognize only credentialed knowledge. Anderson's account of situated knowers includes practical skills, first-person access, and relations to other inquirers. In our example, residents may know the timing and location of flooding better than a visiting specialist. Their access to those facts deserves attention even while technical interpretation requires additional expertise. Treating competence as task-specific permits collaboration without awarding everyone equal authority on every issue. [@anderson2024, § 1].

### Five kinds of evidence, with five different limits

Goldman's five sources of evidence, as presented in the disagreement literature, concern arguments, agreement from other experts, assessments by meta-experts, interests and biases, and past performance. They are possible inputs to a comparative judgment, rather than five independent guarantees. The table below states their practical roles and limitations. [@frances-matheson2026, § 4].

::: positions Assessing competing sources
| Clue | What it can reveal | What still needs checking |
|---|---|---|
| Arguments | How a diagnosis handles objections and observations | Whether accessible reasoning represents the technical case fairly |
| Agreement | Support from other qualified judges | Whether those judgments were formed independently |
| Credentials | Relevant assessment by a profession or institution | Whether the credential covers this question and present circumstances |
| Interests | Reasons for distortion or selective disclosure | Whether a possible influence actually undermines this report |
| Record | Performance on comparable judgments | Whether successes and failures were measured fairly |
:::
[@frances-matheson2026, § 4]. These clues can overlap. Credentials may depend partly on demonstrated performance, while agreement may come from people trained by the same institution. Counting each overlap as a fresh confirmation exaggerates the total support.

**Arguments.** A novice sometimes understands part of a technical exchange. In the wall case, residents could discover that one proposal assumes there is no water behind the wall, despite an inspection recording pooled water. They may also see whether the engineer acknowledges and answers the objection. This is meaningful scrutiny. It does not entitle them to infer that every explanation they find persuasive has passed a specialist's tests.

The distinction between evaluating a result and evaluating a discussion is essential. A specialist may make a mistake a beginner can expose, just as a beginner may be unable to assess a perfectly sound derivation. Successful explanation improves the novice's access to reasons. The novice still needs evidence about what the explanation omits and whether the relevant community regards the underlying method as appropriate. Goldman's inclusion of argument alongside other clues reflects that plurality of possible evidence. [@frances-matheson2026, § 4].

**Agreement.** A large number of qualified judgments can be informative, but their dependence matters. Frances and Matheson discuss the difference between opinions arrived at independently and opinions shaped by a shared background. Formal social epistemology likewise warns that agents may already have updated on one another before their reported credences are pooled. Numbers alone do not reveal how much information has entered the process. [@frances-matheson2026, § 6; @oconnor-etal2026, § 4.1].

Suppose five engineers endorse the drainage diagnosis, but four read only the fifth engineer's summary. Compare that with five engineers who inspect separately and reach the same conclusion by different measurements. The headcount is identical. The evidential structure differs. Independent review need not mean total isolation: collaboration can improve inquiry. The relevant question is which checks provide an additional opportunity to detect error.

**Credentials and meta-experts.** Recognized training and professional evaluation help a novice infer competence without repeating the evaluation personally. However, assessing an institution introduces another layer of dependence. Pauly's discussion of conspiracy theories makes this link explicit: trust in an expert can depend on trust in the body accrediting their expertise. The issue is especially visible when institutional credibility itself is contested. [@frances-matheson2026, § 4; @conspiracy-theories-iep, § 4].

This does not automatically generate a vicious regress. Many practical judgments use several partially independent signs: relevant qualification, checkable employment, transparent methods, and criticism from other practitioners. No sign is self-authenticating. Their combination can still make one option better supported than another. A demand for a completely independent verification of every supporting institution would recreate the impossible demand to inspect all knowledge personally.

**Interests.** A reason to favor a result is relevant to source assessment, but it is not a demonstration that the result is false. An engineer bidding for reconstruction may have an incentive that deserves disclosure. The appropriate response is to scrutinize the recommendation and seek an assessment with different interests. The same scrutiny should apply to an engineer financially committed to a cheaper remedy. Selectively investigating only unwelcome advice makes this safeguard unreliable.

Social epistemology's models show why financial influence need not take the form of fabricated evidence. Industry can shape the research communicated to others by supporting investigators with favorable methods or distributing a biased sample of real findings. These models illustrate possible mechanisms; they do not establish that any particular funding relationship has produced distortion. A source assessment should identify the relevant route of influence and the evidence for it. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 4.3].

**Records.** A relevant record concerns performance on comparable tasks. A history of identifying drainage failures could matter in the wall case. A history of accurate predictions about unrelated markets would have a different bearing. Even within a domain, the record needs a denominator: selected successes leave open how many unsuccessful judgments were omitted. These are practical requirements for making the performance clue informative, rather than a new empirical claim about experts generally.

Records also depend on assessable outcomes. A prediction made before inspection results become available can be checked against those results. Advice about a unique future event may be harder to evaluate, and an ambiguous statement may fit several outcomes after the fact. Recognizing those limits should lower confidence in a record's evidential value. It should not lead the novice to conclude that every imperfect record is equally useless.

### Reconstructing the case for responsible deference

The following argument captures a limited reason to defer. It concerns a novice's rational response to evidence about relative competence, without deciding the entire epistemology of authority.

::: argument When another judgment deserves greater weight
1. I need a judgment about a specified question, and my own assessment has relevant limitations.
2. I have good evidence that another inquirer is better placed to answer that question.
3. I lack a defeating reason to think that their present judgment is unreliable or misreported.
4. Their judgment therefore gives me a reason to revise my own assessment or rely on theirs.
:::
[@frances-matheson2026, § 4; @leonard2023, § 5]. The conclusion is deliberately modest: it identifies a reason, whose strength depends on the premises. It does not require unlimited obedience or an equal response to every confident speaker.

Premise 1 should be assessed concretely. A novice may have access to an important observation that an expert lacks, while lacking the theory needed to interpret it. Premise 2 requires more than the assertion that the speaker is eminent. Premise 3 leaves room for mistakes, conflicts, outdated information, or misunderstood questions. A defeater can concern the present testimony even if the expert remains generally competent.

The argument also explains why refusing all reliance can be unreasonable. If you have grounds to believe another person is better placed, privileging your own judgment solely because it is yours disregards those grounds. Yet the conclusion leaves open whether an authority's judgment supplements your other reasons or replaces some of them. That stronger question divides preemptive and non-preemptive accounts. [@leonard2023, § 5].

### Does authority replace your reasons?

On a preemptive account associated with Zagzebski, authoritative testimony can replace the hearer's other reasons concerning the proposition. The point is to avoid retaining an inferior first-person assessment as though it carried the same weight after recognizing a superior source. On non-preemptive accounts, the authority's testimony adds a strong reason that must be considered with the hearer's remaining evidence. Leonard presents both approaches and objections to preemption. [@leonard2023, § 5].

The wall case illustrates the pressure in both directions. If the residents repeatedly overrule specialist calculations with their unsupported intuitions, deference accomplishes little. But if the engineer's report assumes that the wall has never flooded, a resident's contrary observation should be communicated and assessed. Recognizing authority should not make relevant information disappear. The disagreement concerns how reasons are organized once expertise is recognized, rather than whether novices should disregard expertise altogether.

There is also a difference between believing an expert's factual assessment and granting them the final decision. Two engineers might agree about the chance of failure while residents differ over acceptable risk, costs, and disruption. Technical competence supplies information to collective deliberation. Democratic legitimacy and equal standing raise additional questions that a ranking of expertise does not answer. Social epistemologists explicitly examine this tension between expert authority and public deliberation. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 5.1].

### A consultation can change the question

A further practical difficulty is that advice can clarify what needs answering. The residents initially ask which repair to buy. The engineers might distinguish immediate stability, the cause of deterioration, and prevention of future damage. Evidence that resolves one question may leave another open. The novice should therefore record which judgment an expert is making before interpreting apparent disagreement as incompatible answers to a single question.

In the invented case, one engineer could recommend a temporary restriction while waiting for drainage measurements. Another could estimate the probable long-term repair. Those recommendations need not conflict. Alternatively, they could genuinely disagree about whether the wall is presently safe. Establishing the scope of the claim makes the comparison fairer and identifies the evidence a further assessment should obtain.

This distinction can also reveal an authority boundary. Recognizing technical competence does not establish who should set the association's priorities or decide how to distribute a financial burden. The epistemic literature on democratic expertise treats the relationship between specialized authority and deliberation as a further problem. A consultation becomes clearer when the expert marks which parts concern physical assessment and which parts depend on the decision-makers' aims. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 5.1].

A useful written record separates observations, interpretations, uncertainties, and recommendations. The residents can then ask whether the engineers relied on the same observations and whether a later measurement should change an earlier recommendation. This procedure does not independently verify the technical analysis. It preserves the information needed for a second expert to assess it and for the novice to understand why advice changed. Responsible dependence is often a matter of maintaining that route to revision.

### The distribution of credibility can itself be defective

Evidence about expertise is socially distributed. Anderson describes how dominant groups can promote markers of authority associated with themselves while restricting other groups' opportunities to acquire those markers. Prejudicial stereotypes can also cause genuine competence or honesty to be discounted. A novice who follows prestige mechanically can therefore reproduce a defect in the surrounding credibility system. [@anderson2024, § 9].

The response is not to reverse every credibility judgment according to identity. It is to investigate whether the apparent indicator tracks the competence and access actually required. In the wall case, a resident's unfamiliar accent does not undermine their observation of flooding. An engineer's relevant training can matter without making residents' evidence dispensable. Different contributions require different assessments; fairness helps prevent an irrelevant social signal from replacing that assessment.

Institutions can reduce the burden on novices by making methods, interests, criticism, and correction visible. Individual source assessment then operates within a process that allows errors to be discovered. Conversely, an institution that conceals disagreements or prevents challenge weakens the evidence that its authority is deserved. Social epistemology studies both the hearer's judgment and the design of systems that produce and distribute reliable information. [@oconnor-etal2026, § 5.1; @anderson2024, § 9].

## Beyond the chapter

### A decision can be urgent without making the evidence certain

Sometimes the association must act before the engineers agree. Keep the belief question distinct from the decision question: uncertainty about which diagnosis is correct can coexist with a justified precaution against a serious risk. This page supplies no engineering advice; its example shows why acknowledging uncertainty and choosing an action need not conflict. The broader distinction between belief disagreement and action disagreement appears in Frances and Matheson's account. [@frances-matheson2026, § 2].

### What the chapter simplifies

The five sources are a starting framework, not an algorithm that produces an expert ranking. Their evidential value depends on relevance, dependence, and the availability of a meaningful comparison. Expert advice can also involve several contributors, so reliability may depend on coordination as well as any one person's ability. Social accounts of testimony allow features of the speaker and environment to affect the epistemic standing of the hearer's belief. [@frances-matheson2026, § 4; @oconnor-etal2026, § 3.1].
