> **In short.** The chapter introduces three pairs of words that sound alike but answer three different questions about a statement. *A priori / a posteriori* asks **how you could come to know it**: by thinking, or by looking at the world. *Analytic / synthetic* asks **what makes it true**: the meanings of its words alone, or also how the world is. *Necessary / contingent* asks **whether it could have been false**. For centuries philosophers assumed the three always give the same answer. Much of the most important philosophy of the last 250 years comes from the cases where they seem not to.

## Re-learn

### Why this section is hard

The chapter's first examples ("All bachelors are unmarried", "7 + 5 = 12") come out the same way on all three tests, which hides how different the tests are. The words are Latin and Kantian, not everyday English. And each word has other everyday senses: "necessary" also means "required", and "a priori" is used loosely to mean "assumed in advance". It helps to attach one question and one quick test to each pair:

- **A priori / a posteriori** asks *how someone could know it*. The armchair test: could you know it in an armchair, once you understand the words, without checking anything?
- **Analytic / synthetic** asks *what makes it true*. The dictionary test: is it true just because of what the words mean?
- **Necessary / contingent** asks *whether it could have been otherwise*. The story test: is there any consistent story about how the world might have gone in which it is false?

The first is about **knowledge**, the second about **meaning**, the third about **possibility**. Different questions, so in principle different answers.

### Step by step

#### 1. A priori and a posteriori: how could you know it?

"If Ana is taller than Ben and Ben is taller than Carl, then Ana is taller than Carl" passes the armchair test: **a priori**. "Ana is taller than Ben" fails it; you have to look: **a posteriori**.

Experience can be needed to *understand* a sentence without being needed to *justify* it. You needed experience to learn the word "taller", but once you have it nothing further has to be observed. On the strict use of the term, introspection and memory count as experience too, so knowing that you are thirsty, or what you had for breakfast, is not a priori [@steup-neta2025, § 5.4].

#### 2. Analytic and synthetic: what makes it true?

Kant's own examples are still the clearest. "All bodies are extended" is analytic: to find the predicate, I only unpack my concept of a body, which already includes extension. "All bodies are heavy" is synthetic: weight is "something totally different from that which I think in the mere conception of a body", and I learn it by observation [@kant1787, Introduction, B 11–12]. Kant also called analytic judgments *explicative*, because they only spell out what we already think, and synthetic judgments *augmentative*, because they add something no analysis could have found [@kant1787, Introduction, B 11].

#### 3. Necessary and contingent: could it have been otherwise?

"Napoleon lost at Waterloo" is contingent: the Prussians might have arrived late. "7 + 5 = 12" is necessary: no story makes it false. Philosophers put this in terms of **possible worlds**: a necessary truth is true in every possible world, a contingent truth in ours but not in all [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1].

The chapter's "necessary" is *metaphysical* necessity, which should be kept apart from *logical* necessity (denying it is a contradiction) and *physical* necessity (it follows from the laws of nature) [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1]. And it has nothing to do with the "must" of "it must be raining", which only says what follows from what you know [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1].

#### 4. Why the three come apart

- **Necessary and a priori, but synthetic ("7 + 5 = 12")**, according to Kant: however long you analyse "the sum of seven and five", twelve is not in it; you have to count, adding units one at a time, for instance on your fingers [@kant1787, Introduction, B 15–16].
- **Necessary but a posteriori ("Water is H₂O")**: the word "water" picks out *the stuff, whatever it is,* that fills the lakes and falls as rain; chemistry then discovers what that stuff essentially is. The meaning is known a priori; "the essence of water must be discovered empirically" [@russell-bruce2024, § 2].
- **Contingent but a priori ("Stick S is one metre long at t₀")**: if "one metre" is *defined* as the length of S at t₀, you know without measuring that S is one metre long then, yet the stick could have been longer [@russell-bruce2024, § 2].
- **Contingent and a posteriori ("Paris is the capital of France")**: the ordinary case.

### Common confusions

- **"A priori" means innate.** No. Russell put it plainly: knowledge that is logically independent of experience is still "elicited and caused by experience", and it would be absurd to think "babies are born with a knowledge of everything which men know". That is why, he says, the word "innate" is no longer used for such knowledge, and "*a priori*" is "less objectionable" [@russell1912, ch. VII].
- **"A priori" means certain, or unrevisable.** No. It means justified independently of experience. Quine and the logical positivists "casually" redefined it as *unrevisable in the light of experience*, which would make anyone who calls something a priori claim infallibility about it [@rey2026, § 3.6.1]. You can add up a long column of figures wrongly.
- **Analytic means obvious, synthetic means surprising.** No. Analyses can be long and informative (the old "paradox of analysis"), and synthetic claims can be as safe as anything: "There have been black dogs" is not analytic, but no one would give it up [@rey2026, §§ 3.1, 3.6.1].
- **Necessary means certain; contingent means uncertain or random.** No. These are claims about the world, not about our confidence. You are certain that Paris is the capital of France, though it is contingent; an unproven conjecture in mathematics may be necessary and known to no one.
- **"True by definition" settles an argument.** Only about words. A definition can make "All bachelors are unmarried" true; it cannot make any claim about the world true, only decide what a word will mean.

## The full story

### How the distinctions developed

::: timeline Three hundred years in brief
- **1690** · Locke separates "trifling" propositions, which only repeat what a word means, from certain propositions that are instructive [@locke1690, IV.viii].
- **1714** · Leibniz: truths of reason, whose opposite is impossible, and truths of fact, whose opposite is possible [@leibniz1714, § 33].
- **1748** · Hume's fork: relations of ideas and matters of fact [@hume1748, IV.i.20–21].
- **1781/1787** · Kant names the analytic/synthetic distinction and asks how synthetic a priori judgments are possible [@kant1787, Introduction].
- **1843** · Mill argues that even arithmetic rests on experience [@mill1843, II.vi].
- **1884** · Frege redefines analyticity in terms of logic and definitions, and tries to show arithmetic is analytic [@rey2026, §§ 1.1–1.2].
- **1920s–30s** · The logical empiricists agree that "there was no synthetic a priori": logic and mathematics are analytic, true by the conventions of language [@creath2026, preamble, § 4.2].
- **1951** · Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" attacks the analytic/synthetic distinction itself [@quine1951].
- **1980** · Kripke's *Naming and Necessity*: necessary a posteriori and contingent a priori truths [@kripke1980].
- **1990s–2000s** · Two-dimensional semantics (Jackson, Chalmers) and Williamson's "armchair" knowledge reshape the debate [@schroeter2021, § 2.1; @mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.2].
:::

The story has a simple shape. Before Kant, the leading philosophers recognised two kinds of truth, and assumed the lines coincided. **Leibniz**: "Truths of reasoning are necessary and their opposite is impossible: truths of fact are contingent and their opposite is possible". A necessary truth can be proved "by analysis, resolving it into more simple ideas and truths, until we come to those which are primary" [@leibniz1714, § 33]. So for Leibniz necessity, reason and analysis went together, and so did contingency, fact and experience. **Hume** drew the same line as relations of ideas and matters of fact. Even **Locke**, as the original passage below shows, had noticed something that did not fit.

**Kant** broke the alignment by arguing that mathematics and the principles of natural science are synthetic yet a priori. Everything after him is a reaction. **Mill** went to the empiricist extreme: arithmetic itself is learned from experience. That "two pebbles and one pebble" make "three pebbles" is not an identity of names, since the two descriptions do not stand "for the same physical fact": it is "a truth known to us by early and constant experience: an inductive truth" [@mill1843, II.vi.2]. **Frege** went the other way, trying to show that arithmetic is analytic because it follows from logic and definitions, though for him geometry remained synthetic a priori [@creath2026, § 4.2]. The **logical empiricists**, Carnap above all, made the analytic do the work Kant had given the synthetic a priori: logic and mathematics, and perhaps much else that seemed immune to refutation, were analytic, "true in virtue of the conventions of language" [@creath2026, § 4.2]. That left one clean line again: analytic, a priori and necessary on one side; synthetic, a posteriori and contingent on the other.

Then the line came under attack from both directions. **Quine** denied that there is an analytic side at all. **Kripke** kept the categories but showed that they cross: some necessities are learned by experience, and some contingent truths can be known without it. And **Williamson** has questioned whether the a priori/a posteriori line marks any deep difference in how we know [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.2].

### Read the original

::: original Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book IV, chapter VIII (1690)

> "We can know then the truth of two sorts of propositions with perfect certainty. The one is, of those trifling propositions which have a certainty in them, but it is only a verbal certainty, but not instructive. And, secondly, we can know the truth, and so may be certain in propositions, which affirm something of another, which is a necessary consequence of its precise complex idea, but not contained in it: as that, the external angle of all triangles is bigger than either of the opposite internal angles." [@locke1690, IV.viii.8]

Locke has already drawn the line Kant will draw. His "trifling" propositions say "a part of the definition of the word defined", as when "the genus is predicated of the species" [@locke1690, IV.viii.4]; they are certain but teach nothing. He mocks the extreme case, "what is, is", as no better than a monkey "shifting his oyster from one hand to the other" [@locke1690, IV.viii.3]. But the triangle example is the important part: a truth that follows necessarily from an idea without being contained in it, and is "instructive real knowledge". Seventy years later, Kant would call such truths synthetic and a priori.

:::

::: original Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, section IV (1748)

> "All the objects of human reason or enquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to wit, *Relations of Ideas*, and *Matters of Fact*. Of the first kind are the sciences of Geometry, Algebra, and Arithmetic; and in short, every affirmation which is either intuitively or demonstratively certain. ... Propositions of this kind are discoverable by the mere operation of thought, without dependence on what is anywhere existent in the universe." [@hume1748, IV.i.20]
>
> "The contrary of every matter of fact is still possible; because it can never imply a contradiction ... *That the sun will not rise to-morrow* is no less intelligible a proposition, and implies no more contradiction than the affirmation, *that it will rise*." [@hume1748, IV.i.21]

Hume's two tests are the ancestors of two of the chapter's distinctions: relations of ideas are known by "the mere operation of thought" (a priori) and their denial is contradictory (necessary); matters of fact are known by experience, and their denial is always possible (contingent). There is no third box. That is why Hume concluded that no reasoning *a priori* can tell us what causes what: "A man must be very sagacious who could discover by reasoning that crystal is the effect of heat, and ice of cold" [@hume1748, IV.i.23–25]. Kant's whole project begins from the suspicion that Hume's fork is too simple.

:::

::: original Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Introduction (1787)

> "The proper problem of pure reason, then, is contained in the question: 'How are synthetical judgements à priori possible?' ... Among philosophers, David Hume came the nearest of all to this problem; yet it never acquired in his mind sufficient precision, nor did he regard the question in its universality." [@kant1787, Introduction, B 19]

For Kant, everything depends on this question: "the existence or downfall of the science of metaphysics" [@kant1787, Introduction, B 19]. Mathematics shows that synthetic a priori knowledge exists, since "7 + 5 = 12" and "a straight line between two points is the shortest" are, he argues, both synthetic and known a priori [@kant1787, Introduction, B 15–16]. His answer, worked out in the rest of the *Critique*, is that such knowledge is possible because the forms of our experience are contributed by the mind itself: space, for instance, is, in Michael Friedman's summary of Kant's view, "the pure form of our outer sensible intuition" [@shabel2025, § 3.2]. See [Kant](02-history-of-epistemology.md#kant) in Chapter 2.

:::

### The arguments, step by step

::: argument Kant: arithmetic is synthetic a priori
1. A judgment is analytic only if its predicate is contained in its subject concept, so that analysis alone reveals it [@kant1787, Introduction, B 10–11].
2. However long we analyse the concept of the sum of seven and five, we do not find the concept of twelve in it [@kant1787, Introduction, B 15].
3. So "7 + 5 = 12" is synthetic.
4. The judgment is necessary and universal, and necessity cannot be learned from experience [@kant1787, Introduction, B 3–4; @shabel2025, § 2.2].
5. So it is known a priori: it is synthetic a priori.
:::

The main replies attack premises 1 and 2. Frege rejected the containment test of premise 1: he counted as analytic whatever follows from logic plus definitions, and argued that arithmetic does, which would make "7 + 5 = 12" analytic after all [@rey2026, § 1.2]. His program foundered on Russell's paradox [@rey2026, § 3.2], but the idea that analyticity is about logic and definition, not about concepts inside concepts, survived. Mill attacked from the other side, denying that we know such truths independently of experience: the fundamental truths of arithmetic "all rest on the evidence of sense", which is how children are taught them, with balls and fingers; arithmetic, like all the "Deductive or Demonstrative Sciences", is at bottom an inductive science [@mill1843, II.vi.1–2]. An inductive truth ought to be open to correction by further experience; yet, as Georges Rey observes when discussing Quine's similar view, "There have, historically, been in fact virtually no revisions of mathematics on empirical grounds" [@rey2026, § 3.4].

::: argument Quine: no principled analytic/synthetic distinction
1. "Analytic" can be explained only through synonymy, definition or meaning.
2. Each of these can in turn be explained only through the others, or through analyticity itself: the notions bring "in each other's laundry" [@rey2026, § 3.5].
3. So there is no non-circular account of analyticity.
4. Moreover, "our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually, but only as a corporate body" [@rey2026, § 3.4], so "no statement is immune to revision" [@hylton-kemp2025, § 3.3].
5. So no statement is true by meaning alone, immune to experience. The distinction is a "dogma".
:::

Replies have come at every step. Against 1–3: Grice and Strawson (1956) and Putnam pointed out that "it is unlikely that so intuitively plausible a distinction should turn out to have no basis at all in fact" [@rey2026, § 3.5]. Others note that there may be explanatory circularities in the best of theories, though Rey grants that the circle here "seems particularly vicious" [@rey2026, § 3.5]. Against 4: Quine offered no argument for holism, and mathematics has "virtually no" history of revision on empirical grounds [@rey2026, § 3.4]. And against the conclusion, Rey notes that the unrevisability of analytic truths is better explained by their analyticity than the other way round [@rey2026, § 3.6.1].

Quine's argument was aimed above all at Carnap [@hylton-kemp2025, § 3.2], and seen in that light his most quoted claim, that "no statement is immune to revision", is less shocking than it sounds. Carnap agreed that any sentence can be revised; he held only that revising an analytic sentence means changing the language, and so the meanings of its words [@hylton-kemp2025, § 3.3]. The dispute is therefore about whether there is a notion of meaning clear enough to support the distinction. Quine's critics ask by what standard of clarity he finds it lacking; the answer, not explicit in "Two Dogmas", is his naturalistic standard [@hylton-kemp2025, § 3.3].

::: argument Kripke: water is necessarily H₂O, but we learned it by experiment
1. If water is H₂O, then necessarily water is H₂O. (Known "by a priori philosophical analysis": names for natural kinds pick out the same stuff in every possible world.)
2. Water is H₂O. (Known by chemistry.)
3. So necessarily, water is H₂O.
4. Since premise 2 is known only a posteriori, so is the conclusion: it is necessary and a posteriori.
:::

[@mallozzi-etal2024, § 2.3; @kripke1980]. The standard objections target premise 1 and step 4. Some philosophers deny that "Water is H₂O" is itself a necessary truth: what is necessary, and knowable a priori, is the general principle that the essence of any type of matter is given by its basic elements, while which elements water has is a contingent, empirical discovery [@russell-bruce2024, § 2]. Others (Salmon, Soames) argue that what we learn by experiment is that the *sentence* is true, while the *proposition* is the same one we already knew a priori; LaPorte calls the usual claim "controversial, though common" [@laporte2026, § 3.1]. The essentialist premise has a wider background too: since Kripke and Putnam, philosophers have defended claims about the essences of natural kinds, such as that in any possible world "anything that is not composed of molecules of H2O is not water" [@atkins-etal2026, § 1.4].

**Two-dimensional semantics** offers a different diagnosis of why the case feels puzzling. Before chemistry, it *might have turned out* that the watery stuff was something other than H₂O; in that sense "water is not H₂O" is conceivable. But given that the watery stuff is H₂O, there is no possible world in which *water* is something else; in that sense it is not. David Chalmers calls these **primary** and **secondary** conceivability, and holds that a sentence like "water is H₂O" has two contents, one knowable a priori and one necessary [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.1]. Lewis, Jackson and Chalmers argue that this shows how a posteriori necessities are "consistent with apriori access to truth-conditions" [@schroeter2021, preamble]. The claim that speakers always have such a priori access remains controversial, tied to "broader controversies about apriority, modality, semantic theory and philosophical methodology" [@schroeter2021, preamble].

### The positions

::: positions Where the main views stand

| View | Who | Core claim | The main objection or open question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rationalism | Kant; contemporary rationalists | There is synthetic a priori knowledge, in mathematics and beyond | Empiricists deny that reason alone can tell us about the world |
| Empiricism of Hume's kind | Hume | Only relations of ideas are knowable by reason; everything else by experience | Mathematics does not look like the mere unpacking of concepts (Kant) |
| Logical empiricism | Carnap, Ayer and others | Everything a priori is analytic, true by convention or meaning | Quine: no principled account of analyticity |
| Quine's naturalism | Quine | No sharp analytic/synthetic line; every belief revisable in principle | Ordinary speakers draw the line readily; mathematics is not revised on empirical grounds |
| Kripkean modal realism | Kripke | Necessity and a priority come apart: necessary a posteriori, contingent a priori | Disputes over essences, and over propositions versus sentences |
| Two-dimensionalism | Chalmers, Jackson | Each sentence has an a priori "primary" content and a necessary "secondary" content | Contested assumptions about meaning and a priori access |
| Armchair knowledge | Williamson | The a priori/a posteriori line "doesn't cut at the epistemic joints" | Debated, for instance between Boghossian and Williamson |

:::

### Where the debate stands

Three developments shape the debate now.

- **From meaning to knowledge.** Since Quine, discussion of the analytic has been dominated by "the epistemological issues about justifying our beliefs in necessary truths", not by the metaphysics of meaning [@rey2026, § 2]. The question is less "Is there analytic truth?" than "How do we know logic and mathematics?"
- **The epistemology of modality.** How we could know that something holds in *every* possible world has become a field of its own. The main proposals give conceivability a central role, and the central problem is how a fact about our minds can tell us about mind-independent possibility [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.1]. "Possible world" has been in the philosophical lexicon since Leibniz, but it became firmly entrenched with the formal semantics of modal logic [@menzel2025, § 1].
- **Armchair knowledge.** Williamson calls knowledge of what would happen in counterfactual situations "armchair" knowledge, and argues that much of it is neither strictly a priori nor strictly a posteriori, so the distinction "doesn't cut at the epistemic joints" [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.2].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **How we can know what is necessary at all.** To know a truth holds in *every* possible world seems to require more than observing this one. The main proposal is that **conceivability** guides us: if you can coherently conceive a situation, that is evidence that it is possible. The central problem is explaining how a fact about our minds can tell us about mind-independent possibility [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.1].
- **The kinds of necessity.** Some philosophers think metaphysical and logical necessity coincide; others that metaphysical and physical necessity coincide; still others doubt that metaphysical necessity is a separate notion at all [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1].
- **Non-Euclidean geometry and Kant.** It is often said that non-Euclidean geometry refuted Kant's claim that geometry is synthetic a priori. Contemporary Kant scholars broadly oppose that "long-standard story" [@shabel2025, § 3.1], and Rey adds that the usual account misreads the history: non-Euclidean geometries were developed as pure mathematics, and Einstein argued that physics supported one of them [@rey2026, § 3.4].
- **The epistemological turn.** Since Quine, the debate about analyticity has been mostly about how we justify beliefs in necessary truths, not about the metaphysics of meaning [@rey2026, § 2].
- **Necessity in Islamic philosophy.** Avicenna (Ibn Sina) built his metaphysics on a distinction close to the chapter's necessary/contingent, but with a twist. Everything that exists is necessary, "either per se or per aliud": either necessary in itself, needing no cause, as only the First Principle is, or *possible* in itself but made necessary by its cause. A possible thing "in itself ... does not exist and therefore always receives existence from something else" [@lizzini2026, §§ 3.3, 4.2–4.3]. For Avicenna, contingency is not a matter of being true in some worlds and false in others, but of depending on a cause; the distinction between a thing's possible essence and its existence explains "any causal relationship" [@lizzini2026, § 3.3].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Kant introduced the distinction."** Kant gave it its modern form and its name, but Locke's "trifling" propositions, Leibniz's truths of reason and Hume's relations of ideas all came first [@rey2026, § 1.1; @locke1690, IV.viii].
- **The table.** Placing "Water is H₂O" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus" in the necessary a posteriori cell is standard but contested, and the contingent a priori cell more so: Kripke's examples there "are perhaps more controversial", and many find them insignificant even if genuine [@laporte2026, §§ 3.1–3.2].
- **"Three great distinctions" as fixed categories.** Each line is itself disputed: whether there is an analytic/synthetic line (Quine), whether the a priori/a posteriori line is deep (Williamson), and how many kinds of necessity there are.
