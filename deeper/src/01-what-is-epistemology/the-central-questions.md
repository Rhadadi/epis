> **In short.** Epistemology's many topics come back to a few questions: What is knowledge? What makes a belief justified? Where does knowledge come from? How much do we know? Two of them seem to come before all the rest, *What do we know?* and *How do we know?*, and they trap us in a circle: to say what we know, we need a test for knowledge; to test the test, we need cases of knowledge. This is **the problem of the criterion**, one of the oldest puzzles in philosophy, and there is still no agreed way out.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. The circle

Suppose you want to sort your beliefs into knowledge and mere opinion. You need a criterion, say "knowledge is what you can prove". But how do you know that criterion is right? You would check it against clear cases of knowledge. But to pick out the clear cases, you need a criterion. Kevin McCain puts it as two "seemingly innocent questions" each of which seems to need an answer to the other first [@mccain-iep, introduction].

#### 2. Three ways to respond

Roderick Chisholm, who made the problem famous in modern philosophy, said there are only three [@mccain-iep, § 2]:

- **Particularism**: start from particular cases you are sure are knowledge ("I know I have hands"), and work out the criterion from them.
- **Methodism**: start from a criterion ("only what follows from clear and distinct ideas"), and use it to sort the cases.
- **Skepticism**: you cannot answer either question without the other, so you cannot answer either.

#### 3. Why it matters

The starting point shapes everything after. Descartes and Hume both started as methodists, and that choice committed them to **foundationalism**, the view that knowledge rests on a base of certain beliefs [@carter-sosa2026, § 3.1].

### Common confusions

- **The circle proves we know nothing.** No: that is only the skeptic's reading. On Chisholm's own reading, it shows that every answer starts from an assumption the other side rejects [@mccain-iep, § 2].
- **Particularism is just stubbornness.** Chisholm admitted that particularism begs the question, and defended it anyway on the ground that only it allows epistemology to make progress [@mccain-iep, § 2].
- **The table in the chapter lists separate problems.** The questions interlock: the answer to "what is knowledge?" constrains the answer to "can we know anything?", and so on.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **The ancient origin.** Sextus Empiricus presents the problem in Book 2 of the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* as the "wheel" or *diallelus*, in the debate between the Academic Skeptics and the Stoics [@mccain-iep, § 1]. The idea of a criterion goes back to Epicurus, who spoke of a *kanôn* ("measuring stick") and a *kritêrion*: the evident content of perception, against which claims about the non-evident are tested. The Stoic Zeno proposed instead the "cognitive impression" as the criterion of truth [@vogt2026, § 3.1].
- **Chisholm's frankness.** Robert Amico objected that Chisholm gave no independent reason to prefer particularism [@mccain-iep, § 2]. Chisholm's final word was that "the problem of the criterion has no solution" [@mccain-iep, § 2].
- **One problem or several?** Sometimes the problem is how to tell when we *know* (a question about knowledge), sometimes, as in Montaigne, how to tell *true from false appearances* (a question about truth). McCain suggests it may be "a set of related problems" [@mccain-iep, § 1].
- **The oldest definition of knowledge.** In Plato's *Theaetetus*, Socrates refutes the idea that knowledge is true opinion with the jurors who reach a true verdict from a persuasive speaker without having seen the crime; Theaetetus then proposes "true opinion, combined with definition or rational explanation" [@plato-theaetetus, 201a–d], the ancestor of the analysis of knowledge in Chapter 5.

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Philosophers have argued about their order for 2,500 years."** Not continuously. After Sextus, interest lapsed until Montaigne in the late 1500s, lapsed again until Hegel (1807), and was revived by Cardinal Mercier (1884) and P. Coffey (1917), well before Chisholm [@mccain-iep, § 1].
- **"Epistemology is the theory of knowledge."** Carter and Sosa call this "an oversimplified picture": epistemologists also study justification, understanding, rationality and intellectual character [@carter-sosa2026, §§ 2.1, 3.1].
