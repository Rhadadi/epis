> **In short.** Critical thinking asks "Is this true? How do they know? Should I trust this source?" Those are epistemology's questions, asked quickly. Epistemology supplies the standards: what counts as evidence, when a belief is justified, when testimony deserves trust. But critical thinking is more than knowing the standards. It is also a set of habits and attitudes, which is why the people who study it describe it more broadly than "applied epistemology".

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Every everyday dispute hides epistemological questions

The chapter's dinner-table dialogue ("My cousin lost 10 kg", "Anecdotes aren't evidence") packs in six of them: testimony, generalising from one case, cause and effect, a misheard claim, trust in experts, and how strong evidence must be. Spotting which question is in play is the first skill.

#### 2. Two of those questions are very old

- **When is someone's word a good reason?** Locke already listed what to weigh in testimony: "the number", "the integrity" and "the skill of the witnesses", the author's purpose, whether the story hangs together, and any contrary testimony [@locke1690, IV.xv.4].
- **How confident should I be?** Hume's answer is the motto of the whole field: "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" [@hume1748, X.i].

#### 3. Standards are not enough without habits

The research literature on critical thinking defines it, at its core, as "careful thinking directed to a goal" [@hitchcock2024, preamble], and treats it as a matter of **dispositions** as well as abilities: attentiveness, a habit of inquiry, open-mindedness, willingness to suspend judgment, wanting evidence for one's beliefs, and seeking the truth [@hitchcock2024, § 8.1]. Knowing that anecdotes are weak evidence does not help someone who never stops to ask.

### Common confusions

- **"Anecdotes aren't evidence."** Too strong, as the chapter says. In probability terms, evidence confirms a hypothesis to the degree that it raises its probability [@weisberg2021, § 1.2]; an anecdote usually raises it a little. But how much depends on how it reached you. If you would have heard of the cousin's success whether or not the diet works, because people pass on successes and forget failures, hearing it is almost as likely either way, and it supports the claim very little.
- **Being rational means being right.** No. Thomas Kelly, who takes Hume's sentence as his motto, warns that rationality "is no guarantee of correctness": evidence can mislead [@kelly2016, § 2].
- **Critical thinking means criticising.** It means evaluating carefully, including your own beliefs, and includes the willingness to suspend judgment [@hitchcock2024, § 8.1].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **The live debate behind "My cousin told me".** **Reductionists**, following Hume, hold that we may believe a speaker only if we have positive reasons, not themselves drawn from testimony, to think them reliable. **Non-reductionists**, following Thomas Reid, hold that we have a defeasible right to believe what we are told unless we have reason for doubt [@leonard2023, §§ 1.1–1.2]. Against reductionism: most of us could never check the people we learn from, so it leads to skepticism (C. A. J. Coady's argument). Against non-reductionism: it seems to license gullibility, toward an anonymous blogger for instance [@oconnor-etal2026, § 3.1; @leonard2023, § 1.2].
- **The dispositions that keep inquiry going.** Beyond starting to think critically, the literature lists dispositions that keep it on track: keeping focus on the question, and being willing to drop a strategy that is not working [@hitchcock2024, § 8.2]. Contemporary epistemology studies such traits directly, in virtue epistemology [@carter-sosa2026, § 2.1].
- **The word itself.** In Ferrier's *Institutes of Metaphysic*, "epistemology" names one of three divisions of philosophy: "The Epistemology, or theory of knowledge", "The Agnoiology, or theory of ignorance", and "The Ontology, or theory of being" [@ferrier1856, § 61]. Agnoiology, a theory of what can and cannot be *ignorance*, never caught on.

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Critical thinking is applied epistemology."** Largely true of its standards; not of its dispositions and practice, which is why educators test both abilities and attitudes [@hitchcock2024, preamble].
- **"Ferrier coined the English term."** The reference works go no further than saying that Ferrier was "the first philosopher in English to call the philosophy of knowledge 'epistemology'" [@keefe-iep, “Idealist Metaphysics”]. Whether anyone printed the word before him is a question this page does not settle.
