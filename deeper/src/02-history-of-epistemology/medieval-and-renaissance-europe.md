> **In short.** Medieval philosophers did not ignore skepticism; from the late thirteenth century it was one of their main concerns. Augustine looked for truths that no skeptic could doubt. Later scholastics worried that an all-powerful God could make us seem to see what is not there, and Nicholas of Autrecourt pushed that worry further than anyone before Hume. Renaissance Europe then rediscovered Sextus Empiricus, and Bacon turned the catalogue of human error into a programme for science. Descartes inherited all of this.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Augustine: find something no one can doubt

Augustine's first surviving work is a dialogue against the Academic skeptics. Their standard for knowledge was strict: if an image of a tree beside a house "could be caused by a dream, then the tree's being beside the house cannot be known, even if the tree is in fact beside the house" [@bolyard2025, § 2.1]. Augustine does not try to rescue all our ordinary beliefs. It is enough for him to show "the existence of some kind of knowledge that is immune to skeptical doubt" [@tornau2024, § 5.1]. He points to three kinds:

- self-referential knowledge (the skeptic "knows" the Stoic criterion he attacks);
- private knowledge ("I am certain that something appears white to me even if I am ignorant whether it is really white");
- logical and mathematical structures, known whether we are awake or dreaming [@tornau2024, § 5.1].

#### 2. Aquinas: knowledge in the ideal sense

Aquinas "shows no interest in trying to define anything like knowledge in its loose and popular sense". His ideal is *scientia*: "the apprehension, with certainty, of an entity, event or proposition, based on a demonstrative proof" grounded in the nature of the thing. The model comes from Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* [@pasnau2024, § 6.3].

#### 3. Ockham's razor, properly stated

The slogan that one "must not multiply entities beyond necessity" is "nowhere to be found in his texts", although the sentiment is Ockham's. His own fullest version names the conditions under which we may posit more entities: reason, experience, and the infallible authority of Scripture. Unless one of these supports a putative kind of entity, "we ought not endorse a claim that supposes that sort of entity to exist" [@spade-pelletier2025, § 4.1].

### Common confusions

- **Skepticism vanished in the Middle Ages.** Surveys often leave that impression, Bolyard notes, and it is true that no medieval thinker "professed an active allegiance to a systematic philosophical skepticism". But skepticism "continued to swirl in the thoughts of many of the best philosophers of the period", and from the late thirteenth century "skeptical issues began to exert a dominant and wide influence on epistemological discussions", in Henry of Ghent, Scotus, Ockham, Auriol, Buridan and Autrecourt. "Versions of both his cogito and Evil Demon arguments may be found in the works of medieval philosophers" [@bolyard2025, preamble].
- **Divine illumination is a medieval Christian oddity.** Pasnau notes that illumination "played a prominent part in ancient Greek philosophy, in the later Greek commentary tradition, in neo-Platonism, and in medieval Islamic philosophy" [@pasnau2025, § 1]. He calls it "the oldest and most influential alternative to naturalism in the areas of mind and knowledge" [@pasnau2025, preamble].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Autrecourt's skepticism, in his words.** In his First Letter, Autrecourt argues that his opponent's view implies "you are not certain whether anything appears to you at all". His Second Letter keeps only the principle of non-contradiction and what follows from it, and it denies that from the existence of one thing "it cannot be inferred evidently ... that there is some other thing". Bolyard calls this "the most radical form of skepticism found at any point during the Middle Ages" [@bolyard2025, § 4.2.4].
- **Buridan's reply: lower the bar.** Buridan answered with a "virtual infinity of self-evident principles through the senses, or through experience". Since God could deceive us, he allowed that sensory knowledge reaches only "conditional evidentness"; "mathematical certainty is not expected in every subject" [@bolyard2025, § 4.2.4]. This is an early form of [fallibilism](06-justification.md#fallibilism).
- **The stock illusions.** Peter Auriol's catalogue of illusions includes dreams, mirror images, "the bent appearance of a straight stick that is immersed in water", and the fiery circle made by whirling a burning stick [@bolyard2025, § 4.2.2]. These became the stock examples of later debates about [perception](07-sources-of-knowledge.md#perception).
- **The ant, the spider and the bee.** Bacon compared the "empirics" to ants, which "only heap up and use their store", and the dogmatists to spiders, which "spin out their own webs". "The bee, a mean between both, extracts matter from the flowers of the garden and the field, but works and fashions it by its own efforts" [@bacon1620, I.95].

::: original Bacon on the votive tablets (Novum Organum I.46)
> It was well answered by him who was shown in a temple the votive tablets suspended by such as had escaped the peril of shipwreck, and was pressed as to whether he would then recognize the power of the gods, by an inquiry, But where are the portraits of those who have perished in spite of their vows? All superstition is much the same, whether it be that of astrology, dreams, omens, retributive judgment, or the like, in all of which the deluded believers observe events which are fulfilled, but neglect and pass over their failure, though it be much more common.

This is the same aphorism the chapter quotes, in Devey's older translation. The story is about missing evidence: the drowned left no tablets. Bacon ends the aphorism with the rule that "in establishing any true axiom the negative instance is the most powerful", the idea Popper would build on (see [the twentieth century](deeper:02-history-of-epistemology/the-twentieth-century)).

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Montaigne's motto.** Around 1576 Montaigne had a medal struck with "Epecho", "I abstain" in Greek, and "Que sais-je?" [@foglia-ferrari2026, § 4]. But commentators "now agree upon the fact that Montaigne largely transformed the type of scepticism he borrowed from Sextus": for him "the two sides of the scale are never perfectly balanced", which undermines the Pyrrhonian mechanism of equally strong opposing arguments [@foglia-ferrari2026, § 4]. Popkin read his skepticism as a strategy for "fideism": because reason cannot demonstrate religious dogmas, "we must rely on spiritual revelation and faith" [@foglia-ferrari2026, § 4].
- **Augustine and the Academy.** Augustine's view of his opponents was idiosyncratic. He held that the Academics were "crypto-Platonists" who used skeptical arguments only to combat the materialist schools [@tornau2024, § 5.1].
