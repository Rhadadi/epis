> **In short.** In the 17th and 18th centuries, philosophers asked what, if anything, we can know with certainty, and how. The chapter tells the story figure by figure: Descartes' doubt, Locke's empiricism, Berkeley's idealism, Hume's skepticism, Reid's common sense and Kant's synthesis. This page shows the thread that ties them together, an assumption most of them shared about what we perceive, and looks closely at three arguments that are still discussed: the Cartesian circle, Hume's argument about induction, and Reid's reply to the skeptic.

## Re-learn

### Why this section is hard

There are many names and many -isms, and the usual labels, rationalist and empiricist, suggest two teams. It is easier to follow one question through the period: *if what I am directly aware of is my own ideas, how do I know anything about the world beyond them?* Each figure can be read as an answer.

### Step by step

#### 1. Descartes doubts in order to build

Descartes used doubt as a tool, not as a conclusion. Unlike "the sceptics, who doubt only for the sake of doubting", he aimed "to reach certainty – to cast aside the loose earth and sand so as to come upon rock or clay". As Lex Newman puts it, his innovation "is to employ demolition for constructive ends" [@newman2023, § 2.2].

#### 2. The first certainty is the thinker's own existence

Even while supposing everything false, Descartes found that he who was doing the supposing must exist. In the *Discourse* he calls "I think, therefore I am" so certain that "no ground of doubt, however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it" [@descartes1637, Part IV].

#### 3. The empiricists start from experience

Locke traced all ideas to experience and separated knowledge from probability. For probable matters he asked us to weigh "the conformity of the proposition to our knowledge, observation and experience, and the testimony of others" [@uzgalis2024, § 2.5]. Berkeley went further: there are no mind-independent material things; "to be is to be perceived (or to perceive)" [@downing-hilbert2026, § 2].

#### 4. Hume finds no reason behind induction

Hume argued that our inferences from past to future cannot rest on reason, and that our inductive habits are due to "custom or habit" instead [@qu-radcliffe2026, § 2.3].

#### 5. Reid and Kant change the question

Reid blamed the whole problem on a shared assumption, which he called the "Way of Ideas" [@nichols-yaffe2023, § 2]. Kant proposed that objects as we know them must conform to our cognition, not the other way round [@kant1787, Preface to the second edition].

### Common confusions

- **Descartes was a skeptic.** He used skeptical arguments to test candidate foundations, not to end in doubt [@newman2023, § 2.2].
- **Berkeley denied that stones are hard.** He denied only that there are "mind-independent things", things that "would exist whether or not any thinking things (minds) existed" [@downing-hilbert2026, § 2].
- **Hume told us to stop trusting induction.** He thought we could not, and should not try: "Nature is always too strong for principle" [@hume1748, XII.ii]. He recommended a "mitigated" scepticism, "both durable and useful" [@hume1748, XII.iii].
- **Rationalists and empiricists disagreed about everything.** They shared the assumption Reid attacked (see "The full story").

## The full story

### How the ideas developed

::: timeline From the new science to Kant
- **1620** · Bacon's *Novum Organum* proposes a new method of inquiry [@bacon1620].
- **1637** · Descartes' *Discourse on the Method*, with "I think, therefore I am" [@descartes1637, Part IV].
- **1689** · Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* [@uzgalis2024, preamble].
- **1704** · Leibniz writes the *New Essays* in reply to Locke; they are published in 1765 [@look2020, § 2; @leibniz1765].
- **1710** · Berkeley's *Principles of Human Knowledge* [@berkeley1710].
- **1739–48** · Hume's *Treatise* and *Enquiry* [@hume1739; @hume1748].
- **1764** · Reid's *Inquiry into the Human Mind, on the Principles of Common Sense* [@reid1764].
- **1781/1787** · Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* [@kant1787].
:::

The figures of this period disagreed sharply, but most of them accepted a common picture of the mind. Reid called it the "Way of Ideas": its "principle commitment is to mental representations, called 'ideas' or 'images', that are believed to mediate all our experience of the world" [@nichols-yaffe2023, § 2]. On that picture, when I perceive a pillow, "my perceptual mental state is not about the pillow but rather it is about my idea or mental representation of the pillow". Reid notes that this structure "is explicitly endorsed by Descartes, Locke, Berkeley, Hume and many other Early Modern philosophers, as well as many living philosophers" [@nichols-yaffe2023, § 3.1].

Seen this way, the period has a plot. Descartes needed a non-deceiving God to get from his ideas to the world [@newman2023, § 8]. The mechanists, Descartes and Locke among them, held that bodies have only primary qualities such as figure, solidity and motion, while colour and taste are mind-dependent [@downing-hilbert2026, § 2.2.2]. Berkeley argued that "the same sorts of relativity arguments that were made against secondary qualities can be made against primary ones" [@downing-hilbert2026, § 2.2.2], and concluded that there is no matter beyond our ideas at all, charging that materialism promotes skepticism and atheism [@downing-hilbert2026, § 2]. Hume pushed the same tools to skeptical conclusions about causation and induction. Reid rejected the starting point. Kant kept the idea that the mind shapes experience but turned it into a source of a priori knowledge.

### Read the original

::: original Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part IV (1637)

> "But immediately upon this I observed that, whilst I thus wished to think that all was false, it was absolutely necessary that I, who thus thought, should be somewhat; and as I observed that this truth, I think, therefore I am (COGITO ERGO SUM), was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt, however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it, I concluded that I might, without scruple, accept it as the first principle of the philosophy of which I was in search." [@descartes1637, Part IV]

Notice two things. First, the certainty is tied to the act of thinking: the claim is not that Descartes exists necessarily, but that he cannot coherently doubt his existence while doubting. Second, he goes straight on to a much bigger conclusion: that "I" am "a substance whose whole essence or nature consists only in thinking", "wholly distinct from the body" [@descartes1637, Part IV].

:::

::: original Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the second edition (1787)

> "It has hitherto been assumed that our cognition must conform to the objects; but all attempts to ascertain anything about these objects *à priori*, by means of conceptions, and thus to extend the range of our knowledge, have been rendered abortive by this assumption. Let us then make the experiment whether we may not be more successful in metaphysics, if we assume that the objects must conform to our cognition." [@kant1787, Preface to the second edition]

Kant compares this to Copernicus, who explained the motions of the heavens by moving the observer instead of the stars [@kant1787, Preface to the second edition]. Note the word "experiment": Kant presents the reversal as a hypothesis to be judged by whether it explains how a priori knowledge is possible.

:::

### The arguments, step by step

::: argument The Cartesian circle
1. Descartes argues from premises he clearly and distinctly perceives to the conclusion that an all-perfect God exists.
2. He then argues from God's existence to the rule that whatever is clearly and distinctly perceived is true.
3. If step 1 relies on that rule, the argument assumes what it sets out to prove.
:::

[@newman2023, § 6.1]. Newman calls the two steps "arcs" and notes that everyone agrees the argument has this shape: "The question of interest concerns whether, strictly speaking, these arcs form an epistemic circle." It depends on how premise 1 is read. On the "Vicious Circularity" reading, the premises of the proof of God are "accepted only because of having first accepted the general veracity of propositions that are clearly and distinctly perceived"; other readings deny this, and so deny that the circle is vicious [@newman2023, § 6.1]. Michael Della Rocca has argued that a further circle arises in the Fourth Meditation, one "more difficult to get out of than the traditional problem of circularity" [@newman2023, § 6.3].

::: argument Hume: induction is not founded on reason
1. Every inference from observed to unobserved cases assumes that the future will resemble the past (the Uniformity Principle).
2. The Uniformity Principle is not a relation of ideas: its negation is conceivable.
3. Any probable argument for it would itself presuppose it, and so be circular.
4. So the Uniformity Principle, and with it induction, is not founded on reason; our inductive habits are due to "custom or habit".
:::

[@qu-radcliffe2026, § 2.3]. Hume scholars disagree about what the conclusion means. "There is considerable debate regarding whether Hume's argument that induction is not founded on reasoning establishes a psychological fact—that is, that reason does not cause the mind to take a crucial inductive step—or a sceptical one—that is, that reason cannot justify our inductive inferences" [@qu-radcliffe2026, § 2.3]. The full argument and the replies to it are in [Hume's problem of induction](09-induction-probability-bayes.md#humes-problem-of-induction).

::: argument Reid's parity argument
1. The skeptic trusts reason but demands that every belief from perception be grounded on reason.
2. Reason and perception "came both out of the same shop, and were made by the same artist".
3. So there is no principled ground for trusting one and doubting the other.
4. So either the skeptic must doubt reason too, or he must grant perception the same initial trust.
:::

[@nichols-yaffe2023, § 3.5]. Reid's own words: "Why, sir, should I believe the faculty of reason more than that of perception?" [@nichols-yaffe2023, § 3.5]. The argument does not prove that perception is reliable. It shows that the skeptic's selective trust needs a defence it does not have.

### Where the debate stands

- **The circle.** Interpreters still divide over whether Descartes' proof is viciously circular, and whether other circles lurk in the *Meditations* [@newman2023, §§ 6.1, 6.3].
- **Hume as skeptic or as naturalist.** Whether his conclusion about induction is psychological or skeptical remains disputed [@qu-radcliffe2026, § 2.3].
- **Reid's target is still alive.** The representational picture he attacked is held by "many living philosophers" [@nichols-yaffe2023, § 3.1], so the choice between it and his direct realism is a live one (see [Perception](07-sources-of-knowledge.md#perception) in Chapter 7).

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Leibniz's marble.** Against Locke's blank slate, Leibniz compared the mind to a block of marble with veins: if veins "should indicate the figure of Hercules rather than other figures", Hercules "would be in it as in some sense innate, although it would be needful to labor to discover these veins". Ideas are innate "as inclinations, dispositions, habits, or natural potentialities, and not as actions" [@leibniz1765, Preface].
- **Locke on tolerance.** Locke drew a practical lesson from probability: "We should, Locke also suggests, be tolerant of differing opinions as we have more reason to retain the opinions we have than to give them up to strangers or adversaries who may well have some interest in our doing so" [@uzgalis2024, § 2.5].
- **Descartes the architect.** Descartes described his method in a building image: he began "by taking everything that was doubtful and throwing it out, like sand" [@newman2023, § 2].
- **Hume's scepticism as a temperament.** For Hume, mitigated scepticism corrects the "affirmative and dogmatical" tendencies of most people, who "see objects only on one side" [@hume1748, XII.iii].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Rationalists against empiricists.** The labels mark a real disagreement about innate ideas and the role of reason, but they hide how much the two sides shared: the Way of Ideas was common ground [@nichols-yaffe2023, § 3.1].
- **"Kant reconciled empiricism and rationalism."** Kant himself presented his reversal as an "experiment" [@kant1787, Preface to the second edition], and its price, that we know things only as they appear to us, was controversial from the start.
