> **In short.** After decades of trying to define knowledge, epistemology spread out. The chapter lists the new directions: virtue, knowledge-first, social, formal, feminist and experimental epistemology, pragmatic encroachment, and applied work. This page explains what drove two of them, where they disagree with older views, and how the experimental story about Gettier cases has changed as more data came in.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. From analysing knowledge to using it

After Gettier, much effort went into finding the missing condition for knowledge (see [Chapter 5](05-the-nature-of-knowledge.md)). The **knowledge-first** programme, "championed especially by Timothy Williamson's 2000 book Knowledge and Its Limits", supposes "that the project of analyzing knowledge was a mistake" and uses knowledge to explain other notions instead [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 11]. There is "no very straightforward argument" for this: its case "consists largely in the attempted demonstration of the theoretical success of the knowledge first stance" [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 11].

#### 2. Widening the subject

The other directions widen what epistemology looks at. Social epistemology investigates "the epistemic effects of social interactions, practices, norms, and systems" [@oconnor-etal2026, preamble]. Formal epistemology uses "tools from math and logic" [@weisberg2021, preamble]. Feminist epistemology's central concept is "situated knowledge" [@anderson2024, preamble]. Virtue epistemology models knowledge on skill: "Apt belief, then, is belief that is true because competent" [@turri-etal2021, § 5].

### Common confusions

- **These are rival schools.** Mostly they are overlapping projects. Feminist epistemology "is thus a branch of social epistemology" [@anderson2024, § 1].
- **Pragmatic encroachment says that high stakes make you look harder.** That much is "trivial". The claim is stronger: "A difference in pragmatic circumstances can constitute a difference in knowledge" [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12]. Its stock example is Jason Stanley's pair of bank cases, alike in evidence but different in what is at stake [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 12].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Experiments on stakes.** Contextualism and pragmatic encroachment rest partly on intuitions about such cases. But early experimental studies "reported that participants were similarly inclined to attribute knowledge across cases with shifting stakes" [@carter-sosa2026, § 3.3].
- **Experiments on fake barns.** A common position is that a belief that could too easily have been wrong is not knowledge. Experiments "have pressed back against this orthodoxy": in one study, 80% of participants attributed knowledge in a fake-barn case [@carter-sosa2026, § 3.3].
- **Rival strategies for the same data.** Contextualism and pragmatic encroachment address "some of the same 'shifty' patterns"; contextualism came first, and encroachment theorists wanted to explain the patterns "without contextualism's semantic commitments" [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 13].
- **Accuracy.** A central result of formal epistemology is Joyce's: a degree-of-belief function obeys the probability calculus if and only if no alternative is "at least as accurate in each, and strictly more accurate in some, possible world" [@geninhuber2026, § 3.1.3].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Gettier intuitions are broadly shared."** The story has three steps. Weinberg, Nichols and Stich (2001) reported that participants with East and South Asian backgrounds were more inclined than Western ones to attribute knowledge in Gettier cases. The paper "generated debate" and further studies. Machery and colleagues (2017) then "presented evidence that the Gettier intuition was present across four cultures speaking different languages: Brazil, India, Japan, and the USA" [@carter-sosa2026, § 3.3]. So the later study covers four cultures, not "many countries".
- **What the early results mean.** Georges Rey draws a modest moral from them: determining meaning and analytic truth "can be regarded as a far more difficult empirical question than philosophers have traditionally supposed" [@rey2026, § 4.1].
