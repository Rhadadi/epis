> **In short.** In the nineteenth century, epistemology turned to how knowledge grows. Thinkers stopped asking mainly whether certainty is possible and asked instead how inquiry, especially science, actually makes progress. Whewell and Mill argued about what induction is. Peirce made fallibilism a working method rather than a counsel of despair, and Dewey turned that method into a pattern of inquiry. The chapter's thread is that knowledge is something we do, revise and argue over, not a stock we have or lack.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Mill: even mathematics comes from experience

Mill was a thoroughgoing empiricist and a naturalist. Where we gain genuinely new knowledge, "in cases of mathematics and geometry, for instance—we must, at some level, be reasoning inductively" [@macleod2020, § 3.1]. His methods for finding causes follow the same spirit. The Method of Difference says that where the only feature distinguishing cases in which *a* occurs from cases in which it does not is the presence of *A*, "there is reason to think that A is an indispensable part of the cause of a" [@macleod2020, § 3.3].

#### 2. Whewell: the mind supplies the idea

Whewell disagreed. Facts do not add up to a law on their own; the scientist "superinduces" a conception that binds them together, a process he called **colligation**. "Thus the known points of the Martian orbit were colligated by Kepler using the conception of an elliptical curve" [@snyder2023, § 2]. The best evidence for such a conception is a **consilience**, a "jumping together" of inductions: a law built to colligate one class of facts turns out to colligate another class as well [@snyder2023, § 3].

#### 3. Peirce: fallible, but not skeptical

Peirce insisted that at any point "we are only at a provisional stage" of inquiry, but he was "far from being an epistemological pessimist or sceptic". Every genuine question can be answered in principle, or at least "should not be assumed to be unanswerable", hence his first principle of reason: "Do not block the way of inquiry!" [@burch2024, § 4]. Inquiry has three phases: abduction (conjecture), deduction (what should follow if the conjecture is true) and induction (testing) [@burch2024, § 4].

#### 4. Dewey: inquiry has a pattern

Dewey's inquiry has five phases. It begins (1) with a felt sense that something is amiss. Then (2) a problem is formulated, and "problems do not preexist inquiry, as typically assumed". Next (3) a hypothesis is constructed and (4) its implications are reasoned out, and finally (5) it is tested in action [@hildebrand2026, § 4.3].

### Common confusions

- **Mill's free-speech argument is a single argument.** Mill called *On Liberty* "a kind of philosophic text-book of a single truth", but Macleod notes that this "perhaps belies the fact that there are many distinct arguments and conclusions drawn throughout the text" [@macleod2020, § 4.5].
- **Pragmatism says a belief is true if it is useful to believe.** James's view is subtler. Truth is "a species of the good", but truths are goods because we can "ride" on them into the future without being unpleasantly surprised. James also recognised "resisting factors in every experience of truth-making", including our sensations and the whole body of our prior beliefs [@goodman2024, § Pragmatism (1907)].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Comte and positivism.** The law of the three stages says that humanity passes through "the theological, the metaphysical, and the positive" stages; the first is the mind's necessary starting point, the last "its normal state" [@bourdeau2026, § 4.1]. Comte's positivism was later "taken over by neo-positivism in philosophy of science", the [logical positivism](02-history-of-epistemology.md#logical-positivism) of the next century [@bourdeau2026, § 1].
- **The Whewell–Mill debate.** Mill attacked Whewell's philosophy of science in his *System of Logic*, "causing an interesting and fruitful debate between them over the nature of inductive reasoning" [@snyder2023, preamble]. Whewell's Fundamental Ideas are "supplied by the mind itself", not, as Mill and Herschel protested, received from observation [@snyder2023, § 2]. Snyder judges that Whewell's view of induction has the advantage "of allowing the inference to unobservable properties and entities" [@snyder2023, § 2].
- **Pragmatic meaning.** For Peirce a meaningful conception must have some experiential "cash value", and its entire meaning lies in "the totality of such specifications of possible observations" [@burch2024, § 4]. Yet he was "not a simple operationalist" and believed in the reality of abstractions [@burch2024, § 4].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Mill's four reasons.** The chapter lists four reasons for not silencing an opinion. Mill's own structure is "a proof from the exhaustion of cases": any opinion that might be suppressed is "(i) true, (ii) false, or (iii) partially true", and in each case its assertion helps us discover or keep the truth [@macleod2020, § 4.5]. The "dead dogma" worry belongs to case (ii): without active defence of a truth, knowledge is reduced to "phrases retained by rote" [@macleod2020, § 4.5].
- **"Scientist."** Whewell invented the English word in 1833; before then the only terms in use "were “natural philosopher” and “man of science”" [@snyder2023, preamble].
