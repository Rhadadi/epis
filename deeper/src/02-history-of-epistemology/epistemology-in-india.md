> **In short.** Classical Indian epistemology is organised around the *pramāṇas*, the sources of knowledge. The chapter's table shows which sources each school accepted. What the table cannot show is the arguments behind it: whether testimony is just a kind of inference, whether a skeptic can even state his doubts, and what to say about lucky true beliefs. Indian philosophers argued about all three for more than a thousand years, and their answers are now discussed alongside modern epistemology.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. A pramāṇa is a source of knowledge

Classical Indian philosophers theorised about "knowledge-generating processes, called pramāṇa, 'knowledge sources.' The principal candidates are perception, inference, and testimony" [@phillips-vaidya2026, preamble]. The schools agreed that knowledge comes from such sources; they disagreed about how many there are and which reduce to which.

#### 2. The debates are about reduction

Take testimony. Vaiśeṣika and Yogācāra philosophers held that what you learn from a speaker is really learned by inference: the speaker is trustworthy, the speaker said *p*, so *p*. Nyāya resisted this from its earliest text, arguing that it "conflates inferential certification of the truth of p with testimonial knowledge that p at a first or unreflective level" [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 6]. The founding definition is Gautama's: "Testimony is the (true) statement of an expert (āpta)" [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 6].

#### 3. Inference rests on generalisation

The stock inference ("the hill has fire, because it has smoke, as in a kitchen") depends on a pervasion, smoke everywhere going with fire. That pervasion "is known typically through generalization from cases", and the kitchen hearth counts as an example because it is relevantly similar to the hill [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 7.1].

### Common confusions

- **The Cārvāka were simply skeptics.** No. They accepted perception. They rejected inference because "Inference depends upon generalizations which outstrip perceptual evidence" [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 2]. The usual reply was practical: we could not act as we do without inference, and the skeptic relies on generalisations himself "when he opens his mouth to voice his skepticism, by using words with repeatable meanings" [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 2].
- **Admitting that inference can fail is admitting skepticism.** Buddhist and Nyāya philosophers turned the Cārvāka argument into "an argument for fallibilism about inference". The very idea of a fallacious reason presupposes a genuine one [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 2].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **The regress, and the reply.** Nāgārjuna "identifies a problem of a justification regress in the pramāṇa program" in his *Vigrahavyāvartanī*. The *Nyāya-sūtra* argues that this kind of skepticism is self-defeating, but the problems he raised, taken up by inheritors such as Śrīharṣa, occupied philosophers of several schools for centuries [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 2].
- **Rumour.** Nyāya even has a view about rumour, defined as "a testimony chain whose originator is unknown". Nyāya regards it as presumptively true, in keeping with its generally trusting theory of testimony [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 7.4]. A useful contrast with [misinformation](12-social-epistemology.md#misinformation-disinformation-and-propaganda) today.
- **Luck, then and now.** Recent work brings the Gettier debate into Nyāya. Das draws on Gaṅgeśa to defend "epistemic localism, the thesis that upstream causal factors play no epistemically significant role in the production of knowledge" [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 9.1]. Others compare Nyāya theories of perceptual error with modern disjunctivism [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 9.3].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Dharmottara's mirage.** The chapter's Gettier-style case is real. Ichikawa and Steup give it as an example of such cases being "discussed since antiquity": we see what looks like water, it is a mirage, but "when we reach the spot, we are lucky and find water right there under a rock" [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 3]. The case also shows why some modern analyses fail: the belief is insensitive, since "if there were no water there, you would have held the same belief on the same grounds" [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.1].
- **The table of sources.** The lists were contested within schools as well as between them. Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta accept analogy as a source; Nyāya accepts it only in a reinterpreted form, restricted to "a subject's learning the meaning of a word" [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 7.1].
