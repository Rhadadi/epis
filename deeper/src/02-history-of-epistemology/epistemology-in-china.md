> **In short.** Classical Chinese thinkers asked less "What is knowledge?" than "How do we come to know what to do, and what gets in the way?" The chapter gives four examples: Confucius on admitting ignorance, the Mohists' three tests for a claim, Zhuangzi's dream and fish, and Xunzi on the blinded mind. This page explains each a little further and notes where the chapter's paraphrases differ from the scholarly readings.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. A different starting point

In the Chinese tradition, knowledge has been understood more broadly than in European philosophy, as something that also, "(or primarily) stems from moral training" [@rosker2025, preamble], and the study of knowing has been treated as inseparable from the study of being: "epistemology is inseparable from ontology" [@rosker2025, § 2.3].

#### 2. The Mohist tests

The Mohists offered three standards, or models (*fa*), for judging a claim: "statements must have a root, a source, and a use". The root is the precedent of the ancient sage-kings; the source is "that statements must have an empirical basis; they must be checked against what people see and hear"; the use is that, adopted as policy, a statement "must produce benefit" [@fraser2024, § "The Three Fa"].

#### 3. Zhuangzi's doubts

Zhuangzi's butterfly story ends: "...or a butterfly is dreaming of being Zhuangzi—though there must be a difference. This is called 'things change'" [@hansen2024, § 4.8]. Chad Hansen reads it as an illustration of "mild skepticism" about whether moving to a new perspective is progress [@hansen2024, § 4.8].

#### 4. Xunzi's clear mind

Xunzi held that we know by means of the heart-mind, which has three "cardinal attributes": emptiness, unity and tranquillity. Tranquillity is "the heart-mind's ability to distinguish fantasy from rational thinking" [@goldin2025, § 8].

### Common confusions

- **Zhuangzi was a relativist, full stop.** On Hansen's reading, "Zhuangzi's relativism is mildly skeptical of the relativism itself" [@hansen2024, § 4.8].
- **The Mohist tests are a scientific method.** Only the second is empirical. And the Mohists "seem to overlook the possibility of conflicts between the three", as when the practices of the ancient sage-kings no longer benefit society [@fraser2024, § "The Three Fa"].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **How the tests are justified.** "The texts that present the theory do not say", but the Mohists probably treat the third standard, benefit, as an application of their moral theory [@fraser2024, § "The Three Fa"]. Critics can also question "the vagueness of the three standards" and "the extent to which the Mohists apply them fairly and rigorously" [@fraser2024, § "The Three Fa"].
- **Knowing how.** The same chapter of Hansen's account turns to "Zhuangzi's non-ironic examples of real-life spectacular know-how", above all Butcher Ding, whose knife "sang" as he carved an ox [@hansen2024, § 4.8]. Compare [Three kinds of knowing](deeper:01-what-is-epistemology/three-kinds-of-knowing).
- **Xunzi's other attributes.** "Emptiness" is the heart-mind's capacity to take in new information without losing old; "unity" its ability "to synthesize diverse data into meaningful paradigms" [@goldin2025, § 8].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Xunzi's chapter title.** The chapter translates *Jiebi* as "Dispelling Obsession" or "Undoing Fixation". Paul Goldin gives "Resolving Blindness", explaining that it "refers to the self-destructive acts that people undertake because they fail to employ their heart-minds correctly" [@goldin2025, § 8]. The idea of blindness fits the chapter's comparison with modern biases.
- **"The three standards."** *Fa* means models or standards; the first is a root in "the historical precedent and evidence provided by the deeds of the ancient sage kings" [@fraser2024, § "The Three Fa"], which is closer to an appeal to tested practice than to tradition for its own sake.
