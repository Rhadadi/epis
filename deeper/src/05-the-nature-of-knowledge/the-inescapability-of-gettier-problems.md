> **In short.** Zagzebski's recipe starts with a justified false belief and restores its truth by a coincidence. Adding another independently satisfied condition can leave the recipe intact. The conclusion concerns a family of analyses, not a proof that all accounts of knowledge must fail. A condition that already connects belief to truth is outside the recipe's stated target. The challenge is to explain that connection without making the account empty or circular.

## Re-learn

### Why a new ingredient can leave the same problem

Imagine a researcher who competently follows an established laboratory procedure. A rare fault makes the instrument display the wrong result. Now suppose an independent event changes the sample so that the displayed result becomes true. The researcher's competence and procedure are unchanged; the coincidence repairs the result while leaving the mistaken route to it intact. This is an invented illustration of the structure of Zagzebski's two-stage recipe, as presented by Ichikawa and Steup [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 7].

The first stage must remain possible after the proposed extra condition is imposed. If the extra condition is that faculties function properly in a suitable environment, for example, the question is whether proper function can still yield a false belief. The SEP discussion describes Mary mistaking her husband's similar-looking brother for her husband. Adding the husband elsewhere in the same room makes her belief true without correcting her identification [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 7].

It is tempting to repair each case with another requirement. But an added condition that leaves room for the same pattern may only make the example more elaborate. The target is a nonredundant JTB-plus analysis whose additional condition is logically independent of justification, truth and belief. That qualification matters: it tells us what the recipe purports to generalize over [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 7].

### What the argument does not establish

It does not show that every well-supported belief is defective. An account can allow ordinary mistakes and nevertheless explain why some actual successes count as knowledge. Nor does it show that an observer must eliminate every conceivable error before knowing. The recipe tests a proposed analysis; it is not itself an argument that ordinary knowledge is impossible.

The SEP explicitly identifies strategies outside the target family. A causal condition referring to a fact causing the corresponding belief already entails truth and belief. An anti-luck condition likewise imposes a relation among the elements instead of adding an independent ingredient. These strategies have problems of their own, but those problems are not established merely by invoking Zagzebski's recipe [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 7–8].

## Beyond the chapter

### Truth-entailing justification is not automatically skepticism

The chapter presents an infallibilist response as threatening to leave us with very little knowledge. The fuller discussion requires a distinction. Saying that justification entails truth does not by itself say that a subject must possess a deductive proof eliminating all alternatives. One would also need an account of what justification is and which ordinary beliefs meet that account. The survey names factive approaches to justification and notes that they have defenders, while describing them as outside the mainstream [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 7].

The point is logical rather than a verdict in favour of those views. From the requirement that a condition cannot coexist with falsehood, one cannot simply infer that nobody satisfies it. Knowledge itself entails truth; that fact does not alone prove that nobody knows. The dispute is about whether a truth-entailing condition can be explained and recognized in a way that sustains ordinary knowledge.

### Giving up decomposition need not mean giving up explanation

Another response is to stop looking for a list of independently intelligible constituents. Ichikawa and Steup note that few philosophically important concepts have yielded widely accepted traditional analyses. Treating knowledge as basic still permits investigating what it entails, how it interacts with evidence, and what roles it plays in reasoning. That approach changes the explanatory direction; it does not require abandoning every question about knowing [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 7].
