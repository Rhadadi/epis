> **In short.** Craig asks what identifying knowers does for a community that depends on information from others. His starting point is the search for good informants, rather than a list of reactions to imaginary cases. A good informant for one person here and now is not yet the more general concept of a knower. Objectivizing that role helps explain the difference. The proposal is a functional hypothesis, not evidence that a particular prehistoric event created the word knowledge.

## Re-learn

### Why an informational role is useful

No one can inspect every event relevant to their life. Communities need ways to identify people worth consulting. Craig's project starts from that practical need and asks what a concept serving it would be like. The SEP's Analysis of Knowledge contrasts this starting point with the usual method of analyzing intuitions about cases [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 9].

Imagine needing to know whether a bridge is passable. A person who recently crossed it offers a different basis from someone who repeats a rumour. Calling someone a knower can mark a candidate informant, though the evaluation still depends on the question. A person knowledgeable about the bridge may have no information about tomorrow's weather.

### A good informant for me is a person in a situation

Rysiew's discussion explains the initial role as purpose-relative. An informant may need to be accessible, recognizable and as likely to be right as the inquirer's concerns require. Those features do not all look like necessary conditions for knowing: an inaccessible person can know something. Craig therefore does not simply identify knowledge with being a usable informant for this person at this moment [@rysiew2026, § 5.3].

The account introduces objectivization. A community needs a concept with a shareable core rather than one shifting completely with each individual's immediate access and needs. Craig's proposed knower is an objectivized good informant. This step matters because it explains why the functional starting point and the finished concept are not interchangeable [@rysiew2026, § 5.3].

## Beyond the chapter

### A functional explanation is not a dated origin story

The proposal asks what role a concept can fulfil given general human conditions. That is different from claiming to have discovered when a population first formed the concept. A narrative can illuminate a function without providing archaeological or evolutionary evidence for a particular historical sequence. The SEP presents Craig's project as a distinctive theoretical approach to knowledge, not as an empirical reconstruction of one event [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 9; @rysiew2026, § 5.3].

It would also be too strong to say that every use of knowledge language directly selects an informant. People discuss inaccessible knowers, their own past knowledge, and knowledge they choose not to share. Objectivization is intended to move beyond immediate usefulness, but the resulting theory must still explain the full range of cases.

### Different functions can compete

The Value of Knowledge reports later Craig-inspired approaches and a rival emphasis on closing inquiry. Marking someone as knowing can indicate that a question is settled sufficiently for a purpose, rather than merely identifying a source to consult. These functions can overlap, but they do not coincide: one concerns whom to ask, the other concerns when to stop asking [@pritchard-etal2025, § 6].

An invented disaster-response example makes the difference visible. Identifying a knowledgeable engineer can guide the next inquiry. Accepting an assessment as settled can authorize a decision to act. A concept might contribute to both while requiring a theory of their relationship.

### Counterexamples still matter, but their role can change

The SEP notes that Craig defends a role-based account despite intuitive counterexamples, while Kornblith offers a natural-kind approach in which empirical investigation has a central role. Those are different alternatives to a purely case-driven project. Moving to function does not mean that any useful account is automatically true; it changes the kinds of evidence and explanatory success that need assessment [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 9].
