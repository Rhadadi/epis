> **In short.** Proposed responses disagree about what should connect a true belief to reality. Some exclude false premises or hidden defeaters; some require a cause, a reliable process or an ability; others ask what would happen in nearby circumstances. These requirements are not equivalent, and the fake-barn case makes their differences visible. A further response treats knowledge as basic instead of trying to assemble it from independently understood parts. The best comparison asks what each view explains, what it excludes, and what remains unspecified.

## Re-learn

### Identify the gap before repairing it

In the Ford-and-Barcelona case, Smith's evidence concerns Jones's car, but Brown's location makes the conclusion true. One repair would forbid inference from the false Ford premise. Another would require the truth-making fact to cause the belief. A third would require the belief to change if the proposition were false. These give similar verdicts here for different reasons [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 3–6].

Now consider a real barn surrounded by facades. Henry's actual perception is accurate. There need be no inferred false premise, and the barn does cause the belief. The problem is how easily the same-looking surroundings could have produced an error. A requirement focused on nearby error can therefore have a different verdict from one focused only on the actual cause [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 4–6].

The comparison is easiest when the claim stays fixed. Knowing that there is a barn, knowing that it is red, and knowing that the county contains no facades are three different matters. Changing the claim can change which alternatives matter and which conditions are satisfied. That feature becomes especially important for sensitivity [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.1].

### An ingredient and a connection are different

A person can have a skill and succeed without succeeding through that skill. A carefully aimed arrow might be knocked away by one gust and returned by another. Accuracy and skilful execution coexist, yet the skill does not explain the hit. Sosa's aptness requirement adds the explanatory relation: the performance is accurate because adroit [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 10.1].

Applied to belief, this asks whether the person's competence explains getting the truth. It is stronger than merely finding a true belief and an intelligent person in the same story. That is the sort of distinction a response needs if the original problem is that good reasons and truth are only accidentally connected [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 8, 10].

## The full story

### First strategy: restrict the route through falsehood

The no-false-lemmas proposal adds a condition excluding beliefs inferred from false beliefs. Smith's reasoning about Jones supplies its motivation. The SEP then tests it against perceptual cases: a robot mistaken for a dog with a real dog hidden nearby, and a real barn among facades. If the target belief is directly supported by perception rather than inferred from a falsehood, the added requirement does not prevent the counterexample [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 4].

This is a limitation of a particular structural explanation. It locates the defect in a chain of propositions, while the perceptual example can locate the defect in the relation between an experience and the world. A defender may claim that perception depends on an implicit belief about appearances or environmental normality. But that move requires an account of what implicit beliefs are and why they count as premises.

Consider an invented case of a guide who gives the right route using an outdated map. One false detail on the map need not affect the chosen route. A rule prohibiting any false belief in the background would be excessively broad: nearly any person might possess irrelevant false beliefs. A narrower rule must distinguish a falsehood that supports this conclusion from one that is merely present. The no-false-lemmas formulation in the SEP does this by referring to inference, but direct perception presses against that boundary [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 4].

### Second strategy: rule out hidden defeat

Defeasibility approaches ask whether true information, if added to the subject's evidence, would remove its justification. Learning that Jones is driving a rental seems to defeat Smith's basis. The IEP's discussion explains that the account of a defeater is itself disputed, rather than treating all additional true propositions as equally threatening [@epi-luck-iep, § 1.d.iii].

Its Grabit case makes the difficulty vivid. An observer sees a familiar student take a library book without checking it out. Unknown to the observer, the student's mother has said that he was elsewhere and that an identical twin was present. The fact that she said this is true, though her story is false. If hearing it would undermine the observer's evidence, a simple defeater condition threatens to deny knowledge even where the mother's claim is wholly misleading [@epi-luck-iep, § 1.d.iii].

There are two levels to separate. The reported proposition concerns where the student was. The true proposition concerns what someone said. An account screening only for truth cannot ignore the second proposition by pointing out that the first is false. It must explain why true information about misleading testimony does or does not defeat the original belief [@epi-luck-iep, § 1.d.iii].

This illustrates a broader challenge: information can be true without improving an agent's epistemic position. A truthful report of a deceptive claim can make a justified belief less secure from the subject's point of view. Defeasibility theories need a principled treatment of those interactions, not merely the instruction to add all truths.

### Third strategy: connect the belief to its cause

The simplified causal account replaces justification with an appropriate connection between the fact and the belief. The SEP explicitly cautions that this is a simplified account, rather than Goldman's full theory. It brings a useful distinction into view: Brown's location does not cause Smith's disjunctive belief, whereas an ordinary visible object may cause the corresponding perceptual belief [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 6.2].

The causal route helps explain why the hidden dog does not rescue the robot observer. The real dog has not contributed to the experience. But in the barn case the genuine barn really does contribute. That is why an actual causal connection by itself does not settle the issue. The environment can make error nearby even when the actual causal chain is intact [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 6.2].

One could elaborate the kind of causation allowed. Yet a condition described simply as the kind that gives knowledge would be circular as an analysis: it would rely on the thing it was meant to explain. The explanatory task is to specify the relevant connection in other terms and then show why it delivers plausible verdicts. This resembles the circularity concern raised more explicitly for safety [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.2].

### Fourth strategy: evaluate the process

Simple knowledge reliabilism requires a true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process. Unlike a requirement that the person defend the belief, it can focus on how the belief is formed even when the person cannot articulate the process's reliability. The SEP distinguishes reliabilism about knowledge from reliabilism about justification; the two theories answer different questions even when a particular formulation makes their verdicts coincide [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 6.1].

The barn problem returns. A visual capacity can be generally reliable while being exposed to unusually deceptive surroundings. Calling the process reliable at a broad level does not automatically explain this particular success. The SEP treats the barn example as a challenge to simple reliabilism as well as to the causal theory [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 6.2].

Compare a reader accurately interpreting a sign in an ordinary station with one accurately interpreting the only genuine sign among replicas. Their skill may look the same at one level of description. Their chances of getting the truth differ when the surroundings are included. This invented comparison shows why selecting the process and circumstances of evaluation matters. It does not decide by itself whether reliability should be local, global, actual or counterfactual.

### Fifth strategy: sensitivity

Sensitivity asks whether the subject would refrain from believing the proposition if it were false. Nozick defended a condition of this kind. In the mirage example, removing the hidden water would leave the misleading appearance and the belief intact, so the belief does not track the truth in the required way [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.1].

The requirement sounds like safety, but the situations considered differ. Sensitivity begins with situations where the proposition is false. Safety begins with nearby situations where the subject believes it. A remote falsehood situation may matter to sensitivity without being a nearby danger to the actual belief [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 5.1–5.2].

The SEP also notes that sensitivity does not straightforwardly exclude every fake-barn variant. Whether Henry would believe there is a barn if none stood at this particular location depends on what would occupy the location instead. A claim about a red barn can behave differently from a claim about a barn if facades and real barns have different colours. The details of the counterfactual matter, even if Henry does not understand their significance [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.1].

Its other cost concerns closure. An ordinary person may sensitively believe that they have hands: if the hands had been lost in an ordinary accident, their belief would change. Yet their belief that they are not a handless victim of systematic deception may be insensitive, because in that deception scenario they would still believe it. The theory can thus ascribe ordinary knowledge while denying knowledge of a consequence excluding deception. DeRose's objection to the resulting conjunction is among the reasons the SEP reports widespread resistance to sensitivity conditions [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.1].

### Sixth strategy: safety

Safety asks whether the belief could easily have been false. A common modal formulation requires that in nearby situations where the subject believes the proposition, the proposition is true. Sosa proposed safety as an alternative to sensitivity; the SEP explains why the two are not equivalent simply because their ordinary-language formulations resemble contrapositives [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.2].

Counterfactuals are not material conditionals. Reversing and negating their antecedent and consequent is not automatically truth-preserving. More importantly, a formulation must avoid making every actual true belief trivially safe. The SEP therefore presents safety directly through nearby worlds, rather than relying on an ambiguous counterfactual with an actually true antecedent [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.2].

::: argument Why the choice of nearby situations matters
1. A safety condition evaluates beliefs in nearby situations, not only the actual situation.
2. Which situations count as nearby determines which possible errors are included.
3. Including or excluding an error can change whether a belief counts as safe.
4. Therefore, a safety analysis requires an account of the relevant similarity relation.
:::

[@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.2]. It is not enough to answer every counterexample by announcing that the troublesome possibility is too distant. The account needs to explain distance in a way that is informative across cases. If distance is determined by first deciding where knowledge exists, safety can still characterize knowledge but cannot independently analyze it.

The Halloween-party example illustrates the problem. Judy gives accurate directions but would misdirect a particular unwanted guest. Someone who nearly wore a convincing costume of that guest receives the accurate directions. Does the nearby possibility of misleading directions make their belief unsafe, or does the changed testimonial situation put the error outside the relevant comparison? The SEP presents this as a challenge, with replies depending on how similarity is understood [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 5.2].

### Seventh strategy: success through competence

Sosa's accuracy, adroitness and aptness distinguish truth, skilful belief formation, and truth attributable to that skill. The model is attractive because it is not restricted to epistemology: the same three evaluations make sense for an archer. That offers a prospect of understanding the explanatory relationship without defining it exclusively through knowledge [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 10.1].

The two-gust arrow makes the Gettier structure vivid. The archer's execution is skilful; the hit is accurate; but the unexpected second gust, rather than the skill, restores the hit. Similarly, Smith's competent inference does not explain the truth supplied by Brown's location. Aptness is meant to require that success manifest the competence [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 10.1–10.2].

Fake barns again divide responses. One can say Henry lacks the relevant competence in this environment. One can say he retains competence but his success does not manifest it sufficiently. Or one can accept that Henry knows at one level while lacking a further reflective standing. The SEP reports Sosa taking that third route. It would therefore be inaccurate to claim that every virtue theorist gives the same no-knowledge verdict [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 10.2].

### Eighth strategy: reverse the explanatory order

Knowledge-first approaches challenge the assumption that knowledge must be assembled from more fundamental epistemic notions. Williamson treats knowledge as a fundamental factive mental state and proposes identifying a person's evidence with what the person knows. Rejecting analysis does not reject all useful necessary conditions: truth, belief, justification and safety can still matter [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 11].

This is a substantive program rather than a verbal declaration that knowledge is whatever knowledge is. Its case depends on the theoretical work knowledge can do elsewhere. The SEP notes that there is no single straightforward proof of the program; its attraction depends largely on whether putting knowledge first provides successful explanations [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 11].

### Conditions of knowledge and methods for checking them

It is useful to distinguish a theory's condition from a person's procedure for checking that condition. A safety theorist can say that nearby error would disqualify knowledge without saying that every knower must survey nearby worlds. A reliabilist can require a reliable process without requiring the subject to calculate its success rate. These are external conditions unless the theory adds an access requirement. The SEP makes that separation explicit for safety and for the replacement of justification by reliability [@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 5.2, 6.1].

An invented factory example exposes the difference. An operator reads an instrument whose calibration is in fact sound but cannot personally inspect the calibration mechanism. A theory might permit the resulting knowledge because the external connection is good. Another might require evidence available to the operator about the instrument. Neither theory can be evaluated merely by asking whether the operator has memorized its philosophical formulation.

The opposite distinction matters too. Being able to defend a belief eloquently does not prove that the belief is safe or reliably formed. An articulate person can explain the premises they used while being unaware that the surroundings are deceptive. A useful comparison of theories therefore asks both what must obtain and what the subject must have access to. Combining those questions prematurely can make an externalist proposal sound like an implausibly demanding introspection requirement it never imposed.

### The views compared

::: positions What each approach must explain
| Approach | Focus | Pressure point |
|---|---|---|
| No false lemmas | Inference from true premises | Direct perceptual cases |
| Defeasibility | No relevant undefeated underminer | True reports of misleading testimony |
| Causation | Actual connection to the fact | Accurate perception in deceptive surroundings |
| Reliability | Truth-producing process | The relevant process and environment |
| Sensitivity | Belief changes when the claim is false | Closure and counterfactual details |
| Safety | No nearby false belief | Which possibilities count as nearby |
| Aptness | Truth through competence | How environmental luck affects competence |
| Knowledge first | Knowledge explains other notions | The explanatory success of the wider program |
:::

[@ichikawa-steup2026, §§ 4–6, 10–11; @epi-luck-iep, § 1.d.iii]. These are pressure points, not announcements that all developed versions have been conclusively refuted. A simplified theory can fail while a richer theory survives; the richer theory then owes an explanation of why its additional structure is justified.

## Beyond the chapter

### A causal explanation can coexist with epistemic danger

An accurate reading may be caused by the right instrument while the instrument sits among mislabeled copies. Asking whether the reading has the right cause and asking whether you could easily have selected a misleading copy are different questions. The barn discussion helps preserve both. Neither can be discarded simply because the actual answer turned out right.
