> **In short.** The ability to perform right now is different from the ability involved in knowing how. An injured expert, an instructor and a fortunate novice therefore test different claims. Intellectualism need not say that reciting instructions is sufficient, and anti-intellectualism need not say that every successful movement is a skill. The dispute concerns what makes a performance intelligent and how that relates to propositional knowledge. Those qualifications matter more than the simple opposition between facts and practice.

## Re-learn

### General ability and present opportunity

An experienced pianist who cannot currently use her hands may still be credited with knowing how to play. Intellectualists use such examples to argue that knowing how does not entail ability. Anti-intellectualists reply by distinguishing a general ability from being able to exercise it in the current circumstances. Loss of opportunity need not settle whether the underlying competence remains [@pavese2022, § 5].

The distinction is easy to see without taking a side. Knowing how to unlock a particular door does not imply having the key in one's pocket. Having the key does not imply knowing which way to turn it. One concerns practical availability; the other concerns the relevant cognitive or skilled standing.

### Knowing how oneself and knowing how someone could

The ski instructor example introduces another distinction. An instructor can know how a stunt is performed without being able to perform it. Pavese explains that a knowledge-how ascription can be read generically or with reference to the agent herself. Intellectualists emphasize the latter reading in the relevant cases, while anti-intellectualists question whether the generic instructional knowledge establishes the instructor's own know-how [@pavese2022, §§ 4.1, 5].

A manual can describe how someone with the right training completes a difficult procedure. A reader's knowledge of the manual is not automatically knowledge of how *they themselves* can execute it. That gap is not resolved merely by counting the manual's sentences.

### One successful attempt is not enough

An invented novice may hit the right piano keys once by chance. The occurrence of the right movement does not alone explain whether it reflects a general ability or intelligent control. Conversely, an expert may make an isolated mistake while retaining know-how. An account needs to relate performance to competence rather than identify the two episode by episode.

Pavese notes that contemporary intellectualist proposals also connect knowing how with abilities. It is therefore misleading to place all ability on one side of the dispute and all propositions on the other. The disagreement concerns how the connection is understood [@pavese2022, § 5].

## Beyond the chapter

### The regress argument has several targets

The familiar Rylean objection concerns requiring an intelligent performance to be preceded by another intelligent act of considering a rule. Requiring a prior act can threaten a regress. But the claim that know-how involves propositional knowledge is different from the claim that every exercise of know-how must begin with explicit deliberation. Pavese separates several formulations of the intellectualist legend and several regress arguments [@pavese2022, § 1].

That distinction prevents an invalid shortcut. Observing that an expert acts without consciously rehearsing instructions does not by itself show that propositional knowledge plays no role. It challenges a particular account of how such knowledge must be exercised. Conversely, describing the expert's activity propositionally does not establish that the expert's know-how consists in knowing those propositions.
