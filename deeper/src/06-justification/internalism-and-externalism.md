> **In short.** Internalists say that justification depends on the relevant features of a person's perspective or mental life. Externalists say that some facts beyond those features can make a difference. The dispute is about what gives a belief support, not whether the believer is selfish or open to other people. Two equally careful people with matching experiences might differ in how reliably their methods connect them to the world. Whether they are nevertheless equally justified is one of the central tests of these views.

## Re-learn

### Two identical-looking instruments

Imagine two students reading instruments that display the same temperature. Their experiences, memories, and confidence match. One instrument works; the other produces plausible random numbers. Neither student has any sign of the difference. Are their beliefs equally justified?

An internalist emphasizes the matching perspective. If the relevant internal conditions are the same, the students have the same justification. An externalist can give the actual difference between instruments an epistemic role. The reliable connection can matter even when the student cannot identify it. Which external conditions matter varies between theories [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

This does not ask whether both temperature beliefs are true. They might accidentally both be true, or one might be false. Truth and justification are different questions. It also does not ask whether the students deserve blame. Both might have done everything reasonably expected of them while differing in the epistemic quality of the processes they used [@littlejohn2025, §§ 1, 2.2].

### Two meanings of internal

Mentalism says that justification depends on relevant mental conditions. If those conditions match, justification matches. Access internalism instead emphasizes what the subject can access through reflection or introspection. The two proposals are not identical. A mental condition might be inaccessible, and philosophers dispute whether everything reflectively accessible must be confined to nonfactive inner states [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

Even access internalism has versions. Access to supporting grounds is weaker than access to whether those grounds provide sufficient justification. You might be able to recall a measurement without being able to settle a difficult question about its evidential adequacy. Requiring the second sort of access places a greater burden on believers [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

### External does not mean no mental conditions

Externalism denies that internal conditions alone determine justification. It need not deny that mental conditions matter. A theory can require both a reliable process and the absence of accessible evidence that the process is unreliable. Adding the second condition does not make the first irrelevant. Such an account remains externalist if internally matching believers can differ in justification [@littlejohn2025, § 2.1].

## The full story

### Identify the question before taking sides

Debates about justification and knowledge overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Knowledge normally requires truth. Internally identical people can believe the same proposition when it is true for one case and false in the relevant counterpart case. Internalism about justification therefore does not ordinarily say that all knowledge depends only on the internal. Its central claim concerns the standing of beliefs, including justified false beliefs [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

The terminology also differs from philosophy of mind. There, internal can concern what lies within an organism's bodily boundary or what determines mental content. Here, it typically concerns mental life or reflective access. A theory about the external determination of content does not by itself decide whether justification requires an independently reliable belief-forming process [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

Finally, separate propositional from doxastic justification. A person can possess adequate support and ignore it, forming the belief from a superstition instead. Internalism about what evidence supports does not settle whether the actual belief is appropriately based on that evidence. Externalism about the process does not erase the need to specify how that process produces this belief. The two distinctions cross rather than replace each other [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 1.3.3].

### What supervenience means here

The internalist's dependence claim can be expressed without technical language: no difference in justification without a relevant internal difference. If two people match in all the relevant internal respects, they match in justification. This leaves room for many theories of what those respects include. It does not require every mental detail to be important; an irrelevant background sensation need not change support for a mathematical conclusion [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

The externalist denies that constraint. There can be some pair of internally matching believers who differ in justification because another condition differs. This is a broad position. Reliability is a prominent candidate, but externalism as such is not one complete process theory. It can include accounts requiring different combinations of environmental, historical, and internal conditions [@littlejohn2025, §§ 1, 2.1].

This definition explains why occasional attention to external facts does not establish externalism. An internalist can acquire evidence that an instrument is broken and lose justification. The acquired evidence changes the person's internal situation. The disagreement becomes sharp when the instrument changes while the relevant internal situation does not. It is the unnoticed difference, rather than any mention of instruments, that tests the dependence thesis.

### Access to grounds and access to their adequacy

Suppose you can recall a doctor's advice and the symptoms she considered. Those grounds are available to reflection. But assessing their adequacy might require medical knowledge you lack. A modest access view can treat the grounds' availability as sufficient access, while a stronger view requires access to the justificatory status itself. The survey distinguishes these demands [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

The stronger demand threatens a higher-order regress. If you need justified recognition that your first-order grounds are adequate, do you also need justified recognition that this recognition is adequate? Foundationalists who require strong access must explain where such a regress stops. Possessing support, accessing support, and possessing a supported judgment about its adequacy are three different relations [@hasan-fumerton2022, § 3.1].

Mentalism can avoid imposing that particular requirement while maintaining an internal dependence thesis. A person's relevant mental evidence can determine her justification even when she cannot formulate an epistemic theory. Whether unconscious states count as relevant mental evidence, and whether their relevance is accessible, remain questions for particular mentalist accounts [@littlejohn2025, § 1].

### A historical first-person demand

::: original Locke on comparing conflicting grounds
> there it is, where diligence, attention, and exactness are required, to form a right judgment, and to proportion the assent to the different evidence and probability of the thing
:::
[@locke1690, Book IV, ch. XVI, § 9]. Locke is discussing cases where testimony conflicts with common experience or with other testimony. The passage exemplifies a demand to assess the grounds available when forming a judgment. It does not by itself settle the contemporary internalist–externalist debate, whose definitions concern dependence relations not stated here.

The practical appeal is clear. A believer trying to decide cannot directly consult facts of which she has no awareness. She must proceed from what she can observe, recall, or infer. Internalists can treat this first-person standpoint as central to justification. Externalists can grant its deliberative importance while denying that the complete explanation of a belief's epistemic quality must be accessible from it [@littlejohn2025, §§ 2.2, 3.2].

The distinction is between a guide for deciding and a condition explaining success. A student uses available information to choose a measuring procedure. Its actual reliability can still determine whether it tends to get the measurement right. A philosophical theory must explain how these two questions relate, rather than infer that whichever is important must be the only legitimate question.

### The new evil demon argument

The classic deceiver scenario asks whether we know that an external world exists. The new evil demon objection uses a matching deceived subject to test justification. Imagine that your duplicate has experiences like yours and reasons with equal care, but a demon systematically disconnects those experiences from reality. Actual-environment reliability can be absent even while the subject's perspective matches [@littlejohn2025, § 2.4].

::: argument A challenge to actual-environment reliability
1. A systematically deceived internal duplicate seems just as justified as the ordinary believer.
2. A simple theory requiring reliable production in the actual environment denies justification to the duplicate.
3. Therefore that simple theory conflicts with the equality judgment in premise 1.
:::
[@littlejohn2025, § 2.4]. Accepting premise 1 motivates the internalist conclusion. An externalist can reject the equality judgment, distinguish justification from another positive status, or revise which environments enter the reliability condition. The thought experiment identifies a disagreement rather than forcing one answer by description alone.

It is especially important not to stipulate the verdict into the case. Describe the matching experiences, conduct, and reasoning; then ask whether justification matches. Calling both subjects equally justified before presenting the example would merely assume the internalist conclusion. Likewise, calling the deceived subject epistemically worthless would bypass the intuition that makes the objection interesting.

Some reliabilists assess processes in normal environments rather than exclusively in the environment where a belief is formed. Other responses appeal to home worlds or refine the process account. Such moves can preserve a role for reliability while accommodating more of the intuition about deceived subjects. They also generate questions about how the relevant environments are selected and whether the revised account remains explanatory [@littlejohn2025, § 2.4].

### Norman and the other direction of pressure

The Norman case moves in the opposite direction. Norman has a reliable clairvoyant capacity and forms a true belief through it, but lacks evidence for or against the faculty and this deliverance. BonJour's challenge is that Norman's belief seems unjustified despite the reliable connection. This pressures a theory that treats reliability alone as sufficient [@littlejohn2025, § 2.1].

Norman is not simply a person with known evidence that his method is unreliable. In the case described, he has no evidence either way. Adding a warning that clairvoyance is fraudulent would create an accessible defeater and change the target. A defender who requires absence of known defeat must still explain the original case, rather than reply only to the altered version [@littlejohn2025, § 2.1].

The opposing pressure concerns tacit competence. The survey discusses Srinivasan's case of Nour, who detects racist attitudes through subtle cues without being able to recover which cues she registered. It is tempting to credit her sensitivity even though she cannot reconstruct the grounds. That example challenges a blanket equation between inability to articulate evidence and lack of epistemic standing [@littlejohn2025, § 2.1].

These examples should not be settled by describing one capacity as respectable and another as strange. A theory needs to identify the relevant difference: perhaps the subject has tacit evidence, perhaps reliable sensitivity is enough, or perhaps justification and knowledge diverge. Externalists can also require both internal and external conditions. The survey emphasizes that externalism is broader than pure externalism [@littlejohn2025, § 2.1].

### Responsibility does not automatically settle the issue

An internalist motivation connects justification to epistemic responsibility. If two people have the same accessible grounds and behave the same way, it can seem unfair to praise one and blame the other merely because an unnoticed external condition differs. This supports an internalist account of blameworthiness, but another premise is needed to infer internalism about justification [@littlejohn2025, § 2.2].

The missing premise identifies being blameless with being justified. Externalists can reject it. An excuse can explain why a person deserves no blame while leaving the belief insufficiently supported. The survey compares this distinction with disputes about justification and excuse in other normative domains. Neither moral language nor talk of duty makes internalism true by definition [@littlejohn2025, § 2.2].

Historical responsibility creates another complication. Two people can now have matching confidence and apparent recollection although one originally learned responsibly and the other originally relied on an obviously unreliable source. If that history matters, a purely present-time account must explain how. Losing the memory of a negligent origin might remove an accessible warning without automatically making the original process responsible [@littlejohn2025, § 2.2].

Internalists have replies. Present recollection may supply new support, and an internalist dependence thesis need not be limited to a single time slice. But those replies need to state their temporal scope. A theory about all relevant mental history differs from a theory about currently accessible evidence. Treating both as the inside obscures a substantive choice [@littlejohn2025, §§ 2.2, 3.2].

### Forgotten evidence and retained information

You may remember that a historical event occurred while forgetting the lesson, book, or conversation through which you learned it. The externalist can explain continued justification through reliable memory preserving appropriately acquired information. A demanding access view risks saying that justification disappears when the original grounds can no longer be reconstructed [@littlejohn2025, § 3.2].

Internalist responses appeal to present apparent memory or to the permissibility of trusting memory without reason to doubt it. The strongest challenge compares this person with someone whose initial acquisition was poor but whose current apparent memory is the same. If present internal features justify both, the second belief can acquire support despite its dubious origin. If they justify only the first, some historical difference matters [@littlejohn2025, § 3.2].

This is not merely a dispute about how much people forget. It tests whether epistemic status can improve when information about the grounds disappears. Perhaps a current memory experience supplies support in its own right. Perhaps preservation depends on an appropriate earlier basis. A convincing account has to explain both ordinary retained knowledge and the misleading cases without simply announcing that memory is usually trustworthy.

### Children and theoretical sophistication

Small children seem able to learn many things through perception and testimony without possessing beliefs about reliability. Externalists use such cases against theories demanding elaborate reflective certification. Internalists can argue that the relevant requirements are lighter than that, or distinguish the positive status of a child's belief from the standards applied to a mature reflective believer [@littlejohn2025, § 3.2].

The dispute should therefore specify the burden. Requiring a perceptual experience is different from requiring a verbal account of the experience's adequacy. Requiring access to testimony is different from requiring an independently defended reliability theory for the speaker. Examples of children challenge the latter demands more directly than the former. They do not refute every internalist account at once.

The explanatory question reaches beyond children. Why are perception and memory normally good sources while arbitrary imagining is not? An externalist can appeal to how the sources tend to connect with truth. An internalist must explain their different roles through relevant internal features or appropriate epistemic rules. Both owe an account of the contrast, even when they agree about many ordinary verdicts [@littlejohn2025, § 3.2].

### Two axes of a larger debate

Foundationalism and coherentism concern the structure of justification. Internalism and externalism concern which conditions determine it. They are different axes. A foundationalist can appeal to internally accessible experience or an externally reliable process. A holistic account can evaluate relations among beliefs while differing over what gives the system its epistemic connection to reality [@hasan-fumerton2022, § 6; @littlejohn2025, § 1].

Likewise, evidentialism needs an account of evidence. If evidence consists of nonfactive mental states, internally matching subjects can share it. If evidence is identified with knowledge, a deceived counterpart may not possess the same evidence. The latter proposal changes the dependence question because knowledge itself involves conditions beyond matching appearances [@littlejohn2025, § 2.3].

The point is not to make the labels infinitely complicated. It is to prevent a slogan from deciding several separate questions unnoticed. Ask what determines justification, whether that factor must be accessible, whether the belief must be based on it, and whether the account concerns present grounds or their history. Then examples can reveal a real disagreement rather than a difference in terminology.

## Beyond the chapter

### A practical use for both questions

When choosing what to believe, examine the reasons available to you. When evaluating a method, examine how it actually performs, including errors you would not detect from a single use. These activities can improve inquiry together. Their practical value does not decide whether justification is ultimately internal or external; it shows why neither accessible support nor method quality should be ignored in evaluation.
