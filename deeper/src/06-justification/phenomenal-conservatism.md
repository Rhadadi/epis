> **In short.** Phenomenal conservatism says that something's seeming true can provide some support for believing it, when defeating considerations are absent. A seeming is not simply a belief or a desire that the claim be true. The support is defeasible and need not be enough for action or knowledge. The hardest cases involve appearances shaped by unsupported beliefs or biased expectations. Explaining those cases matters more than repeating that appearances sometimes mislead.

## Re-learn

### An appearance you do not accept

An optical illusion can continue to look unequal even after measurement convinces you that its lines are equal. The appearance remains while the belief changes. This illustrates why phenomenal conservatives distinguish a seeming from the belief it naturally invites. If seemings were just beliefs, the view would risk making belief justify itself [@phen-con-iep, § 2.a].

The proposal gives an appearance initial epistemic significance. Looking at an ordinary table can give you some reason to believe a cup is there without first proving that all visual experiences are reliable. Learning that the scene is projected can defeat that support. Immediate support and ultimate immunity are different claims [@phen-con-iep, §§ 1.b, 4.b].

Wanting a cup to be there is also different from its looking as though one is there. You can desire it while looking at an empty table. A desire can incline you to believe, but the relevant appearance is supposed to present the proposition as true rather than merely make believing attractive. The experience account distinguishes those routes [@phen-con-iep, §§ 2.b, 2.d].

### Some support is not a complete permission to act

A weak appearance might contribute a reason without making the conclusion sufficiently supported for a consequential decision. The IEP entry emphasizes that having some justification does not entail enough justification for action. Calling a belief prima facie supported should therefore not become a universal instruction to follow the appearance [@phen-con-iep, § 4.a].

Likewise, the view was developed principally for non-inferential justification. Extending it to inference raises additional questions. A conclusion might seem to follow from a premise even though the inference is fallacious or the premise unjustified. Some sympathetic accounts restrict the appearance principle and add separate conditions for inferential beliefs [@phen-con-iep, §§ 1.c, 4.d].

## Beyond the chapter

### Biased appearances

Suppose an unsupported suspicion makes another person's face look hostile. The appearance is genuinely an experience rather than just an explicit judgment, but it arose from a dubious belief. If it now justifies the suspicion, the suspicion seems to have created support for itself through its effect on experience. This is the cognitive-penetration objection surveyed through Siegel's example [@phen-con-iep, § 4.c].

The reply cannot simply say that appearances are not beliefs. The critic grants that distinction. The issue is whether the origin of an appearance can affect its justificatory power when the subject is unaware of that origin. Replies distinguish the belief's standing from the person's rationality or defend the support despite its history. The case remains disputed [@phen-con-iep, § 4.c].

### A regress motivation has limits

Defenders question a universal demand for an independently justified belief that appearances are reliable. Applying the demand to every justification can generate a higher-order regress. But rejecting that demand does not establish that every appearance supplies support. It clears space for immediate justification while leaving the positive principle and its scope to be defended [@phen-con-iep, § 4.b].
