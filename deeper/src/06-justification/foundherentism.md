> **In short.** Foundherentism combines experiential input with support among beliefs. The crossword analogy shows how a clue and already filled answers can constrain the same entry. Mutual fit helps, but it cannot replace the clues. Conversely, a clue can leave several answers open until the crossings narrow them down. The philosophical challenge is to explain both sources of support without turning either into an infallible starting point.

## Re-learn

### One answer, two constraints

An invented crossword clue asks for a five-letter watercraft. Several answers might initially be possible. Letters from other answers narrow the options. But you should not enter any word merely because its letters match: it must also answer the clue. Nor should you treat the first plausible answer as unquestionable and change every crossing to preserve it.

Olsson presents Haack's foundherentism as a theory combining coherentist and foundationalist ingredients, illustrated through crossword solving. The analogy makes room for two roles: input that supplies information and integration that connects it with other information. It does not say that a real belief literally has a clue or that every inference works by matching letters [@olsson2025, § 4].

In an everyday investigation, a thermometer reading can constrain a diagnosis while other observations help determine whether the reading was taken properly. The reading and the background beliefs do different work. A theory combining them must explain which claims depend on observation, which depend on inference, and how uncertainty in either affects the conclusion.

### Why fit alone is insufficient

A completed crossword can be internally neat while answering a different set of clues. Similarly, an account of an event can explain its own assumptions while failing to match the observations. The isolation objection to pure coherentism asks how internal organization connects a belief system to reality. Foundherentism's experiential ingredient addresses the need for input [@olsson2025, §§ 1, 4].

There is a reverse danger. One misread clue can spread mistakes through nearby answers. Treating input as relevant does not make every interpretation of it secure. Further information and cross-checks can revise the initial belief. The picture is therefore compatible with fallible, mutually constraining inquiry rather than a collection of incorrigible starting judgments.

## Beyond the chapter

### A hybrid is not just a compromise label

The IEP evidentialism survey identifies Haack's *Evidence and Inquiry* as a defense of a novel theory blending foundationalism and coherentism. This page uses that survey and Olsson's account; the book itself has not supplied evidence for detailed claims here. The accessible sources support the broad structural comparison, not an exhaustive reconstruction of Haack's technical account [@evidentia-iep, § 5.a; @olsson2025, § 4].

A successful hybrid must make positive commitments. What counts as experiential support? How much does an additional explanatory connection contribute? When does a conflict require abandoning the observation, revising its interpretation, or changing the background account? Merely announcing that both experience and coherence matter leaves these questions unanswered.

### Why the three traditional pictures overlap

Contemporary replies to Agrippa need not accept just one horn as conventionally described. Comesaña and Klein point to theories combining elements of foundationalism and coherentism, including Haack's. The three-part classification is useful for exposition, but a verdict about one simple version does not settle every combination [@comesana-klein2026, § 6.5].

Weak foundationalism already allows initial experiential credibility to be amplified through coherence. That overlap raises a classification question: which features make a hybrid distinctive rather than another version of modest foundationalism? The crossword is an illuminating model, but the answer requires a fuller account of dependence and evidential support [@olsson2025, §§ 1, 5].
