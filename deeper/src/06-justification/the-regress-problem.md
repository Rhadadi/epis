> **In short.** A reason can need support of its own, so an explanation of one belief can generate a chain of further beliefs. The regress problem asks what gives that chain justificatory force. It does not merely ask why someone eventually stops speaking. Some theories allow non-inferential support, some assess systems, and some permit indefinitely available further reasons. The skeptical outcome follows only if the proposed sources and structures all fail.

## Re-learn

### From a forecast to the forecast's basis

You believe rain is coming because a weather service predicts it. You trust the service because its forecasts have often been accurate. You believe that record is good because you remember comparing forecasts with the weather. You trust those memories because further evidence suggests your memory works well. Each answer introduces another consideration whose standing can be questioned.

The problem becomes general if every justified belief must derive all its support from other justified beliefs. Under that requirement, any final belief still needs a predecessor. A finite chain can stop unsupported or return to a previous belief; a nonrepeating chain continues indefinitely. Theories of justification dispute what those patterns can accomplish [@hasan-fumerton2022, § 1].

Foundationalism denies that every justified belief needs inferential support from another belief. A direct experience might supply support. Coherentism denies that support must be transmitted along a line from independently justified predecessors. Infinitism disputes the assumption that further reasons must end to provide justification. These replies concern the premise generating the regress as well as its possible shapes [@hasan-fumerton2022, § 1; @olsson2025, § 2; @inf-epis-iep, § 1].

### Stopping the conversation is not supplying a foundation

People stop giving reasons because they become tired, run out of time, or assume that an interlocutor understands. None of those facts alone establishes an epistemic foundation. A basic belief is one with non-inferential support, not the final sentence someone happened to utter. Likewise, an unfinished conversation does not prove that an infinite supply of further reasons exists.

The epistemic question concerns having support. The conversational question concerns presenting it. An interlocutor can demand more even when the believer already has adequate support; a believer can be unable to answer because her support is poor. Treating every unanswered challenge as proof of universal skepticism confuses these possibilities.

## Beyond the chapter

### Two different inferential demands

Hasan and Fumerton distinguish the demand that premises be justified from the demand that a person have justification for believing the premises support the conclusion. The latter can generate higher-order chains about the adequacy of inferences. An argument can become much more demanding when that second condition is introduced [@hasan-fumerton2022, § 1].

For instance, remembering previous accurate forecasts might support your reliability judgment. A further question asks whether you correctly appreciate how that sample bears on future forecasts. Requiring appropriate sensitivity to this connection differs from requiring a separately articulated argument proving every rule you use. A theory needs to say which demand it makes.

### Against a quick proof by exhaustion

Even if unsupported endpoints, vicious circles, and certain infinite chains fail, that does not yet identify a successful positive theory. The skeptical possibility remains. Nor does showing that linear reasoning fails refute a system-level account unless that account is shown to reduce to the linear model. The literature's regress arguments therefore need premises about what kinds of support are available [@hasan-fumerton2022, § 1; @olsson2025, § 2].
