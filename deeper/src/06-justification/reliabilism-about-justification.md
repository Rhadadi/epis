> **In short.** Reliabilism explains justification partly through processes that tend to produce true beliefs. A reliable process can make mistakes, and a correct result can arise from an unreliable process. The account must specify which process is being assessed and in what conditions. It must also explain how contrary evidence can defeat a belief that was reliably formed. Reliability is therefore a substantive relation between method, environment, and truth, rather than a synonym for feeling confident.

## Re-learn

### Reading and guessing

Two people give the same temperature estimate. One reads a working instrument; the other guesses. Even if both estimates are correct, the methods differ. A process reliabilist gives that difference a role in justification. The relevant tendency concerns the method's production of true rather than false beliefs, not just this one successful output [@steup-neta2025, § 3.2].

A method can be mostly reliable without being infallible. A careful reading can fail when an unusual malfunction occurs. Conversely, one lucky success does not validate a generally poor method. This makes process evaluation different from checking whether a conclusion happened to be true.

For inferential beliefs, input matters too. A dependable inference procedure used on false, unsupported premises need not generate justified conclusions. Reliabilist accounts can assess both the premises' standing and the conditional reliability of the inferential process. It is not enough to count the rule's successes while ignoring where its inputs came from [@goldman-beddor2021, § 3.2].

### The believer need not know the reliability

If justification depends partly on actual reliability, it can depend on something a person cannot establish by reflection alone. That makes typical process reliabilism externalist. A subject's experience of using an instrument and the instrument's real performance are different factors [@steup-neta2025, § 3.3].

The theory does not thereby make accessible evidence irrelevant. Someone can receive evidence that her instrument is broken even when it works. Many accounts need conditions handling such defeat. A reliable initial formation is not necessarily enough to justify retaining a belief through later contrary information [@goldman-beddor2021, § 2.4].

## Beyond the chapter

### Which process type?

A single perceptual event belongs to many types: perception, vision, vision in daylight, or recognition of this kind of object under these conditions. Those types can have different success rates. The generality problem asks which type determines the belief's epistemic standing and why. Selecting a description merely because it gives the desired result would make the assessment arbitrary [@goldman-beddor2021, § 2.3].

This is a practical problem too. A test validated on one population may perform differently in another. Calling both uses the same method can conceal the relevant difference; defining each use so narrowly that no comparisons remain can conceal it in the other direction. Appropriate process description is part of the account, not a harmless preliminary label.

### Which environment?

The new evil demon case compares ordinary believers with internally matching people whose experiences are systematically deceptive. A simple actual-environment success requirement denies reliability to the latter. Internalists argue that their justification should nevertheless match. Some reliabilists revise the environment in which the process is assessed, while others distinguish justification from blamelessness or rationality [@littlejohn2025, § 2.4].

Norman's reliable clairvoyance tests the opposite direction: whether a reliable deliverance without accessible grounds should count as justified. The two cases press necessity and sufficiency respectively. Neither is answered simply by observing that ordinary perception is often reliable [@littlejohn2025, §§ 2.1, 2.4].
