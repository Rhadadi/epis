> **In short.** Coherentism locates justification in how beliefs fit together as a system. Fitting means more than avoiding contradiction: explanation, evidential connections, and responsiveness to information can matter. A whole-system account does not simply authorize arguing in a circle. Its central problem is why internal fit should indicate anything about the world. Agreement can improve evidence, but only an account of the sources and their relations explains when it does.

## Re-learn

### Three observations, one explanation

Suppose a bicycle disappears from a locked shed. The lock is broken, tire tracks leave the shed, and a neighbor reports seeing someone ride away. The theft explanation connects these pieces. Each also constrains the others: tracks alone might have been old, and a neighbor's report might have been mistaken. Their fit can make the combined explanation more persuasive than any isolated item.

Coherentism makes a general proposal about this kind of fit. A sufficiently coherent system is justified, and individual beliefs can be justified through their contribution to that system. It opposes a picture in which support must start with independently justified beliefs and travel in one direction. But a belief's mere membership in a largely coherent collection is not enough; an unsupported item tucked into an otherwise good account remains a problem [@olsson2025, § 2].

### Consistent does not mean well-supported

The claim that a bicycle was stolen by an invisible visitor might be compatible with the other observations. Compatibility alone does not explain why you should believe it. A system can contain no contradiction while adding arbitrary details. Traditional accounts therefore appeal to mutual support, explanatory relations, or probabilistic connections as well as consistency [@olsson2025, § 3].

These connections differ. A conclusion can be entailed by premises; it can instead become more probable given them; or it can form part of an explanation that unifies observations. Replacing all three with the vague word fit conceals the work a coherentist theory must do. The account needs to say which relations confer justification and how conflicts among them should be resolved.

### Ten retellings are not ten independent checks

If ten people repeat one neighbor's story, their agreement does not supply ten separate observations. If they saw the event from different locations and reported independently, their convergence can have different significance. The contents may agree equally in both cases. Their evidential value depends on more than the contents' agreement [@olsson2025, §§ 5, 8].

This distinction exposes coherentism's central task. A convincing story and a well-supported story can share the same internal organization. To explain why one deserves belief, the theory must make clear what brings information from the world into the system.

## The full story

### Holism changes the unit of assessment

The regress problem asks how each supporting belief becomes justified. If support always moves from one independently justified belief to another, a circle appears unable to generate anything. The coherentist's strongest response rejects that transmission picture. Systems can be the primary bearers of justification; their members can inherit support through appropriate participation in the whole [@olsson2025, § 2].

This is different from the following exchange: the witness is trustworthy because the bicycle was stolen, and the bicycle was stolen because the witness says so. That exchange repeats what is disputed. A holistic account instead assesses a richer collection containing the observation, the testimony, background information about the shed, competing explanations, and possible corrections. Whether that collection supplies justification depends on the substantive account of coherence [@olsson2025, § 2].

The distinction helps explain why circularity objections need careful targets. Some systems contain mutually reinforcing inferences; that is not yet a proof that they are vicious. Conversely, calling a system holistic does not give every member support. A theory must explain which connections matter and why a peripheral unsupported belief does not receive a free ride. Olsson explicitly raises this membership problem [@olsson2025, § 2].

::: argument The coherentist response to a linear regress
1. The familiar regress assumes that individual beliefs acquire justification through already justified predecessors.
2. Justification might instead belong primarily to sufficiently coherent systems.
3. A member's justification could derive from its contribution to such a system.
4. If that account succeeds, a demand for a first independently justified member misdescribes the source of support.
:::
[@olsson2025, § 2]. The conclusion is conditional. Premises 2 and 3 require a theory of system justification and contribution. They are proposals for stopping the regress argument from getting started, rather than an independent demonstration that every coherent system is justified.

### Three kinds of coherence

Ewing's account combines consistency with a demanding inferential relation: each belief follows logically from the others taken together. The survey observes that many natural belief sets fail this condition while still looking coherent. A suspect's presence, possession of a relevant weapon, and unusual bank deposit can support one another without any two logically entailing the third [@olsson2025, § 3].

Lewis replaces logical entailment with probabilistic support. On his account, the other members raise the probability of each member. This permits ordinary evidential connections that are weaker than deduction. It also raises questions about which comparisons matter: support for individual members, support for groups of members, or some measure covering the whole collection [@olsson2025, § 3].

BonJour's account uses several dimensions: logical and probabilistic consistency, inferential connections, integration between subsystems, and the absence of unexplained anomalies. This captures a richer idea of organized belief, but introduces a problem of weighting. One account can have more connections while another has fewer anomalies. Without a way to compare those advantages, an overall coherence judgment remains underdetermined [@olsson2025, § 3].

::: positions Accounts of fit and their difficulties
| View | Example discussed in the literature | Main relation | Question it faces |
|---|---|---|---|
| Logical | Ewing | Each member follows from the others | Is the requirement too strict for ordinary inquiry? |
| Probabilistic | Lewis | The others raise a member's probability | Which members and subsets should be compared? |
| Multidimensional | BonJour | Consistency, connection, integration, few anomalies | How are the dimensions combined? |
| Explanatory | Lycan and Poston | Beliefs belong to strong explanations | What makes explanation epistemically good? |
:::
[@olsson2025, §§ 3, 4]. These are accounts surveyed in the literature, not four universally agreed definitions. A theory of explanation can overlap with a probabilistic theory, and an account can incorporate more than one relation.

### More connections can be cheap

A belief system becomes larger when you add new beliefs. It then has more possible connections. Counting connections without considering size can reward expansion for its own sake. The survey notes this problem for a simple reading of BonJour's connection criterion and considers a density measure as a possible modification [@olsson2025, § 3].

The bicycle example illustrates the issue. Adding fifty guesses about the thief's motives might create many links to the theft hypothesis. Those links need not improve the evidence for theft. A system containing a smaller number of well-grounded observations can be epistemically stronger. A useful measure must distinguish meaningful integration from proliferation of claims devised to fit a favored conclusion.

The same caution applies to anomalies. A theory can reduce anomalies by adding a separate rescue assumption for each troublesome observation. The resulting account may fit all the data while becoming less unified or less independently testable. Coherence understood as explanation therefore needs constraints on what counts as a good explanatory connection. A numerical count of unexplained observations cannot supply those constraints alone.

### A primary critic separates truth from a test of truth

::: original Russell on the limits of fit
> For the above two reasons, coherence cannot be accepted as giving the _meaning_ of truth, though it is often a most important _test_ of truth after a certain amount of truth has become known.
:::
[@russell1912, ch. XII]. Russell's chapter concerns theories of truth. He argues that more than one coherent system is possible and that assessing coherence already uses logical standards. His qualification preserves a possible evidential role for coherence even while rejecting it as the meaning of truth. That distinction prevents a common confusion in discussions of justification.

A theory of justification asks when someone has adequate grounds. A theory of truth asks what makes a proposition true. A coherentist about justification need not define truth as agreement within a system. She might accept correspondence with the world while claiming that coherent integration is our source of justification. Russell's objection to a definition of truth therefore does not directly refute every coherentist epistemology [@russell1912, ch. XII; @olsson2025, § 1].

It does nevertheless reveal a problem such epistemologies must address. If incompatible systems can fit equally well, what selects one as a better guide to the world? Having reasonable grounds for a false belief is possible, so equal justification does not entail equal truth. But an account that routinely treats insulated inventions as fully justified risks losing the epistemic connection it was meant to explain [@olsson2025, § 1].

### The isolation objection

The isolation objection targets coherence understood entirely inside a system. A novel can be carefully consistent. A delusion can integrate new events into its own narrative. Those systems may be richly interconnected while lacking appropriate contact with what they claim to describe. Why should their internal organization confer the same justification as a system responsive to observation [@olsson2025, § 1]?

Influential accounts give beliefs close to experience a special role. The survey discusses Lewis's supposed facts asserted, Rescher's truth-candidates, and BonJour's cognitively spontaneous beliefs. Such proposals try to preserve input without assigning every observational belief independent certainty. But the status of that input becomes decisive. Does it have some initial credibility, or is all its credibility generated by the system [@olsson2025, §§ 1, 2]?

If input has initial credibility, even a little, the account may resemble weak foundationalism. Coherence then amplifies or organizes support that does not arise wholly from coherence. If input has no initial credibility, the theory owes an account of how system relations create support from scratch. These are different ambitions, and the success of the former does not automatically vindicate the latter [@olsson2025, § 5].

The distinction is useful even outside philosophy. Combining several imperfect sensors can improve a measurement if their readings have informative connections to the target. Combining several arbitrary numbers does not become a measurement merely because they agree. Calling both collections integrated conceals the difference that makes one useful.

### The Lewis–BonJour dispute

Lewis's witness example concerns convergence among individually weak reports. The agreement may be much less likely if the account is false than if it is true. The combined reports can therefore supply stronger support than each report alone. Yet Lewis does not say that all initial credibility can be removed. The survey classifies his position as weak foundationalism on precisely that ground [@olsson2025, § 5].

The philosophical issue is whether coherence can supply justification from scratch. Suppose a report initially offers no reason at all to favor its content. What does a second equally uninformative report add? Agreement might point to a shared source or common error rather than to truth. A response needs assumptions linking the reporting process to the reported state, or an explanation of why such assumptions are unnecessary [@olsson2025, § 5].

These questions differ from whether a weakly credible report can become strong through corroboration. Most practical examples concern that second situation. An uncertain witness may still have seen the event, and a noisy sensor may still respond to the measured property. Such examples support the value of combining information while leaving pure coherence theories open to further challenge.

### Probability makes the challenge more precise

Formal treatments distinguish report contents from reports. If three people each say that the bicycle was stolen, there are three reporting events but one repeated content. Representing the system as the simple set containing *the bicycle was stolen* loses the repetition. An ordered representation of report contents preserves it [@olsson2025, § 6].

A coherence measure can then assess relationships among those contents and their probabilities. But a measure of content relationships is not automatically a measure of source reliability. The same content pattern can be produced by three independent witnesses, one witness and two copies, or a coordinated fabrication. Information about the reporting processes has to enter the truth assessment somewhere [@olsson2025, §§ 6, 8].

Even defining coherence by joint probability is problematic. Two people making exactly the same specific allegation differ in their agreement from two people making broader disjunctive allegations that happen to overlap at that allegation. The conjunctions can have the same probability while the patterns of agreement differ. The survey uses this kind of case to motivate more discriminating measures [@olsson2025, § 6].

The mathematics is valuable because it prevents an intuitive example from silently changing its assumptions. A result about independently generated credible reports should not be applied to copied reports. A result holding source credibility fixed should not be treated as a comparison between sources of very different quality. A claim about a particular measure should not become a claim about everything anyone calls coherence.

### What the impossibility results do and do not show

The survey describes impossibility results under specified formal conditions. Even assuming conditional independence and individual credibility, no coherence measure in the relevant framework guarantees that higher coherence means higher probability of truth in the required general sense. The comparison holds individual credibility fixed; otherwise source quality could trivially overwhelm a coherence difference [@olsson2025, § 8].

This is stronger than saying that copying can produce misleading agreement. It says that favorable source conditions still do not supply the hoped-for universal ordering by coherence alone. The result constrains a precise truth-conduciveness ambition. It does not establish that corroboration never helps, that independent witnesses provide no evidence, or that a coherentist cannot use a different account [@olsson2025, § 8].

Replies can weaken the claimed connection to truth or question the formal assumptions. The survey discusses weaker defeasible connections, possible relations to reliability in restricted cases, and challenges to conditional-independence or content-determination assumptions. Each response changes what is being defended. A weaker practical guide is not the universal theorem initially sought [@olsson2025, § 8].

Coherence also has a negative role. Discovering that your beliefs conflict, or that a proposed explanation cannot accommodate a well-supported observation, can give you a reason to revise. That role survives even if increased coherence cannot always be ranked as increased probability of truth. Identifying a defect can be easier than measuring the complete epistemic quality of a corrected system [@olsson2025, § 8].

### Keep the practical lesson proportionate

When investigating the missing bicycle, combine observations, test alternative explanations, and examine where reports came from. Those are applications of evidential integration. They do not require settling whether all justification originates in a coherent system. A foundationalist can value corroboration, and an infinitist can value explanatory fit. Practical agreement about inquiry does not erase the disagreement about the ultimate structure of support.

Conversely, the philosophical debate can improve practical inquiry by exposing misleading shortcuts. Ask whether an apparent second source is independent. Distinguish a newly observed fact from a restatement of the theory. Notice when an explanation becomes flexible enough to accommodate any outcome. Keep unsupported details from borrowing authority from better-supported neighbors. These questions give content to the demand for coherence without treating a polished narrative as sufficient evidence.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the witness example simplifies

Independence is one relevant condition, not a complete explanation of evidential strength. Initial credibility, background probability, the contents reported, and the way the reports were generated also matter. The survey's formal results make this limitation explicit. A slogan that independent agreement always makes every reported claim more probable goes beyond what the literature establishes [@olsson2025, §§ 5–8].
