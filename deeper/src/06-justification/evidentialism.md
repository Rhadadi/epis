> **In short.** Evidentialism connects justified belief with what a person's evidence supports at a time. Support can favor believing, disbelieving, or suspending judgment. The view needs accounts of what evidence is, what it means to possess it, and how it supports an attitude. Possessing support does not automatically make an actual belief well-based. Nor does the theory of present support by itself settle whether the person conducted a responsible inquiry.

## Re-learn

### One envelope, three possible attitudes

You receive an envelope that might contain your test result. Before opening it, the envelope supplies little reason to believe you passed or failed. Suspension is appropriate. Reading a clear pass result changes the evidence. Learning that it belongs to somebody else changes the connection between that result and your own performance.

Evidentialism makes the available evidence central to which attitude is justified. Feldman and Conee formulate it for belief, disbelief, and suspension, rather than treating every case as a choice between belief and unbelief. The IEP survey also stresses that evidentialism is a family of theories whose accounts of evidence, possession, and support can differ [@evidentia-iep, preamble; @evidentia-iep, § 2].

Evidence need not be a written argument. Perceptual experiences, memories, and seemings can be candidates, depending on the account. A fact elsewhere in the world is not automatically part of your evidence merely because an investigator could discover it. The standard internalist versions discussed in the survey focus on information the person mentally possesses [@evidentia-iep, § 2.b].

### Support and basing

Suppose the test result gives you good support for passing, but you believe you passed only because a lucky charm promised success. Your evidence fits the proposition while the belief's actual basis is poor. An evidentialist account of doxastic justification therefore needs an appropriate basing relation in addition to its account of what the evidence supports [@evidentia-iep, § 2.a].

The distinction prevents a convenient after-the-fact defense. Finding evidence that agrees with your conclusion is valuable, but you must also let it inform the belief. A contrary result should matter. Otherwise evidence becomes a decoration attached to a conclusion determined independently of it.

## Beyond the chapter

### Present evidence and responsible inquiry

Imagine that today's service timetable is readily available, but you rely on yesterday's time and arrive late. Critics argue that your failure to check defeats justification. Feldman's response distinguishes what your possessed evidence now supports from whether you ought to have gathered more evidence. The IEP entry presents this disagreement through his cinema example [@evidentia-iep, § 3.c.ii].

That response is substantive, not a rule that inquiry never matters. A critic can maintain that epistemic justification includes responsible acquisition or sensitivity to readily discoverable defeating information. An evidentialist can preserve separate evaluations of present belief and inquiry. Stating which evaluation is being made explains why the same case can elicit different verdicts.

### Moral and practical demands

A comforting belief might benefit a person while conflicting with her evidence. An evidentialist can distinguish epistemic support from moral or prudential considerations rather than treating every benefit as evidence. The survey discusses conflicts among these demands and warns that their relation needs a philosophical account [@evidentia-iep, § 3.c.iii].

This distinction also applies to action. You can take precautions against a possibility without believing it occurred. Bringing an umbrella because rain is possible does not require changing your estimate of its likelihood. A practical threshold for action and an epistemic standard for belief answer different questions.
