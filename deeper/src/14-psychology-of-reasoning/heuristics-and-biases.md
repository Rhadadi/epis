> **In short.** A heuristic simplifies a judgment or decision. A bias is a systematic departure from a relevant standard, not just any isolated mistake. A simplifying rule can work well in one environment and badly in another. To assess it, identify the task, the information it uses, and the information it ignores. Naming a bias does not yet explain its mechanism or provide a remedy.

## Re-learn

### A shortcut with a job

Imagine a fictional buyer choosing a replacement part. She first excludes parts that do not fit, then chooses the least expensive remaining one. This is a simple rule that ignores several other features. It might be useful when the qualifying parts are equally reliable. If durability differs greatly, the same shortcut can select a poor option.

Wheeler contrasts the heuristics-and-biases tradition with the fast-and-frugal programme. The former investigates characteristic deviations from standards; the latter specifies simplifying algorithms and the environments where they perform effectively. Both recognise simplification, but their explanatory questions differ. [@wheeler2024, § 5.1, 5.2]

The fast-and-frugal approach makes search, stopping, and decision rules explicit. This is more informative than merely saying the buyer uses intuition. It states where information enters, when further search ends, and how a conclusion is selected. [@wheeler2024, § 5.2]

### A bias requires a comparison

Bazerman explains anchoring and framing as patterns affecting judgment. Anchoring concerns the influence of an initial value; framing concerns responses to alternative presentations of equivalent outcomes. The standard and equivalence matter: if the presentations provide genuinely different information, a changed response does not by itself demonstrate a framing bias. [@bazerman-noba, Anchoring, Framing]

For the fictional buyer, an arbitrary suggested price might influence her estimate. But a quoted price could also supply real information about the market. The distinction requires asking what the initial value represents and whether the response appropriately uses that information.

## Beyond the chapter

### The task can be represented differently

Wheeler discusses objections concerning normative standards, participants’ interpretation of instructions, and generalisation beyond a particular task. Changes in wording or sampling can change performance. These issues qualify a diagnosis; they do not show that every apparent error is secretly optimal. [@wheeler2024, § 5.1]

A person might understand an everyday request for a likely answer as asking which description is most representative, rather than as a formal probability comparison. The researcher needs evidence about that interpretation. A mathematical standard can still be appropriate, but its application to the actual response needs explanation.

### Less information is not always better

The bias–variance discussion shows how a simpler prediction model can avoid fitting noise, while also warning that the trade-off depends on how error is measured. Ignoring information is neither inherently rational nor inherently irrational. Compare performance on relevant new cases under the intended loss measure. [@wheeler2024, § 3.5]

The buyer’s price rule illustrates that dependence. It can save time but fail if durability is central. Adding more criteria can help, yet inaccurate estimates of those criteria may introduce new error. The right comparison considers both the available information and the purpose of the choice.

See [ecological rationality](deeper:14-psychology-of-reasoning/ecological-rationality) for environmental fit and [common probabilistic errors](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/common-errors-in-probabilistic-reasoning) for standards involving probabilities. Those distinctions prevent a catalogue of bias names from becoming a catalogue of accusations.
