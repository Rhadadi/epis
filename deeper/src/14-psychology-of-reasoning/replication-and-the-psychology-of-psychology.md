> **In short.** Research about reasoning needs the same scrutiny as other empirical research. Repeating an analysis, repeating an experiment, and testing a hypothesis in a new setting are different achievements. A successful repetition does not establish unlimited generality. A failed repetition needs interpretation in light of design and uncertainty. Publication incentives can distort the visible record even when individual studies are competently conducted.

## Re-learn

### What was repeated?

Imagine a fictional study finding that a reminder improves accuracy on a numerical task. Another analyst runs the same code on the same data. A second laboratory recruits new participants and follows the method. A third uses a different task to test the proposed mechanism. These projects answer different questions.

Fidler and Wilcox distinguish computational reproduction, repetition of methods, successful reproduction of results, and direct versus conceptual replication. They also note that disciplinary terminology varies. An account should specify the operation rather than assume that one label has the same meaning everywhere. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 1]

Recovering the published number from the original data would establish an important kind of reproducibility. It would not show that the intervention works with new participants. A new result on another task might support generality while leaving a question about whether the same mechanism produced it.

### A result has uncertainty

Suppose the second laboratory finds a smaller effect. That difference might reflect sampling variation, a changed procedure, a moderator, or an inflated initial estimate. Comparing only whether each result crosses a significance threshold can conceal the estimates and their uncertainty. The target is the relevant claim and evidence, not a binary contest between two publications.

## Beyond the chapter

### The visible literature is selected

Fidler and Wilcox describe publication biases against replications and towards significant or positive findings. They also discuss flexible analyses, selective reporting, and presenting post hoc hypotheses as if they had been predicted. These practices can make the published record more impressive than the underlying evidence. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 2.2, 2.3]

The fictional reminder might have been one of many attempts, with only the successful comparison published. If so, judging it as though it were the sole planned test would misrepresent the evidential situation. This is a possible selection mechanism, not an allegation about any named study.

### Planning and reporting address different failures

Preregistration makes predictions or plans visible before relevant outcomes are known. Registered reports move review of the research design before results, with publication conditional on appropriate completion rather than a favoured outcome. Fidler and Wilcox explain these reforms and the distinction between exploratory and confirmatory work. Exploration remains valuable when represented honestly. [@fidler-wilcox2026, § 4.2, 4.3]

Neither reform guarantees a good measure, representative sample, or correct theory. A preregistered mistake remains a mistake. A transparent exploratory result can still contribute to later investigation. The improvements concern identifiable risks in planning, selection, and reporting, not a certification of truth.

For a particular bias claim, ask what was measured, what comparison was planned, what results were available, and what later work tests. Read [the replication crisis](deeper:10-science-and-evidence/the-replication-crisis) and [statistics and significance](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/statistics-and-significance) for the wider framework. Skepticism about a specific claim should remain answerable to evidence too.
