> **In short.** Forecasting makes a probability claim answerable to a later outcome. A good record needs clearly resolved questions and a consistent scoring rule. Superforecaster performance can reflect selection, skill, training, engagement, and teamwork. These contributions should not be conflated. The strongest lesson is to examine the design and the record before turning successful forecasts into unlimited authority.

## Re-learn

### A claim that can be scored

Imagine a fictional forecast that a delivery will arrive before noon with probability 0.7. Once the deadline passes, the event either occurred or did not. A scoring rule can compare the probability with that outcome. One outcome does not prove the probability was correct; a suitable series of forecasts provides a record for assessment.

Mellers and colleagues report a two-year geopolitical forecasting tournament, with training, group arrangements, and performance tracking. Their study encourages updates before questions close and scores probabilities using proper scoring rules. The design makes accuracy observable rather than inferred from a forecaster’s reputation. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF p. 4, PDF p. 5]

The paper uses a Brier score summing squared errors across outcomes. For the invented two-outcome forecast, arrival gives a score of 0.18; non-arrival gives 0.98. A convention using only the event’s squared error gives half those values. State the convention before comparing numerical scores. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF pp. 4–5; @mellers-etal2014, PDF p. 5]

### A good probability is not a promise

Events assigned a high probability can fail to occur. Calibration asks how predicted probabilities relate to frequencies across appropriate cases; the paper distinguishes this from resolution and other aspects of performance. Predicting the base rate for every case can be calibrated while providing little discrimination. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF p. 7]

Read [calibration and scoring rules](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/calibration-and-scoring-rules) for that distinction. Evaluate forecasts as probabilities, rather than treating every realised surprise as a demonstration that uncertainty was dishonest.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the tournament shows

The study reports improvements associated with training and teaming. Its Year 1 design randomly assigns participants to conditions, while elite Year 2 teams select top performers from Year 1. The selected teams’ strong performance is important, but it does not isolate the effect of placing any arbitrary participant in such a team. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF p. 4, PDF p. 5, PDF p. 7]

The recruited population also has particular entry requirements and characteristics. It is not a representative sample of everyone who makes everyday judgments. Claims about transfer to other domains need evidence about those domains, tasks, and conditions. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF p. 4]

### A record needs a comparator

For the fictional delivery forecast, compare performance with an appropriate baseline, such as the historical arrival rate for similar deliveries. Preserve the question’s resolution rule, timestamp, and updates. Otherwise a forecaster can appear accurate by changing the question after the outcome or remembering only the successes.

Good performance on that record would support competence for a relevant task. It would not establish expertise about every cause of delays or every other future event. The [experts and novices page](deeper:12-social-epistemology/experts-and-novices) explains why authority should remain task specific.

The paper also distinguishes opportunities to share information from risks of herding and poor group dynamics. Teamwork can be part of a useful process without making consensus infallible. Ask what the team contributed and how its conclusions remained correctable. [@mellers-etal2014, PDF p. 3]
