> **In short.** Epistemology evaluates belief and inquiry; psychology helps explain how people perform them. Describing a common response does not establish that it is correct. Conversely, advice must account for the capabilities and conditions of the person who is supposed to follow it. Research can identify errors, investigate their causes, and test remedies. Those are separate tasks with different evidential requirements.

## Re-learn

### A standard and a way to meet it

Imagine a fictional student checking a calculation. Arithmetic supplies a standard for the result. Psychology can investigate how she represents the problem, remembers intermediate values, and notices mistakes. Advice might recommend writing the intermediate values down. The advice depends on both the standard and the practical obstacle.

Wheeler distinguishes descriptive, normative, and prescriptive projects. A descriptive account addresses what an agent does; a normative account identifies a standard of assessment; a prescriptive account recommends a procedure suitable for the agent’s capabilities. Confusing them can turn an abstract ideal into unusable advice, or a frequent behaviour into its own justification. [@wheeler2024, § 1.5]

For the student, discovering that many classmates give one answer would not prove it arithmetically correct. Discovering that a correct algorithm exceeds her current memory capacity would not make arithmetic change. It would give a reason to alter the available procedure or provide support.

### What reliability asks

Whether a procedure usually succeeds is a factual question about its operation in relevant conditions. It differs from whether its output on this occasion is correct. A lucky guess can succeed once; a generally good procedure can encounter an unusual failure. Evaluating the procedure therefore requires a suitable set of tasks and conditions, rather than counting one success as a demonstration.

Bounded-rationality research considers both the costs of decision procedures and the fit between procedures and environments. It asks how limited agents can make effective judgments without assuming unlimited computation. This gives epistemology reasons to attend to processes and resources as well as propositions. [@wheeler2024, § 2.1, 3.1]

## Beyond the chapter

### An error and its explanation

The same wrong response can arise from misunderstanding, missing information, or a defective strategy. Wheeler’s discussion of judgment tasks stresses that participants’ representations and the experimenter’s intended standard can differ. A finding about performance therefore needs an explanation before it becomes a sweeping verdict on human rationality. [@wheeler2024, § 5.1]

The fictional student might have multiplied when asked to add because she misread a symbol. Teaching her a better addition algorithm would then target the wrong failure. Conversely, if she routinely loses a remembered intermediate value, merely clarifying the symbol would leave the difficulty in place.

### Testing the remedy

Bazerman’s overview distinguishes awareness of biases from interventions aimed at improving decisions, including changes to the decision environment. Knowing a label is different from having a usable corrective procedure. An intervention’s effect must be assessed rather than inferred from the attractiveness of its message. [@bazerman-noba, Fixing Our Decisions]

A written calculation sheet for the student is a proposed support, not a reported experimental result. Assessment would compare performance on relevant tasks and consider whether the sheet introduces new errors. It would also distinguish immediate success from learning that transfers to other problems.

This connects to [dual-process theories](deeper:14-psychology-of-reasoning/dual-process-theories), which propose explanations of processing, and [debiasing](deeper:14-psychology-of-reasoning/debiasing-what-works), which evaluates interventions. A sound explanation and a useful remedy can support one another without being the same achievement.
