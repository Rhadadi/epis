> **In short.** Processing fluency concerns how easy information is to process. Ease and familiarity can affect judgments without supplying new evidence for a claim. The illusory truth effect concerns higher perceived truth associated with repetition. A repeated statement can feel familiar because many copies trace back to one source. Assess the underlying support and independence rather than counting appearances.

## Re-learn

### Familiarity and support

Imagine a fictional claim that a particular battery lasts twice as long as its competitor. You encounter it in an advertisement, a repost, and a shopping guide copied from the advertisement. On the third encounter, the wording is familiar. But the three appearances contain only one original assertion.

The Decision Lab’s secondary overview describes the illusory truth effect and explains fluency and familiarity as proposed mechanisms. It also presents a referential account involving coherent connections in memory. These are explanations of judgments of truth, not demonstrations that repeated content is false. [@illusory-truth-tdl, Familiarity making processing easy, Referential theory]

Repetition can occur for true claims too. Conversely, three independent measurements could provide additional support even if expressed in similar words. What matters epistemically is the informational contribution and dependence of the sources, rather than novelty or repetition alone.

### Feeling easy is not a measurement

The overview recounts Hasher, Goldstein, and Toppino’s early study in which repeated statements were rated more accurate across sessions. It notes that those items were plausible and not necessarily already known to participants. The page uses that account as a secondary reading; it does not provide an independently checked effect size or a claim about every audience. [@illusory-truth-tdl, How it all started]

For the fictional battery, readable wording might help you understand the assertion. That benefit does not establish the test conditions, sample size, or comparison. Ease of comprehension and evidential support are different properties.

## Beyond the chapter

### Different outcomes need different evidence

Perceived truth, liking, remembering, believing, and sharing are distinguishable outcomes. The secondary overview connects fluency with familiarity and positive evaluation, but a finding about one outcome should not automatically be extended to every other. A person can remember a claim clearly while rejecting it, or pass it along to criticise it. [@illusory-truth-tdl, Familiarity making processing easy]

This limits practical conclusions. Merely observing that a message was repeated does not show that repetition caused someone to adopt it. An evaluation needs an appropriate comparison and a measure of the relevant response. The battery example identifies a possible mechanism and an evidential dependence, rather than supplying experimental data.

### Checking without an automatic counter-rule

For the battery assertion, trace the shopping guide to its source and look for an actual test. Record whether the advertised comparison concerns the same use conditions. These are proposed steps with an intelligible target: replacing a feeling of familiarity with evidence about performance.

Do not adopt the opposite rule that unfamiliar statements are more credible. Novelty is no guarantee of truth. Nor does the overview alone establish a universal rule about how to phrase corrections or how many repetitions make a message persuasive. Claims about those interventions require further evidence.

The connection with [echo chambers and bubbles](deeper:12-social-epistemology/echo-chambers-and-epistemic-bubbles) concerns informational dependence and access. The connection with [heuristics](deeper:14-psychology-of-reasoning/heuristics-and-biases) concerns a cue’s usefulness in its environment. Familiarity may be informative in some settings, but its value needs an account of how it arose.
