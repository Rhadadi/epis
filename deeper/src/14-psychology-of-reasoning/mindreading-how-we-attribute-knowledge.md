> **In short.** Mindreading means interpreting other people in terms of beliefs, knowledge, desires, and intentions. It involves ordinary inference rather than literal access to another mind. Your knowledge of the situation can interfere with representing what someone else knows. Several capacities contribute, including attention to perspective, background information, and the person’s behaviour. A mistaken attribution can affect communication even when your own factual belief is correct.

## Re-learn

### Two informational positions

Imagine a fictional colleague who leaves a file in the blue cabinet. While she is away, you move it to the green cabinet. You know its new location. She has not received that information. Predicting where she will search requires distinguishing the world’s current state from her informational position.

Malle’s Noba module explains theory of mind, also called mentalising or mindreading, as a collection of concepts and processes for understanding mental states. It distinguishes automatic relating from more explicit inference based on information about the person and situation. The label does not imply that everyone possesses an explicit psychological theory. [@malle-noba, Introduction, Explicit Mental State Inference]

The module describes false-belief tasks and the difficulty of suppressing one’s own better knowledge. Such tasks assess a particular performance under particular demands. They are not a direct window into every capacity a child or adult possesses. [@malle-noba, Explicit Mental State Inference]

### What the other person had access to

For the colleague, ask what she saw, heard, or was told before attributing knowledge. A glance at the room might not reveal the file’s location. Receiving a message might supply the information, but only if she read and understood it. Access, uptake, belief, and knowledge are different steps.

That distinction also explains why communication can fail despite clear words. You might describe the green cabinet as the usual location, assuming shared information that the colleague lacks. Her confusion would not demonstrate that she is inattentive or unreasonable.

## Beyond the chapter

### Projection can be useful and misleading

Malle discusses simulation and social projection: using one’s own mental state as a model of another person’s. This can be useful when backgrounds and circumstances are shared, and misleading when they are not. The module reviews examples involving knowledge and perspective, rather than establishing that all mindreading is egocentric. [@malle-noba, Projection, Simulation (and the Specter of Egocentrism)]

In the fictional case, imagining where you would look now gives the wrong prediction because you know about the move. Imagining what you would do with the colleague’s information is a different operation. The latter requires reconstructing the relevant informational history.

### Shared attention is another contribution

The module describes joint attention and perspective taking as parts of social understanding. Following what someone is attending to can help establish a shared referent; taking another spatial viewpoint can help interpret directions. Those contributions differ from inferring a complex belief about a disputed topic. [@malle-noba, Joint Attention, Visual Perspective Taking]

Consequently, a successful gaze-following or location task does not establish every richer form of epistemic attribution. Likewise, difficulties with one task should not be inflated into a global claim about a person’s capacity for understanding others.

Read [testimony](deeper:07-sources-of-knowledge/testimony) for the transfer of information and [experts and novices](deeper:12-social-epistemology/experts-and-novices) for competence in a particular subject. The ability to represent someone’s belief is distinct from determining whether it is well supported or amounts to knowledge.
