> **In short.** Confirmation bias concerns favouring evidence or tests that support a hypothesis. Myside bias concerns favouring one’s own position in producing or evaluating reasons. Seeking support is not always a bad strategy. The difficulty arises when the inquiry cannot discriminate the preferred claim from relevant alternatives. Evaluate what a test could reveal, rather than whether its result feels reassuring.

## Re-learn

### Confirmation can leave the rival untouched

Imagine an invented inspection rule: a mark indicates that a component is either aluminium or steel. A worker guesses that every marked component is aluminium and inspects only marked aluminium pieces. Each observation fits the guess, but none distinguishes it from the broader rule. A marked steel piece would matter because the two hypotheses predict different results there.

The lesson is a logical comparison, not a report about how many workers would make this mistake. Some confirming observations can discriminate hypotheses well. Others fit every live alternative. The value of a test depends on that relationship, not simply on whether the favoured hypothesis predicts success.

Bazerman’s account includes searches favouring confirmation when disconfirming information would be more informative. Mercier and Sperber discuss the search for supportive arguments and connect it to their argumentative theory. The pattern and the proposed explanation should remain distinct. [@bazerman-noba, Framing; @mercier-sperber2011, PDF p. 6]

### A position changes the comparison

Suppose the worker publicly proposed the aluminium rule. Defending it now matters to her standing. She may become inventive at explaining exceptions while dismissing equally elaborate replies from others. That is a possible myside pattern in this fictional case, not evidence that commitment invariably produces bias.

Mercier and Sperber review studies of argument production and biased evaluation of arguments with favoured or unfavoured conclusions. Their account also notes that argumentative settings can change the quality of the reasoning displayed. The review does not establish that every disagreement or every supporting argument is biased. [@mercier-sperber2011, PDF p. 6, PDF p. 11]

## Beyond the chapter

### Standards can differ legitimately

Different hypotheses may have different prior support, measurement procedures, or implications. Applying different scrutiny is not automatically unfair. The worker could have strong independent evidence about aluminium pieces and very little about steel. The question is whether the asymmetry follows from relevant differences rather than ownership of the proposal.

Wheeler’s discussion of experimental tasks warns that a response needs to be assessed against the problem participants actually represent, with the normative assumptions specified. This also applies to practical accusations of bias: the comparator must be genuinely relevant. [@wheeler2024, § 5.1]

### Make the discrimination visible

For the inspection, write down which outcomes would favour each hypothesis before checking the parts. Preserve ambiguous cases instead of silently reclassifying them. A favourable result should count to the extent that it is harder to explain under the rival. These are proposed procedures for the invented case, not claims of validated bias elimination.

The same approach helps with a discussion: identify the strongest relevant objection, state the response, and ask whether that response would still count if another person offered it. Reflection directed only toward defence can reinforce a prior view; reflection directed toward discriminating alternatives has a different aim. [@mercier-sperber2011, PDF p. 10, PDF p. 11]

For the motivational explanation, read [motivated reasoning](deeper:14-psychology-of-reasoning/motivated-reasoning). For evidence that changes support, read [Bayes’ theorem](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/bayes-theorem). Neither provides an automatic diagnosis of another person’s motives.
