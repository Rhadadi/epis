> **In short.** Believing a claim and assigning it high confidence are different ways of representing an attitude. A threshold proposal tries to connect them: believe a claim when its probability is high enough. The lottery paradox shows why that simple bridge conflicts with combining beliefs. Identifying full belief with probability one creates a different difficulty about revision. The debate concerns how these attitudes relate, not whether careful thinkers should have any confidence at all.

## Re-learn

### An attitude and its strength

An invented traveler believes that a bridge is open, but assigns a small chance to an unexpected closure. The categorical belief helps the traveler plan; the residual uncertainty helps explain keeping an alternative route available. This example makes intelligible the possibility that full belief does not require literal certainty. Genin and Huber survey competing formal accounts of the relationship. [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.1, 4.2.2]

The **strong Lockean thesis** proposes a fixed threshold below one: fully believe a proposition exactly when its probability reaches that threshold. It gives a clean numerical bridge, but its simplicity has a cost. No matter how high the threshold is set below one, a sufficiently large lottery generates trouble when beliefs must remain consistent and combine under conjunction. [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.2]

### The lottery puts combination under pressure

Consider an invented fair lottery with 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner. Each ticket has probability 0.999 of losing. A threshold of 0.99 licenses belief that ticket 1 loses, ticket 2 loses, and so on. If all those beliefs may be conjoined, the agent believes every ticket loses. Yet the agent also believes one ticket wins. The individual numerical assignments remain coherent; the corresponding set of categorical beliefs is inconsistent. [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.2]

Raising the threshold merely requires a larger lottery. Possible responses restrict the bridge from probability to belief, relax the conjunction requirement, or develop a more contextual connection. Genin and Huber explain why the puzzle does not select one response automatically. It identifies commitments that cannot all be retained in their simple unrestricted forms. [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.2]

### Why not reserve belief for probability one?

That reply avoids the finite lottery's high-but-not-certain tickets, but creates pressure elsewhere. Under ordinary Bayesian conditioning on positive-probability evidence, a proposition assigned probability one retains probability one. If every full belief has that status, the update rule cannot revise those beliefs in the familiar way. Genin and Huber discuss corrigibility as an objection to this identification. [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.1]

The traveler need not claim that bridge closure is impossible. Making ordinary belief irrevocable under the chosen updating rule is a substantial theoretical consequence, rather than an obvious feature of everyday conviction. A model can distinguish categorical commitment from its numerical strength instead of forcing them to be identical.

## Beyond the chapter

### The preface has a different structure

An author can reasonably endorse each carefully checked statement in a book while anticipating at least one remaining error. Combining all the individual endorsements produces tension with that expectation. Sorensen explains the preface paradox and emphasizes that it does not depend on a numerical acceptance threshold. It tests the combination of beliefs even when confidence levels were never explicitly specified. [@sorensen2024, § 4]

Lottery and preface are therefore related without being interchangeable. The lottery displays a conflict between a probability-to-belief rule and deductive cogency. The preface raises aggregation problems through an author's qualitative recognition of fallibility. Simply choosing a higher probability threshold does not settle both. [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.2; @sorensen2024, § 4]

A further distinction concerns action: crossing a bridge can be sensible without assigning it certainty, and declining a risky crossing need not mean believing it is closed. Read [Bayes' theorem](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/bayes-theorem) for the separation of probability from a decision, and [Bayesian epistemology](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/bayesian-epistemology) for norms governing credences.
