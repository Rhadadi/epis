> **In short.** Evidence can favor a conclusion without guaranteeing it. Sampling, applying a population rate, comparing similar cases, and explaining an observation favor conclusions in different ways. A strong inference identifies both its evidential route and the conditions under which that route works. More premises can defeat a previously reasonable conclusion. The categories are useful, but their boundaries are disputed.

## Re-learn

### Four routes beyond the premises

**Enumerative induction** moves from observed members of a group to unobserved members or a wider generalization. In an invented case, inspected seals from a production line withstand a specified pressure, so we expect further seals to withstand it. The support depends on how the inspected seals were selected and whether the relevant production conditions continue. Favorable instances do not deductively entail a universal result. [@douven2025, § 1.1]

**Statistical inference to a case** uses a population rate. Suppose, as an invented assumption, 95% of stored batteries remain usable after a month. A battery selected randomly from that population is likely to remain usable. A battery selected because it is swollen is not automatically covered by the same inference. Membership in a group and a description of the selection procedure jointly determine the relevance of its rate. Douven distinguishes such statistical support from deductive necessity. [@douven2025, § 1.1]

**Analogical reasoning** compares cases. An invented proposal transfers a cleaning method from one lens to another because their coatings are similar. The inference becomes stronger if the similarities concern what the solvent affects, and weaker if they concern only packaging. This example shows why the number of similarities alone is not the issue: relevance to the projected feature matters.

**Abduction**, or explanatory inference, favors an explanation of the observed result. A damp patch below a pipe makes a leak a candidate explanation, but condensation or spilled water may also account for it. The inference needs a comparison with alternatives. Douven identifies the appeal to explanation as a way to distinguish abduction from purely statistical induction, while noting that statistical evidence can also enter an explanation. [@douven2025, § 1.1]

### A historical comparison

Peirce's bean example separates deduction, induction, and hypothesis by rearranging rule, case, and result. Knowing that a bag's beans are white and that these beans came from it supports a deduction about their color. Knowing their origin and observing their color supports an induction about the bag. Knowing their color and the bag's contents supports a hypothesis about their origin. Peirce calls the last a fair guess, rather than a conclusion compelled by the premises. [@peirce1878, § I]

The comparison is a useful diagram of direction, not a proof that every real argument belongs to one simple form. An explanation can itself contain sampling assumptions, and a statistical argument can be strengthened by causal information. The IEP discussion cautions that ordinary arguments do not always fit a sharp deductive–inductive division. [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 1]

## Beyond the chapter

### Defeat is compatible with earlier support

New evidence can remove a conclusion's support without showing that the earlier inference was foolish. In the invented pipe case, learning that a bucket spilled above the damp patch weakens the leak explanation. Douven describes this as nonmonotonicity: adding premises can defeat a conclusion that was warranted by a smaller set. Deductive validity has a different property; adding premises cannot remove an existing entailment. [@douven2025, § 1.1]

The practical question is therefore which added information would change the inference. A better sampling description can change a generalization; information about selection can change a population-to-case inference; a relevant difference can weaken an analogy; a competing explanation can weaken abduction. These are different vulnerabilities, even when the conclusion is the same.
