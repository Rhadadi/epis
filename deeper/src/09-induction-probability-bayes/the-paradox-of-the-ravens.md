> **In short.** All ravens are black says the same thing as all non-black objects are non-ravens. A green apple is an instance of the second statement. If favorable instances confirm generalizations and equivalent hypotheses receive the same support, the apple appears to confirm the claim about ravens. That result feels strange, but the strangeness alone does not identify the faulty principle. The observation's significance depends on background information and how the object was selected.

## Re-learn

### The apple enters through equivalence

Let H be the universal claim that every raven is black. Its contrapositive says that every non-black object is a non-raven. The two have the same counterexamples: a non-black raven. A green apple is a non-black object that is not a raven, so it is a favorable instance of the contrapositive. Hempel's confirmation theory extends the resulting support to the equivalent raven claim. Crupi reconstructs this route to the paradox. [@crupi2025, § 1.2]

::: argument The three commitments
1. A favorable instance confirms a universal generalization.
2. Logically equivalent hypotheses receive the same evidential support.
3. A green apple is a favorable instance of all non-black objects are non-ravens, which is equivalent to all ravens are black.
4. Therefore, observing the apple confirms the raven hypothesis.
:::
[@crupi2025, § 1.2]. This is an argument about confirmation principles. Its conclusion does not say that the apple proves H, supplies as much support as a raven under every theory, or reveals a biological mechanism.

### Two observations that look alike can ask different questions

Imagine searching among ravens and checking their colors. A black result rules out the possibility that this selected raven is a counterexample. Now imagine searching among non-black objects and checking whether one is a raven. Finding a non-raven also avoids a possible counterexample, but it may be overwhelmingly expected whether H is true or false. A sampling description helps explain why the evidential contributions can differ. [@crupi2025, § 2.2, 3.6]

Finally, imagine taking a known apple from a fruit bowl and observing that it is green. Your procedure effectively guaranteed a non-raven in advance. The raven hypothesis has little opportunity to distinguish that outcome from an alternative. This invented procedure illustrates why we must say what was already known rather than treat each observed sentence as evidence obtained without a background.

### A Bayesian answer is qualified

Bayesian confirmation asks whether the observation raises the hypothesis's probability relative to the background assignment. For constrained sampling, Crupi explains how a black raven can offer stronger support than a non-raven drawn from the enormous collection of non-black objects. The comparison uses assumptions about how probable the respective outcomes already were. It does not follow from the word raven alone. [@crupi2025, § 3.6]

For unconstrained sampling, Crupi gives conditions under which observing a black raven confirms H while observing a non-black non-raven leaves its probability unchanged. Those conditions concern how raven populations and black non-raven populations relate to H. Different probability structures can give other results. The correct lesson is therefore not that every apple gives a tiny amount of confirmation; that familiar answer needs specified assumptions. [@crupi2025, § 3.6]

## Beyond the chapter

### Equivalence does not erase a procedure

Rephrasing a hypothesis logically should not create a new hypothesis. But confirmation also relates a hypothesis to evidence and background information. Preserving the first relation while omitting the other two is what makes the quick apple argument misleading as a description of actual inquiry. Crupi compares Hempelian, hypothetico-deductive, and Bayesian treatments, which disagree about what counts as support in these cases. [@crupi2025, § 1.2, 2.2, 3.6]

The paradox differs from [Goodman's riddle](deeper:09-induction-probability-bayes/goodmans-new-riddle-of-induction). Ravens concerns the relation between equivalent hypotheses and apparently irrelevant instances; grue concerns rival projections matching the same observed cases. Both show why a bare count of favorable instances is an incomplete account of evidence.
