> **In short.** An observation supports a prediction only after we decide which features belong together. Goodman makes that choice visible by inventing a category that fits every observation so far but gives a different prediction next. His puzzle concerns which patterns we may project, even if we put Hume's demand for a justification of induction aside. Calling a category strange does not explain why it is unsuitable. Goodman's own answer appeals to the history of using predicates in successful projection, but that answer raises further questions about inherited habits and new discoveries.

## Re-learn

### Two descriptions of the same notebook

Imagine that every emerald you have examined is green. Set a future cutoff date, T. Define an object as **grue** if it was examined before T and is green, or was not examined before T and is blue. Until T, every examined green emerald is also a grue emerald. Your notebook therefore contains instances of both generalizations: all emeralds are green; all emeralds are grue. The observations alone do not distinguish them. This is Goodman's constructed case, as explained by Cohnitz and Rossberg. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

Now consider an emerald first examined after T. The green hypothesis predicts green. The grue hypothesis predicts blue, because that object falls into the second part of the definition. The hypotheses disagree about the unexamined case while agreeing about the examined cases. Nothing in the definition says that a previously examined green emerald must suddenly change color on the cutoff date. The division concerns examination history, not a magical physical transformation. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

### Counting matches does not settle the issue

Suppose a simple rule says that an unchallenged generalization becomes better supported whenever another instance matches it. Applied without qualification, that rule treats the two descriptions alike. Yet we want to project green and decline to project grue. The missing qualification concerns **projectibility**: which predicates, and which hypotheses using them, can legitimately figure in this kind of inference. Goodman is challenging an unrestricted instance-counting account, not reporting a disagreement among gemologists about actual emeralds. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

An invented example makes the structure clearer. Every bus you have observed on weekdays has arrived before nine. Define an on-time-or-weekend-late category: before the coming Saturday, arriving before nine counts; on previously unobserved Saturdays, arriving after nine counts. This category can fit your weekday notebook while licensing an incompatible Saturday expectation. Bus schedules supply reasons to distinguish weekdays and weekends; merely counting matches to a phrase does not supply those reasons.

### Why an odd word is not an answer

We can define grue using green, blue, and examination time. That makes grue look complicated in our vocabulary. But we can reverse the presentation: take grue and its partner bleen as basic, then define green and blue using them and the examination condition. Goodman uses this symmetry to challenge the idea that a predicate's written definition alone determines whether it is fit for projection. Simplicity relative to a language needs a defense of that language. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

## The full story

### A problem about differences between inductions

Hume's question asks how an inference from observed cases to unobserved cases can be justified. Goodman's question asks what distinguishes acceptable from unacceptable projections with the same pattern of favorable observations. These problems interact, but solving one would not automatically solve the other. Even a permission to use induction would leave us needing to identify which generalizations that permission covers. [@henderson2024, § 1, 4.2; @cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

An earlier passage in Mill makes the selective character of induction vivid. Mill contrasts a chemical generalization drawn from an experiment with a generalization about the color of crows. He asks why apparently similar moves from cases to general conclusions carry such different confidence. This is an antecedent question about evidential discrimination, not Mill's statement of the later grue puzzle. [@mill1843, Book III, ch. III, § 3]

::: original Mill's question about a single instance
> Why is a single instance, in some cases, sufficient for a complete induction, while in others, myriads of concurring instances, without a single exception known or presumed, go such a very little way toward establishing a universal proposition?
:::
[@mill1843, Book III, ch. III, § 3]. The contrast matters more than the force of Mill's word sufficient. A theory of induction must explain differences in evidential strength, rather than count all observations as interchangeable votes. The quotation does not establish that any actual chemical experiment licenses certainty.

Goodman's presentation sharpens this concern by holding the evidence fixed and changing its classification. A green emerald is not a second piece of evidence when redescribed as grue. It is the same observation placed under another predicate. If our rule cannot discriminate between those uses, adding more pre-cutoff emeralds repeats the problem rather than resolves it. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

### Lawlike and accidental generalizations

Cohnitz and Rossberg introduce the problem through a contrast between copper conducting electricity and men in a particular room being third sons. Both sentences have the form all members of this class have that property. Yet the former is presented as a lawlike generalization, while the latter is at most an accidental grouping. Shared logical form does not confer shared inductive standing. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

The contrast also concerns counterfactuals. A lawlike generalization can support a claim about what would happen if an additional object belonged to the relevant kind. An accidentally true statement about the occupants of a room need not support a claim about someone who might have entered it. A stranger's joining the room would not make that person a third son. The features that selected the group matter to the inference. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

This explanation creates a task rather than finishing one. Calling a hypothesis lawlike helps only if we can say what earns that status without assuming the projections we wanted to justify. Goodman asks how to distinguish the lawlike regularities from rivals that fit the same observed cases. The puzzle therefore bears on explanation and prediction as well as on the narrow question of accepting a universal sentence. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

An invented contrast illustrates the dependence on background information. Every screw in a sealed box might be brass because a manufacturer sorted the box by material. Every screw currently lying on a desk might also be brass because someone happened to empty that box there. Predicting the material of another manufactured screw requires information about production; predicting the material of whatever someone next places on the desk requires information about that person's activity. A bare list of matching cases hides these different routes by which cases entered the list.

### The new riddle, premise by premise

Let E be the conjunction of our reports of emeralds examined before T and found green. Let G say all emeralds are green, and R say all emeralds are grue. Restrict attention to the usual simple rule that favorable instances confirm a generalization. Goodman uses the construction to show why this rule needs restrictions on the hypotheses to which it applies. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

::: argument Why favorable instances are not enough
1. Each emerald reported in E is green and, by definition, grue.
2. An unrestricted instance-confirmation rule counts E in favor of both G and R.
3. G and R yield incompatible color predictions for an emerald first examined after T.
4. Counting those instances alone provides no preference between the incompatible projections.
5. Therefore, an adequate account must say more about which hypotheses or predicates are projectible.
:::
[@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]. Premise 2 identifies the rule under pressure. The conclusion is not that every possible account of confirmation must give the two hypotheses equal support. An account incorporating background knowledge or unequal priors may discriminate between them, but then it must explain that additional discrimination.

Premise 1 is easy to overlook. It does not describe two rival measurements, one of green and one of grue. The category grue was defined to fit the same measurements before the cutoff. Taking another measurement under the same condition yields another match to both predicates. A richer experiment could supply other evidence, but the counting rule cannot claim that richer evidence while pretending to use only E.

Premise 3 also requires care. The conflicting predictions concern an emerald not previously examined. An already examined green stone can retain its color and still satisfy the first clause of grue after T. Defining the predicate as everything is green until T and everything turns blue afterward changes the thought experiment and makes it easier to dismiss. The examination condition is part of the construction. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

Finally, the conclusion concerns the inferential rule. We can continue to prefer the green prediction while admitting that a proposed explanation of that preference has failed. The puzzle does not oblige us to treat every bizarre hypothesis as practically plausible. It asks us to articulate what our reasonable preference depends on, instead of locating the whole explanation in the shared observation record. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

### Three tempting replies and what they owe

One reply says that grue explicitly mentions a date, whereas green does not. Goodman responds with the alternative vocabulary. Define bleen as blue among objects examined before T and green among objects not so examined. In the grue-and-bleen vocabulary, green itself needs an examination-time definition: grue before T, bleen otherwise. A ban on predicates defined with individual times is therefore sensitive to what a language treats as basic. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

A second reply says that green is simpler. This may identify a useful preference, but the linguistic symmetry shows why simplicity cannot be measured by counting English words alone. If the preferred measure depends on our vocabulary, the defense must say why that vocabulary captures the relevant structure. Merely making grue a one-syllable primitive would not make the grue prediction scientifically responsible. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

A third reply says that the green projection receives a higher prior probability. Bayesian coherence permits unequal priors, but coherence alone does not dictate the familiar inductive preference. Lin explains that coherent priors can produce inductive or counter-inductive changes of credence. Assigning a lower prior to grue can represent our judgment; a further account is needed if the aim is to justify that judgment. [@lin2024, § 1.5]

These replies need not be discarded in every application. A date can mark a genuine causal change, such as a factory's scheduled switch to a new pigment. A simple model can be valuable when its variables track a known process. A prior can summarize relevant background evidence. The mistake would be to apply a superficial test universally and treat its preference as an explanation independent of that background. These examples are illustrations, not claims that Goodman endorsed particular modern modeling methods.

### Goodman's answer: entrenchment

Goodman's proposal appeals to **entrenchment**, the history of using predicates in projections. Green has an established role in past hypotheses and predictions; grue lacks a comparable history. If two competing hypotheses have the same empirical record, the one using better entrenched predicates can override its rival. The relevant history is a history of projection, not merely how often a word appears in conversation. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.4]

This is more qualified than always choose the familiar word. Goodman distinguishes hypotheses that are supported, unviolated, and unexhausted. Supported hypotheses have positive instances; unviolated ones have no counterinstance; unexhausted ones still reach beyond the checked cases. Competition and overriding then matter to their projectibility. Entrenchment can also be inherited through relations to other predicates, so the account is not limited to an isolated word's direct use. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.4]

An exhausted hypothesis would leave nothing further to project. If the sentence describes exactly five numbered objects and we have inspected all five, its truth is settled by those inspections. A universal claim about emeralds extends beyond the observed stones and therefore raises the projectibility issue. Keeping this distinction prevents us from treating confirmation of a fully inspected finite list as a model of every open-ended inference. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.4]

Goodman's answer explains a preference through the development of inferential practice. It does not deliver an external guarantee that the selected predicates carve nature correctly forever. Cohnitz and Rossberg explicitly compare this feature with Hume's appeal to habit: the account describes how predicates are selected without furnishing the ultimate justification demanded by the older problem. Readers must decide whether that is the right kind of answer to seek. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.4]

### Rules and judgments can correct each other

Goodman also addresses justification by comparing induction with deduction. We test an inference against accepted rules, but we can revise a rule when a sufficiently compelling example conflicts with it. Neither an unexamined stock of intuitive judgments nor an unrevisable rulebook does all the work. The process aims to bring the rules and judgments into an adjusted equilibrium. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.2]

Cohnitz and Rossberg note that Rawls later supplied the term **reflective equilibrium** for Goodman's technique. The method lets judgments correct rules and rules correct judgments, while retaining concerns such as consistency and practical usability. For Goodman, this is the available form of justification for inferential practice. It is not an additional prediction that the future must resemble the past. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.2]

The method faces a familiar concern: an established practice might preserve its own prejudices. The possibility of revising both judgments and rules gives it resources for criticism, but the bare instruction to seek equilibrium does not tell us which initial judgments deserve the greatest weight. That is a question to put to this method, rather than a proof that revision is impossible. Lin describes analogous debates about the reach and justification of Bayesian norms. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.2; @lin2024, § 1.7]

[@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.2, 5.3, 5.4; @lin2024, § 1.5]. These are different ways of accounting for evidential discrimination. The table does not imply that there is one universally accepted solution or that each approach addresses every version of the problem.

## Beyond the chapter

### Categories are part of a prediction

When an invented forecasting system groups customers by a category, its predictions depend on both the collected outcomes and the definition of the group. A grouping chosen solely because it reproduces yesterday's data may hide a future boundary. Goodman's case supplies a conceptual reason to examine that choice. It does not by itself provide an empirical diagnosis of any particular algorithm or a formula for fixing one. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.3]

The lesson is also narrower than evidence never determines anything. Evidence can rule out hypotheses, and background information can favor particular variables. The riddle shows that favorable cases, taken through an unrestricted syntactic rule, do not explain all such preferences. A responsible explanation names the sampling procedure, the background constraints, and the reason the classification is expected to remain informative.

### Familiarity and innovation

Entrenchment helps explain why ordinary predicates have a standing that newly contrived rivals lack. But inquiry also introduces new concepts. The hard question is how a new predicate gains projective authority through its relations to established ones and through evidence. Goodman's provision for inherited entrenchment makes this more subtle than a permanent veto on unfamiliar language. [@cohnitz-rossberg2024, § 5.4]
