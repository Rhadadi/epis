> **In short.** The textbook rules for definitions (not too broad, not too narrow, not circular, and so on) go back to Aristotle's requirement that a definition apply to exactly the things defined and capture what they essentially are. Modern logic adds two sharper requirements for stipulated definitions: they must not let you prove anything new, and they must settle how the new term is used. And one rule, "never circular", turns out to be less absolute than it looks.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Not too broad, not too narrow

In Aristotle's terms, a definition and what it defines must "counterpredicate": the definition applies to everything the term applies to, "and conversely" [@smith2022, § Essential Predication and the Predicables]. A definition that applies to more things is too broad; one that applies to fewer is too narrow.

#### 2. Essential, not just coextensive

Matching the extension is not enough. "“Capable of laughing”, for example, counterpredicates with “human” but fails to be its definition", because it is not essential; Aristotle called such a predicate a *proprium* [@smith2022, § Essential Predication and the Predicables]. That is the chapter's rule 6, and the point of the featherless-biped story.

#### 3. A stipulation must not create knowledge

Logicians state two criteria for stipulative definitions. Conservativeness: "a stipulative definition should not enable us to establish essentially new claims"; "We should not be able to establish, by means of a mere stipulation, new things about, for example, the moon" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 2.1]. Use: "the definition should fix the use of the defined expression" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 2.1].

#### 4. Eliminability

The traditional account adds that a definition must let you replace the defined term everywhere: it must "reduce each formula containing the defined term to a formula in the ground language" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 2.2]. A definition, on this view, is an abbreviation.

### Common confusions

- **One standard fits all definitions.** "The standards by which definitions are judged are thus liable to vary from case to case" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1]. That is the chapter's rule 7, and it is why arguing about the "real" meaning of a word often dissolves once the purposes are separated.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Russell's ban on hidden circles.** Russell argued that definitions such as "z is a liar if all propositions asserted by z are false" involve "a subtle kind of vicious circle", because they refer to a totality that includes what they define. His Vicious-Circle Principle: "Whatever involves all of a collection must not be one of the collection" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 2.7].
- **Ostensive definitions break the rules.** A definition by pointing ("let this dog be called ‘Freddie’") can introduce terms that cannot be eliminated, so it can fail both the Eliminability and the Conservativeness criteria [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.6]. That is not a defect: it is how language gets new content.

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Not circular."** Some circular definitions are perfectly usable. A circular definition can settle most cases and leave a few unsettled, and the unsettled cases behave exactly as "truth" does in the liar paradox. "Since truth is manifestly a legitimate concept, so also are concepts defined by circular definitions" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 2.8]. On the revision theory, such a definition supplies "a rule of revision", not a rule of application [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 2.8]. The rule against circularity is sound advice for everyday definitions, not a law of logic.
