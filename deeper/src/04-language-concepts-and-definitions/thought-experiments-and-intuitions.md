> **In short.** A thought experiment sets up a scenario in imagination, lets it run, and draws a conclusion, from Lucretius's spear thrown at the edge of the universe to Gettier's cases. Thought experiments can be powerful and are also fallible: the spear argument fails once we see that space can be finite without an edge. The live debate is whether the intuitions they produce are good evidence, now that experimental philosophers have shown such intuitions can shift with framing, order and culture.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. How a thought experiment works

Lucretius argued that space is infinite: toss a spear at the supposed boundary of the universe. "If the spear flies through, it isn't a boundary after all; if the spear bounces back, then there must be something beyond the supposed edge of space" [@brown-fehige2026, § 1]. The pattern: we imagine a situation, then "we let it run or we carry out an operation; we see what happens; finally, we draw a conclusion" [@brown-fehige2026, § 1].

#### 2. Thought experiments can mislead

The same example "illustrates the fallibility of thought experiments": we now know space could be "both finite and unbounded", like a circle with no edge [@brown-fehige2026, § 1].

#### 3. Parity in ethics

Thomson's violinist case has "the same structure as the anti-abortion argument" but "does not seem convincing in this case"; so even if the fetus has a right to life, "it may still be morally permissible to abort" [@brown-fehige2026, § 1]. This is the [parity-of-reasoning move](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/dialectical-moves) done with an imagined case.

### Common confusions

- **A vivid case settles the matter.** Counter-thought-experiments can undo one. Dennett retold Jackson's Mary story with a blue banana: Mary, knowing everything physical about colour, says she is being tricked, so "Mary does not learn anything new" [@brown-fehige2026, § 1]. Thought experiments show "an intriguing plasticity" [@brown-fehige2026, § 1]. That is Dennett's advice to "turn the knobs" in action.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Cross-cultural intuitions.** Weinberg, Nichols and Stich reported that East Asian participants often gave the "wrong intuitions" about Gettier-type cases, "though see Machery et al 2017 for evidence that some of these intuitions may in fact be universal" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 5.2]. "At the very least, this work suggests that philosophers should be cautious about moving from their own intuitions to claims about the proper analysis of a concept" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 5.2].
- **Replies on behalf of intuitions.** "Intuition apologists" argue that survey answers are not real philosophical intuitions: they are "too quick and not suitably reflective" (Kauppinen), or "track different concepts than philosophers are interested in" (Sosa) [@sytsma-willemsen2026, § 4.4.2].
