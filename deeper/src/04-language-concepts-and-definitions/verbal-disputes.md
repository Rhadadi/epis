> **In short.** A dispute is merely verbal when the parties agree on the facts and differ only in how they use a word. The diagnosis is old: Bacon complained that the "great and solemn disputes of learned men often terminate in controversies about words and names", and James dissolved an argument about a squirrel by separating two senses of "go round". The cure is to make the distinction. But a dispute about which sense to adopt can itself be substantive, and some apparently verbal disputes are not verbal at all.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. James's squirrel

James's friends argued whether a man circling a tree goes round a squirrel that keeps the trunk between them. Taking meaning to be the "conceivable effects of a practical kind", James found "two “practical” meanings of “go around”": passing north, east, south and west of the squirrel, or facing its head, side, tail and other side in turn. "“Make the distinction,” James writes, “and there is no occasion for any further dispute.”" [@goodman2024, § Pragmatism (1907)].

#### 2. Bacon's diagnosis

"The great and solemn disputes of learned men often terminate in controversies about words and names." Bacon's advice: proceed "imitating the caution of mathematicians" and settle such disputes "by definitions" [@bacon1620, I.59].

#### 3. Two concepts, both useful

Carnap faced a long dispute between those who took probability to be a frequency and those who took it to be a degree of confirmation. By 1945 he insisted that the two approaches "were not competitors but were attempting to explicate two different concepts of probability. One need not choose one as the only concept; both concepts were useful" [@creath2026, § 4.4]. Reichenbach, by contrast, "never conceded that both concepts were needed" [@creath2026, § 4.4]. Recognising a verbal element does not always end the argument; it can move it to whether one concept is enough.

### Common confusions

- **Verbal disputes are trivial.** Bacon thought the cure, definitions, was itself limited, since definitions "consist themselves of words, and these words produce others" [@bacon1620, I.59]. And deciding which concept to use is often a real question about purposes; see [Conceptual engineering](deeper:04-language-concepts-and-definitions/conceptual-engineering).

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **A verbal reply in experimental philosophy.** When surveys find that ordinary people answer thought experiments differently from philosophers, one reply is that the respondents "track different concepts than philosophers are interested in" (Sosa) [@sytsma-willemsen2026, § 4.4.2]. If so, the disagreement between philosophers and the people they survey is partly verbal. See [Thought experiments and intuitions](deeper:04-language-concepts-and-definitions/thought-experiments-and-intuitions).
- **Pragmatism as a method for disputes.** For James, resolving disputes this way was one of the six things pragmatism is: "a method of resolving philosophical disputes" [@goodman2024, § Pragmatism (1907)].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **The method of elimination.** The chapter's test, banning the disputed word, works well when the parties agree on all the facts once the word is gone. Carnap's probability case shows the other outcome: once the word is split in two, a substantive disagreement can remain about whether both concepts are needed [@creath2026, § 4.4].
