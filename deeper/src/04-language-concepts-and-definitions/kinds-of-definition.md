> **In short.** "Definition" covers very different activities: a lexicographer reporting usage, a chemist discovering what gold is, a mathematician laying down a new term, a philosopher trying to say what knowledge is. They answer to different standards. The kind most worth knowing about is the one the chapter leaves out: Carnap's *explication*, which keeps what matters in an old concept and openly replaces the rest. It is the ancestor of today's conceptual engineering.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Different definitions, different goals

We speak of a commission defining a border, a court defining "person", a chemist discovering the definition of gold, a lexicographer defining "cool", and a mathematician laying down the definition of "group". They aim at different things: "the boundary commission may aim to achieve precision; the Supreme Court, fairness; the chemist and the lexicographer, accuracy; the debater, clarity; and the mathematician, fecundity" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1].

#### 2. Real and nominal

Locke distinguished "real essence" from "nominal essence". "The chemist aims at real definition, whereas the lexicographer aims at nominal definition" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.1]. Whether Socrates's question "What is virtue?" seeks a real or a nominal definition depends on what you take that philosophical activity to be [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.1].

#### 3. Stipulative and descriptive

"A stipulative definition imparts a meaning to the defined term, and involves no commitment that the assigned meaning agrees with prior uses (if any) of the term" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.3]. A descriptive definition also spells out meaning but aims "to be adequate to existing usage". When philosophers define "know" or "free", "a lack of fit with existing usage is an objection to them" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.4].

#### 4. Explication

An explication "aims to respect some central uses of a term but is stipulative on others" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.5]. The standard example is the set-theoretic definition of an ordered pair, ⟨x, y⟩ = {{x}, {x, y}}. It has consequences the ordinary notion lacks, "But the mismatch is not an objection to the explication. What is important for explication is not antecedent meaning but function" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.5]. The logician's truth-functional conditional is another explication: it differs from ordinary "if" in essential respects, yet it can serve "for certain purposes in certain contexts" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.5].

### Common confusions

- **A philosophical definition is a better dictionary entry.** "When an epistemologist seeks a definition of “knowledge,” she is not seeking a good dictionary entry for the word ‘know’" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.2]. Dictionaries need only give enough information to let you use a word; philosophical definitions try to explain what the word's meaning is.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Stipulation and the a priori.** If you stipulate that a "raimex" is "a rational, imaginative, experiencing being", then "raimexes are rational" is "assured of being necessary, certain, and a priori". "Philosophers have found it tempting to explain the puzzling cases of, e.g., a priori judgments, by an appeal to stipulative definitions" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.3].
- **Fixing reference without fixing meaning.** Kripke noted that a name like "Jack the Ripper" can be introduced through a description ("the man who murdered X, Y and Z"), where "the description serves only to fix the reference of the new name; the name is not synonymous with the description" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.3]. See [Sense and reference](deeper:04-language-concepts-and-definitions/sense-and-reference).
- **Ostension as the root of language.** Ostensive definitions "can introduce terms that are ineliminable". Russell held that all nominal definitions, "if pushed back far enough, must lead ultimately to terms having only ostensive definitions" [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.6].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **The list of kinds.** The chapter's list of lexical, stipulative, precising, theoretical, operational, persuasive and ostensive definitions omits explication, the kind that philosophers and scientists most often actually give. Quine upheld the definition of "ordered pair" "as a philosophical paradigm" for this reason [@gupta-mackereth2023, § 1.5]. See [Conceptual engineering](deeper:04-language-concepts-and-definitions/conceptual-engineering).
