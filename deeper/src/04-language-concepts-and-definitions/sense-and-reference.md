> **In short.** "Mark Twain is Mark Twain" tells you nothing; "Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens" can be news. Yet both are about one man. Frege's answer was that words have a sense, a way of presenting their object, as well as a reference. Russell answered differently, by treating names as disguised descriptions, and Kripke later argued that names refer directly, through a chain of use back to a naming. The debate matters for epistemology because it decides what we know when we know a name, and why knowledge reports resist substitution.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. The puzzle about identity

"We can learn that ‘Mark Twain = Mark Twain’ is true simply by inspecting it; but we can't learn the truth of ‘Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens’ simply by inspecting it — you have to examine the world to see whether the two persons are the same" [@zalta2026, § 3.1.1]. If the meaning of a name were only the person it names, the two sentences would mean the same. So "how do we account for the difference in cognitive significance between ‘a=b’ and ‘a=a’ when they are true?" [@zalta2026, § 3.1.1].

#### 2. Frege's answer: sense

Names and descriptions also "express a sense". "The sense of an expression accounts for its cognitive significance—it is the way by which one conceives of the denotation of the term." "‘The morning star’ and ‘the evening star’ denote the same planet, namely Venus, but express different ways of conceiving of Venus" [@zalta2026, § 3.2].

#### 3. The puzzle about belief

Normally you can swap co-referring names: from "Mark Twain was an author" and "Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens", infer "Samuel Clemens was an author" [@zalta2026, § 3.1.2]. But not inside belief reports. If John learned "Mark Twain" from reading *Huckleberry Finn* and "Samuel Clemens" while learning about nineteenth-century American authors, "John may not believe that Samuel Clemens wrote Huckleberry Finn" [@zalta2026, § 3.1.2].

#### 4. Frege's answer: shifted reference

After a verb like "believes", Frege said, a term "no longer denotes what it ordinarily denotes. Instead, Frege claims that in such contexts, a term denotes its ordinary sense" [@zalta2026, § 3.2]. Since "Mark Twain" and "Samuel Clemens" have different senses, swapping them inside a belief report can change its truth.

### Common confusions

- **Sense and reference apply only to names.** Frege extended them to whole sentences: a sentence's denotation is a truth value and its sense is a thought [@zalta2026, § 3.2].
- **Only fictional names lack reference.** Frege allowed that "the most powerful Greek god" has a sense but no denotation, just like "Pegasus" [@zalta2026, § 3.2].

## The full story

### How the idea developed

::: timeline From sense to causal chains
- **1892** · Frege's "On Sense and Reference" poses the two puzzles and introduces sense [@zalta2026, § 3.1].
- **1905** · Russell's theory of descriptions treats "the F is G" as a quantified claim [@ludlow2023, § 2; @ludlow2023, § 3.3].
- **1910–11** · Russell's "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description" links the theory to what we can know [@ludlow2023, § 3.3].
- **1961** · Ruth Barcan Marcus proposes that names are "tags" with no meaning beyond their reference [@michaelson2024, § 2.2].
- **1969–72** · Geach, Donnellan and Kripke develop the causal theory of reference [@michaelson2024, § 2.2].
:::

### Frege's system

Frege's picture extends from names to whole sentences. In "John loves Mary", "the words ‘John’ and ‘Mary’ in this sentence are names, the expression ‘loves’ signifies a function, and, moreover, the sentence as a whole is a complex name" [@zalta2026, § 3.2]. A sentence denotes a truth value: "the sentence ‘John loves Mary’ names a truth value" [@zalta2026, § 3.2]. Its sense is a *thought*, and "whereas there are only two truth values, he supposes that there are an infinite number of thoughts" [@zalta2026, § 3.2]. This is why two true sentences can share a denotation, The True, and still say very different things.

::: argument The substitution argument, and where it fails
1. John believes that Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*.
2. Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens.
3. So John believes that Samuel Clemens wrote *Huckleberry Finn*.
:::

[@zalta2026, § 3.1.2]. The same form is valid outside belief reports (from 4 > 3 and 4 = 8/2, infer 8/2 > 3), but "This argument is not valid. There are circumstances in which the premises are true and the conclusion false" [@zalta2026, § 3.1.2]. Frege's diagnosis is that premise 1 is not about Mark Twain at all, but about a sense. That is the logic behind the chapter's [masked man fallacy](04-language-concepts-and-definitions.md#the-masked-man-fallacy): "knows who" and "believes that" track ways of presenting a thing, not just the thing.

### Russell: descriptions without senses

Russell solved some of the same problems without senses. On his theory, "The F is G" is "shorthand for the conjunction of three claims: (3a) There is an F. (3b) At most one thing is F. (3c) Something that is F is G" [@ludlow2023, § 2]. Because descriptions are quantifiers, they have scope. "The present king of France does not exist" has a true reading ("it is not the case that the present king of France exists") and a false one; so we need not believe in non-existent kings to make sense of it [@ludlow2023, § 3.1]. And "George wondered whether the Morning Star is the Evening Star" has readings on which George wonders about one object, or about "the law of identity" [@ludlow2023, § 3.2].

Russell's epistemology pushed the theory further. He distinguished objects we are "directly acquainted with" from those we know only under a description, and, wary of error about acquaintance, shrank acquaintance down to oneself and sense data. "This in turn led Russell to extend the theory of descriptions to almost all uses of names—treating them as definite descriptions in disguise" [@ludlow2023, § 3.3].

::: original Russell, "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description" (1910–11)
> Common words, even proper names, are usually really descriptions. That is to say, the thought in the mind of a person using a proper name correctly can generally only be expressed explicitly if we replace the proper name by a description. Moreover, the description required to express the thought will vary for different people, or for the same person at different times. The only thing constant (so long as the name is rightly used) is the object to which the name applies.

Russell's example is Bismarck. Bismarck himself might use his name for the person he is acquainted with; a friend is acquainted only with sense data connected with Bismarck's body; we who never met him have "some more or less vague mass of historical knowledge". What lets us communicate despite our different descriptions is that "we know there is a true proposition concerning the actual Bismarck", and the proposition stays the same however we vary the description.
:::

[@russell1918, ch. X]

### Kripke's objections to descriptivism

Descriptivism explains a lot. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative because two different descriptions turn out to fit one object; "Santa Claus lives at the North Pole" is meaningful though Santa does not exist; and Fred can believe that Cicero, but not Tully, was Roman [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]. Kripke raised two objections.

**The modal objection.** Names keep their reference when we talk about what might have been; descriptions do not. Suppose the description I attach to "David Cameron" is "the U.K. Prime Minister who called for a referendum on Brexit". I can truly say "David Cameron might not have called for a referendum on Brexit". But it is false that "the U.K. Prime Minister who called for a referendum on Brexit" might not have called for one, when we mean metaphysical rather than epistemic possibility [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]. So the name does not mean the description.

**The semantic objection.** "Often, we don't associate enough information with a name to pick out any particular individual. Nonetheless, we seem to be capable of using that name to refer to a specific individual." Ordinary people may know only that Feynman was a physicist, yet someone who has merely overheard the name can ask "Who is Feynman?" and be asking about Feynman [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]. A related case is Gödel: if Schmitt had really proved incompleteness and Gödel stole the proof, descriptivism implies that "we would be referring to Schmitt when we say “Gödel”", which seems wrong [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.4].

### The causal theory

The main alternative descends from Mill: "a name's meaning is simply its referent". Marcus proposed that names are "tags" with "no linguistic meaning beyond their reference"; one consequence is "the necessity of identity statements between co-referring proper names" [@michaelson2024, § 2.2]. The causal theory explains how a tag gets attached. "Reference, on this sort of view, is fixed by a dubbing", and after that "the name gets passed on from speaker to speaker through communicative exchanges"; later speakers "‘borrow’ their reference from speakers earlier in the chain, though borrowers needn't be able to identify any of the lenders they are in fact relying on" [@michaelson2024, § 2.2].

::: positions What fixes the reference of a name?
| View | Who | Claim | Main objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sense | Frege | A name has a sense, a way of conceiving its bearer, which fixes its reference | Kripke's modal and semantic objections, if senses are descriptions |
| Disguised description | Russell | Ordinary names abbreviate descriptions that vary between speakers | Kripke's modal and semantic objections |
| Cluster or expert description | Strawson | Reference can rest on descriptions held by experts in the community | Speakers lose special access to what they mean |
| Direct reference, causal chain | Marcus, Kripke, Donnellan | A name is a tag; a chain of use back to a dubbing fixes its reference | Names can be reused, so a chain alone may not settle reference |
:::

[@zalta2026, § 3.2; @michaelson2024, § 2.1; @michaelson2024, § 2.2]. Classical descriptivists, "like Frege and Russell", allowed that the descriptive content "might vary, sometimes quite markedly, from one speaker to the next" [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]. Strawson's community version handles Feynman, "but at an intuitive cost": the descriptivist risks "making it the case that speakers will have no special access to who or what they are talking about" [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]. And the causal theory has its own complication: "we can apparently re-use names" [@michaelson2024, § 2.2].

### Why this matters for knowledge

The debate decides what a name contributes to what we know. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is true, "but it is not knowable apriori". Yet if a name means only its bearer, the sentence "would mean Venus is Venus", and "simply understanding the meaning of (3) would be enough to know that it is true" [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]. Frege and the descriptivists explain the informativeness; direct-reference theorists must explain it some other way. Russell's own path ran the other direction: an epistemological doctrine about acquaintance drove his semantics. Ludlow suggests he "could have saved himself from some of the more troubling consequences of his view if he had jettisoned the Cartesianism and opted for a more liberal notion of acquaintance", and Hawthorne and Manley argue that "reference comes much cheaper than Russell imagined" [@ludlow2023, § 3.3].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Whose intuitions?** The Gödel case relies on an intuition that may not be universal; Machery and colleagues (2004) tested whether it is [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.4]. See [Thought experiments and intuitions](deeper:04-language-concepts-and-definitions/thought-experiments-and-intuitions).
- **Concepts as tags.** Conceptual atomism extends the Kripkean "tag" picture from names to concepts generally: a concept's content is fixed "by its relation to the world", not by other concepts [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.4].
- **Locke's version.** Locke held that words signify ideas; Kretzmann argues that for Locke "ideas provide the meaning but not the reference of words", a distinction close to Frege's [@uzgalis2024, § 2.3].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Translation.** "Reference" translates Frege's *Bedeutung*, from the title "Über Sinn und Bedeutung" (1892); the Stanford entry renders it "denotation" [@zalta2026, § 3.1; @zalta2026, § 3.2].
- **Frege's verdict on substitution.** Shifted reference does not abandon the principle that co-referring terms can be swapped. On Frege's analysis, the failure of substitution in belief reports "does not constitute evidence against the Principle", since the names there denote different senses [@zalta2026, § 3.2].
- **"Knowable a priori."** Whether "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is a posteriori depends on the theory: on descriptivism it says "that two distinct descriptions are satisfied by the same object", which "should only be knowable aposteriori" [@michaelson2024, § 2.1]; on a pure tag view it says Venus is Venus.
