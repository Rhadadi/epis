> **In short.** Thin concepts like *good* and *wrong* evaluate without saying much about what a thing is like. Thick concepts like *cruel*, *generous* and *gullible* do both at once. That makes them powerful in argument: calling a policy "reckless" sounds like a report but carries a verdict. Philosophers still disagree about how the two parts combine, and even about whether the evaluation belongs to the word's meaning or only to the way it is used.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Description plus evaluation

"Even if calling something selfish evaluates it as bad in some way or respect, not just any bad act can count as selfish; it must also involve the agent giving a certain degree of priority to herself over others." Thick concepts thus "involve both evaluation and non-evaluative description" [@vayrynen2026, preamble].

#### 2. Where the label comes from

The term "thick concept" "originates in Bernard Williams's Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy (Williams 1985)", borrowing from Ryle's and Geertz's "thick description" [@vayrynen2026, § 1].

#### 3. Not only in ethics

The class includes virtue and vice concepts, "practical concepts such as shrewd and imprudent, epistemic concepts such as open-minded and gullible, and aesthetic concepts such as banal and gracious" [@vayrynen2026, preamble].

### Common confusions

- **The descriptive part can always be stated on its own.** It is widely assumed that thick terms have some necessary descriptive conditions (an act is courageous "only if it involves some risk to the agent"), but "It doesn't seem possible to state what thick terms and concepts semantically express" in context-free descriptive terms. Many thick terms are context-sensitive: how offensive a remark must be to count as "rude" varies [@vayrynen2026, § 2.2].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Meaning or use?** Some philosophers think evaluation is only pragmatically attached to words like *courageous*, so "there are no thick concepts, but only loaded words" (Blackburn, following Stevenson 1938) [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1]. Others keep the label "thick" for terms that characteristically combine evaluation and description "in some way or other" [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1].
- **Objectionable thick terms.** People who reject the values behind *chaste* and *lewd*, or *blasphemous*, can accept the description while refusing the verdict; they may use the word "in some non-evaluative sense", marked by "scare quotes or a certain tone of voice" [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1]. That is the practical defence the chapter recommends: separate what happened from whether it was bad.
- **Knowledge as thick.** Most epistemologists treat knowledge as a thin concept, "But Brent Kyle argues that knowledge is better understood as a thick concept and uses its thickness to cast light on why the so-called Gettier problem for definitions of knowledge arises" [@vayrynen2026, § 6].
