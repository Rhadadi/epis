> **In short.** Hume noticed moralists sliding from "is" to "ought" without explanation. On the standard reading this became "Hume's law": no evaluative conclusion follows validly from purely factual premises. That gives a practical rule for any policy argument: find the value premise. But what Hume himself meant is disputed, and thick concepts such as *rude* have been used to argue that the gap is not as clean as the law suggests.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. The passage

Hume closes his argument against moral rationalism by observing that moralists "at some point make an unremarked transition from premises whose parts are linked only by “is” to conclusions whose parts are linked by “ought”". "Few passages in Hume's work have generated more interpretive controversy" [@cohon2018, § 5].

#### 2. Hume's law

On the dominant twentieth-century reading, "no ethical or indeed evaluative conclusion whatsoever may be validly inferred from any set of purely factual premises". Hare and others call this "Hume's Law" [@cohon2018, § 5]. Put generally: "Any (non-trivial) valid argument for an evaluative conclusion must somehow (perhaps implicitly) involve evaluative content in its premises" [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1].

#### 3. Why the gap is thought to exist

"Evaluative and normative statements commend things and guide action, whereas purely non-evaluative statements play no such practical role by themselves" [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1].

### Common confusions

- **Hume's law says values are not facts.** Not necessarily. "An important gap can be held to exist even if values are a special kind of facts" [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1]. The gap concerns inference between kinds of statement; whether there are evaluative facts is a further question in moral metaphysics [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Other readings of Hume.** Some readers take the paragraph to express noncognitivism; others think it only supports his claim that moral properties are not discernible by demonstrative reason, or that discovering virtue and vice "must involve the use of sentiment". On that reading, once we have moral concepts we may reach "particular moral conclusions by inference from causal, factual premises", and "Hume himself makes such inferences frequently" [@cohon2018, § 5].
- **Foot's challenge.** Philippa Foot argued that "x causes offense by indicating lack of respect" entails "x is rude", so a thick evaluative claim follows from a factual one [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1]. The reply, modelled on Hare, uses objectionable terms: one can accept that something "is an overt sexual display" without accepting that it is "lewd" in the condemning sense [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1]. Whether this shows Foot's inferences invalid "is unclear" [@vayrynen2026, § 2.1].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Hume's law."** The chapter states it as Hume's thesis. Snare observes that on this reading "Hume must simply assume that no purely factual propositions are themselves evaluative, as he does not argue for this" [@cohon2018, § 5].
