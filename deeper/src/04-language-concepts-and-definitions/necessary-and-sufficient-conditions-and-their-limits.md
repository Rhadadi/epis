> **In short.** The classical theory says a concept is a definition in disguise: BACHELOR is UNMARRIED plus MAN, and anything with both falls under it. That picture explains a great deal, and it underwrites philosophy's oldest method, testing definitions against counterexamples. But after 2,400 years there are very few successful definitions of interesting concepts, and psychologists have found effects the classical theory cannot explain. Many philosophers now suspect that most concepts simply have no definitions.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. The classical theory

"According to the classical theory, a lexical concept C has definitional structure in that it is composed of simpler concepts that express necessary and sufficient conditions for falling under C." The stock example is BACHELOR, made of UNMARRIED and MAN [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1].

#### 2. Why it was so attractive

One set of components does three jobs. Learning a concept is assembling its parts; categorizing something is checking that every part applies; and the parts fix what the concept refers to [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1]. The theory also supports conceptual analysis, in which definitions "are to be tested against potential counterexamples that are identified via thought experiments" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1].

#### 3. Two kinds of trouble

From psychology: people judge some members of a category more typical than others, and typicality predicts many other things. "Apples are judged to be more typical than plums with respect to the category of fruit", and people decide faster that apples are fruit. The classical theory is not inconsistent with these results; "it does nothing to explain them" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1]. From philosophy: "there are too few examples of successful definitional analyses, and certainly none that are uncontroversial" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1].

### Common confusions

- **The failure is just that definitions are hard.** Perhaps. "But another possibility—one that many philosophers are now taking seriously—is that our concepts lack definitional structure" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1]. The analysis of knowledge is the standard case: after Gettier, philosophers agree that the traditional definition fails, "But no one can seem to agree on what the correct definition is" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.1].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Concepts without structure.** Conceptual atomism holds that lexical concepts "have no semantic structure": a concept's content is fixed by its relation to the world, not to other concepts. It extends Kripke's argument that names are not hidden descriptions [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.4].
- **What failure means for philosophy.** If concepts lack definitions, conceptual analysis is in trouble, and some naturalists treat this as an argument for doing philosophy "continuous with science" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 5.2]. Others, like Chalmers and Jackson, deny that analysis requires definitions [@margolis-laurence2023, § 5.2].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **The featherless biped.** The chapter tells Diogenes's chicken story as a counterexample. But the right lesson depends on what a definition is for. Mill, testing definitions only by whether they apply to exactly the right things, counted "Man is a featherless biped" among "correct definitions of Man", along with "Man is an animal who cooks his food" [@mill1843, ch. VIII]. Aristotle demanded more: a definition must state what a thing essentially is, so a feature that merely picks out the right things is a *proprium*, not a definition [@smith2022, § Essential Predication and the Predicables].
