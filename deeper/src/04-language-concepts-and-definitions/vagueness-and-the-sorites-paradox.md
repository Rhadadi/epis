> **In short.** One grain of sand is not a heap, and adding one grain never seems to make the difference; yet a million grains are a heap. The sorites paradox has been argued over since the fourth century BCE, and every solution costs something. Epistemicists keep classical logic and say there is a sharp boundary we cannot know. Supervaluationists keep the laws of logic but give up the idea that every sentence is true or false. Degree theorists make truth come in degrees, and contextualists say the boundary moves whenever we look for it. For epistemology, vagueness raises a direct question: what can we know about a borderline case?

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Borderline cases

"There is wide agreement that a term is vague to the extent that it has borderline cases" [@sorensen2023, preamble]. Some borderline cases can be settled by a better test. A boy may look borderline obese, and his mother can calculate his body mass index. But some cannot be settled at all: "The boy will only count as an absolute borderline case of ‘obese’ if no possible method of inquiry could settle whether he is obese" [@sorensen2023, § 1]. "No amount of conceptual analysis or empirical investigation can settle whether a 1.8 meter man is tall" [@sorensen2023, § 1].

#### 2. The paradox

In its simplest form the puzzle is a chain of modus ponens steps:

1. 1 grain of wheat does not make a heap.
2. If 1 grain doesn't make a heap, then 2 grains don't.
3. If 2 grains don't make a heap, then 3 grains don't, and so on up to 999,999.
4. So 1 million grains don't make a heap.

"The argument is a paradox because apparently impeccable reasoning from apparently impeccable premises yields a falsehood" [@raffman-hyde2026, preamble]. It also runs backwards: start from a million grains and remove them one by one, and "even a single grain makes a heap" [@raffman-hyde2026, preamble].

#### 3. What makes a sorites

Three conditions must hold. There must be a series ordered on the dimension that decides the predicate (heights for "tall", ages for "old"); neighbours in the series must differ only slightly; and the predicate must be true of the first item and false of the last [@raffman-hyde2026, § 2]. The idea that a vague term applies "across incremental differences" is called its "tolerance" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 2].

#### 4. Four ways out

"As with any paradox, four broad types of response appear to be available." One can deny that logic applies to vague expressions; reject a premise; deny that the argument is valid; or "embrace the paradox and conclude that vague terms are either incoherent or vacuous" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3]. Most theories take the second route and reject the major premise.

### Common confusions

- **Vagueness is ambiguity.** An ambiguous word has several meanings, and the speaker can choose between them "without departing from literal usage". Disambiguating a vague word does not remove its borderline cases: "tall for an eighteenth-century French man" is still vague [@sorensen2023, § 2].
- **Rejecting the major premise is easy.** Rejecting the step from *n* grains to *n* + 1 seems to commit you to its negation, a sharp cut-off. "The hitch is that death for the induction step is life for its negation" [@sorensen2023, § 3].

## The full story

### How the idea developed

::: timeline The sorites from Eubulides to today
- **4th century BCE** · "The Megarian philosopher Eubulides (4th century BC) is usually credited with the first formulation of the puzzle" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 1].
- **3rd–2nd century BCE** · The Sceptics use it against the Stoics' claims to knowledge [@raffman-hyde2026, § 1]; Carneades builds sorites arguments against the existence of the gods [@allen2024, § 4.2].
- **1902** · Peirce's dictionary entry "vague" first defines absolute borderline cases [@sorensen2023, § 1].
- **1975** · Kit Fine applies supervaluation semantics to vagueness [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.1].
- **1981** · Hans Kamp proposes a contextualist solution [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.4].
- **1994** · Timothy Williamson's *Vagueness* defends the epistemic theory [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2].
:::

For a long time the paradox was not taken seriously as a problem for logic. "Curiously, the paradox attracted little subsequent interest until the late 19th century", and the ideal-language tradition of Frege and Russell treated vagueness as "a defect to be eliminated": "Logic simply does not apply to them" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 1; @raffman-hyde2026, § 3.1]. Quine accepted the cost of dropping vague terms in order to keep the "sweet simplicity" of classical logic [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.1]. Interest revived when ordinary language came back into favour: "If logic was to have teeth, it had to apply to natural language as it stands" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.1].

::: original Peirce defines "vague" (1902)
> A proposition is vague when there are possible states of things concerning which it is intrinsically uncertain whether, had they been contemplated by the speaker, he would have regarded them as excluded or allowed by the proposition. By intrinsically uncertain we mean not uncertain in consequence of any ignorance of the interpreter, but because the speaker’s habits of language were indeterminate.

This is Peirce's entry in the *Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology*, as quoted by Sorensen. It marks the line between relative borderline cases, where "the question is determinate but our means for answering it are incomplete", and absolute ones, where "there is incompleteness in the question itself".
:::

[@sorensen2023, § 1]

### The epistemic theory

"Epistemicists think that vagueness is just a form of ignorance: vague terms have sharp boundaries whose locations are hidden from us" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2]. The paradox "is dispatched immediately": one premise is simply false, and "bivalence is preserved: any application of a vague term either is true or is false, though we cannot always know which" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2].

Why can't we know? For Williamson, "meaning supervenes on use", and the boundaries are fixed by "speakers’ dispositions to use it as they do", which we cannot survey [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2]. And our knowledge here is inexact, governed by margin-for-error principles. Near the boundary, a shade of blue may look the same as a shade that is not blue; "if we classify the former shade as blue, that classification is correct by luck, and so does not constitute knowledge" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2]. So vagueness meets the theory of knowledge here: a classification that is right only by luck is not knowledge (compare [the Gettier problem](05-the-nature-of-knowledge.md#the-gettier-problem)).

The cost is plausibility. "Even its proponents grant that epistemicism is intuitively implausible; and it seems to multiply mysteries." In the end the boundaries are "fixed by an unknowable function of their unknowable patterns of use", and the theory "may have the feel of a “just so” story" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2].

### Supervaluationism

Supervaluationists treat vagueness as a fact about meaning. A sentence is "super-true (-false) just in case it is true (false) on every admissible precisification of the predicate" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.1]. Borderline sentences are neither. Yet every precisification draws some line, so "it is nonetheless true according to supervaluation theory that some conditional is" false, though no particular one is [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.1]. "Whilst it is true that there is some cut-off point, there is no particular point of which it is true that it is the cut-off point" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.1].

The view keeps the laws of classical logic, including excluded middle: "irrespective of the vagueness of ‘heap’ it is logically true of any number of grains of wheat that it either does or does not make a heap" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.1]. The price is strange logic. There are "true disjunctions neither of whose disjuncts is (super) true", and "some existential statements can be true without having any true instance". "In effect, the sorites paradox is resolved only at a cost to other intuitions" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.1].

### Degrees of truth

Many-valued theories make truth-values come between true and false. "The most popular approach is to use an infinite number of truth-values represented by the real numbers between 0 (for full falsehood) and 1 (for full truth)" [@sorensen2023, § 4]. Three-valued versions face an obvious objection: two sharp lines (true/indeterminate and indeterminate/false) are no better than one. "Vague concepts appear to be concepts without boundaries at all. No finite number of divisions seems adequate" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.3]. Sainsbury compared membership of a vague category to "being attracted to a magnetic pole" rather than "fitting into a pigeon hole" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.3].

### Contextualism

Contextualists compare the sorites to a sophistry about the horizon: "If the horizon is more than *n* meters away, then it is more than *n* + 1 meters away." You cannot find where it fails, because the horizon "shifts with your location" [@sorensen2023, § 7]. Vague words behave in a similar way: "the set of things to which the term applies (its “content”) shifts with the context", and "The very process of trying to refute the induction step changes the context so that the instance will not come out false" [@sorensen2023, § 7]. In Raffman's version, a speaker on a forced march eventually switches category, and at that moment the boundary jumps backwards "akin to a Gestalt-shift" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.3.4].

::: positions Where does the sorites go wrong?
| View | Main figures | Diagnosis | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ideal language | Frege, Russell, Quine | Logic does not apply to vague terms | Gives up on ordinary language |
| Epistemicism | Williamson, Sorensen | One premise is false; the sharp boundary is unknowable | Intuitively implausible; "just so" story |
| Supervaluationism | Fine, Keefe | The universal premise is false, though no instance is | Disjunctions and existentials without true instances |
| Degree theories | Goguen, Zadeh, Tye | Truth comes in degrees and declines step by step | Seems to replace one sharp line with many |
| Contextualism | Kamp, Raffman, Graff Fara | The boundary shifts as we move along the series | Non-standard semantics for the general premise |
| Embrace the paradox | Dummett, Unger, Wheeler | Vague terms are incoherent or apply to nothing | Everyone is bald and no one is |
:::

### Embracing the paradox

A few philosophers bite the bullet. Dummett argued that vague observational predicates are incoherent; Unger and Wheeler concluded that "Terms like ‘heap’, ‘bald’, ‘hirsute’, ‘rich’ and ‘poor’ apply to nothing" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.4]. The trouble is that "the paradoxes come in pairs". Accepting all sorites arguments means that "everyone is bald and no one is; everyone is rich and no one is, and so on" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.4].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Higher-order vagueness.** "There is normally unclarity as to where the unclarity begins." Twilight is borderline between day and night, "But we are equally uncertain as to when twilight begins", so "‘borderline case’ has borderline cases" [@sorensen2023, § 1]. This is why adding a third truth-value only "delays the inevitable" [@sorensen2023, § 1].
- **The liar connection.** McGee and Tappenden proposed treating the liar and the sorites together, and Priest argues that the two share "a common underlying structure", which motivates a paraconsistent response to both [@raffman-hyde2026, § 4]. See [Paradoxes as stress tests](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/paradoxes-as-stress-tests).
- **Vagueness in law.** "Judges frequently seem to exercise and control discretion by means of vague language", and in a borderline case "Judges cannot confess ignorance and so are compelled to assert beyond their evidence" [@sorensen2023, § 5].
- **The opposite mistake.** Calling a question indeterminate too soon can also be an error: "A false attribution of indeterminacy will lead to the premature abandonment of inquiry" [@sorensen2023, § 1]. Where a snake's tail begins sounds unanswerable, "But the tail can be located by tracing down from the snake’s rib cage" [@sorensen2023, § 1].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Eubulides.** The chapter calls him "of Miletus"; the Stanford entry calls him "The Megarian philosopher" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 1].
- **Epistemicism is not new.** The Stanford entry lists Chrysippus (3rd century BCE) alongside Williamson among those who hold that sharp boundaries exist but cannot be discovered [@raffman-hyde2026, § 3.2].
- **The continuum fallacy.** Most theorists think the sorites is "a resolvable fallacy", and on that view "soriticality is an illusory feature of words like ‘old’ and ‘rich’; their vagueness is real" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 2]. That is the point of the chapter's [continuum fallacy](04-language-concepts-and-definitions.md#the-continuum-fallacy): a vague boundary does not erase the difference between clear cases.
