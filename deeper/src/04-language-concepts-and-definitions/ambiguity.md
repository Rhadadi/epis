> **In short.** A word or sentence is ambiguous when it has more than one meaning, and an argument that slides between the meanings can look valid when it is not. Ambiguity is easy to confuse with three neighbours: vagueness (blurry boundaries), generality (one meaning covering several kinds of case) and context-sensitivity. Linguists and philosophers have developed tests to tell them apart, and none of the tests is decisive. That matters, because "the word is ambiguous" is a common and sometimes too easy way out of an argument.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Ambiguity breaks arguments

"Babe Ruth owned a bat. Bats have wings. Babe Ruth owned something with wings." The argument "looks valid and the premises seem true, on at least one reading, but the conclusion doesn't follow" [@sennet2023, § 5.1]. Frege took ambiguity to be "the main defect of natural language and a real obstacle to trying to formalize it" [@sennet2023, § 5.1].

#### 2. Ambiguous is not the same as vague

"‘Bat’ is not vague but it is ambiguous. ‘Is bald’ looks to be vague but not ambiguous." "A general hallmark of vagueness is that it involves borderline cases" [@sennet2023, § 2.1]. Ambiguity is having several meanings; vagueness is having a meaning with fuzzy edges.

#### 3. Ambiguous is not the same as general

"While ‘is an uncle’ can be satisfied by both brothers of mothers and brothers of fathers, the phrase is not ambiguous but unspecified with respect to parent" [@sennet2023, preamble]. Aristotle's test captures this: "try to construct a definition that encompasses both meanings and posit an ambiguity only if you fail". "Uncle" has one definition that covers both cases [@sennet2023, § 4.4].

#### 4. The zeugma test

Put the word in two sentences that favour different meanings, then merge them using the word once. "The colours are light" and "The feathers are light" are fine, but "The colours and the feathers are light" is odd, a sign that "light" is ambiguous. "Toronto and numbers and triadic relations exist" is not odd, which tells against the claim that "exist" is ambiguous [@sennet2023, § 4.1].

### Common confusions

- **If speakers do not notice two meanings, there are not two.** The zeugma test is limited: "If a term can be ambiguous but in a way so subtle that competent speakers may miss it, then the zeugma might not be noticeable" [@sennet2023, § 4.1]. And zeugma depends on context: "Judy's dissertation is thought provoking and yellowed with age" sounds odd, while "still thought provoking although yellowed with age" does not [@sennet2023, § 4.6.2].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Kripke's translation test.** Real ambiguity is like accidental homophony, so "we would expect the ambiguity to be disambiguated by separate and unrelated words in some other languages" [@sennet2023, § 4.5]. The test has limits: Croatian has two words for "uncle" (*stric*, a father's brother; *ujak*, a mother's brother), though English "uncle" is general, not ambiguous [@sennet2023, § 4.5].
- **Ambiguity in law.** The law distinguishes patent from latent ambiguity: roughly, a law whose meaning is unclear versus one whose clear meaning "applies equally well to highly disparate things". In effect, "the difference between ambiguity in sense and ambiguity in reference" [@sennet2023, § 5.5].
- **Ambiguity as a resource.** Writers use it deliberately: Shakespeare's dying Mercutio says "Ask for me tomorrow and you shall find me a grave man", and Groucho Marx's "I shot an elephant in my pajamas" is "a classic example" of ambiguity in comic misdirection [@sennet2023, preamble].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Modal scope.** The chapter's list of ambiguity types could include the modal one Sennet uses to open: concluding from "All bachelors are necessarily unmarried" and "Adam was a bachelor" that "Adam was necessarily unmarried" is "logical folly" [@sennet2023, preamble]. The premise is true only on the reading where "necessarily" governs the whole conditional. See [Modal logic basics](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/modal-logic-basics).
