> **In short.** If concepts are not definitions, what are they? Wittgenstein's answer was a network of overlapping similarities, like resemblances in a family. Eleanor Rosch turned the idea into a psychological theory: categories are organised around typical examples. Prototype theory explains why robins seem birdier than penguins, but it struggles with reflective judgments and with combining concepts. So most researchers now think concepts have more than one layer, and some think "concept" names several different things.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Prototype structure

On the prototype theory, "a lexical concept C doesn't have definitional structure but has probabilistic structure in that something falls under C just in case it satisfies a sufficient number of properties encoded by C's constituents" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.2]. Its roots are Wittgenstein's remark about family resemblance and "Eleanor Rosch's groundbreaking experimental treatment of much the same idea" [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.2].

#### 2. Why it explains typicality

Categorization is treated as similarity comparison. "The reason apples are judged to be more typical than plums is that the concept APPLE shares more of its constituents with FRUIT", which is also why apples are recognised as fruit faster [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.2].

#### 3. Where it struggles

Prototype categorization "works best for quick and unreflective judgments". Ask whether a dog surgically altered to look like a raccoon is a dog or a raccoon, and "the answer for most of us, and even for children", is that it is still a dog [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.2]. Combining concepts is also a problem: PET FISH has the emergent property "brightly colored", which comes from neither PET nor FISH, and "CHAIRS THAT WERE PURCHASED ON A WEDNESDAY" has no prototype at all [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.2].

### Common confusions

- **Family resemblance means anything goes.** For the later Wittgenstein the move "from an emphasis on definition and analysis to ‘family resemblance’ and ‘language-games’" was part of a rejection of dogmatism, not of rigour [@biletzki-matar2026, § 3.1]. A concept without a definition can still have clear cases.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **The theory theory.** One popular account adds a "core" to the prototype: concepts relate to each other "in the same way as the terms of a scientific theory". Children judge the altered dog to be a dog because even they have "a rudimentary biological theory" that tells them "being a dog isn't just a matter of looking like a dog"; hidden properties matter more [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.3]. The trouble is holism: if a concept's content is its role in a whole theory, different people may never share a concept [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.3].
- **Many kinds of concept.** Faced with these rival theories, some propose pluralism about conceptual structure, or even eliminativism, "the view that there are no concepts", on the grounds that whatever we call concepts share no natural-kind commonalities [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.5].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Prototypes versus definitions.** The chapter presents prototype theory as the alternative to definitions. Current views often combine them: "a prototype constitutes just part of the structure of a concept", alongside a core that handles considered judgments and combination [@margolis-laurence2023, § 2.2].
