> **In short.** A formal fallacy is an argument whose form is invalid: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, the undistributed middle. Each looks like a valid form, which is what makes it tempting. But calling something a fallacy is a stronger charge than calling it invalid. Many good arguments in science and daily life are deductively invalid, and an invalid form proves only that the premises do not guarantee the conclusion, not that the conclusion is false.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Formal and informal

"Formal fallacies are those readily seen to be instances of identifiable invalid logical forms such as undistributed middle and denying the antecedent" [@hansen2024b, preamble]. Informal fallacies may be invalid too, but their weakness is better shown "through analyses that do not involve appeal to formal languages" [@hansen2024b, preamble].

#### 2. Why fallacies tempt

A working definition: "a fallacy to be an argument that seems to be better than it really is" [@hansen2024b, § 1]. Aristotle already explained the appeal of affirming the consequent: consequence is not convertible, yet "if A is, B necessarily is, men also fancy that, if B is, A necessarily is" [@hansen2024b, § 2.1.4]. This "is sometimes claimed as being an early statement of the formal fallacy of affirming the consequent" [@hansen2024b, § 2.1.4].

#### 3. How to show a form is invalid

The method is the counterexample. Finocchiaro's first kind of fallacious argument, formal fallaciousness, "can be demonstrated by producing a suitable analogous counter-example in which the premises are true and the conclusion is false" [@hansen2024b, § 3.2].

### Common confusions

- **Every invalid argument is a fallacy.** Defining fallacies by deductive invalidity is "too wide because it condemns good but non-deductive arguments as fallacies" [@hansen2024b, § 3.5]. "If my hypothesis is true, we will see this result; we see it; so my hypothesis is true" has the form of affirming the consequent. As a deduction it fails; as evidence it may be strong, as the chapter notes.
- **Every fallacy is an invalid argument.** That definition is also "too narrow because it excludes begging the question which is not invalid" [@hansen2024b, § 3.5].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Do formal fallacies even happen?** Finocchiaro observed that textbook treatments "contain very few examples of actual fallacies". He allows that errors in reasoning are common but thinks that "types of logically incorrect arguments" are probably not, and so prefers to speak of fallacious arguments rather than fallacies [@hansen2024b, § 3.2].
- **One logic or many?** Woods and Walton argue that standard formal logic "is too coarse conceptually to reveal the unique character of many of the fallacies", and that each fallacy needs a fitting logical system: inductive logic for hasty generalization, dialectical game theory for begging the question. Woods calls this "methodological pluralism" [@hansen2024b, § 3.4].
- **Fallacies and biases.** Mill saw biases as predisposing causes of fallacies. Correia links particular biases to particular fallacies: the "focussing illusion", for example, to hasty generalization and the straw man [@hansen2024b, § 4.4]. See [Heuristics and biases](14-psychology-of-reasoning.md#heuristics-and-biases).

### What the chapter simplifies

- **The chapter's definition.** It defines a formal fallacy as an argument with an invalid form. Most fallacy theorists add an appearance condition: the argument must also seem good. Copi's influential definition is "a form of argument that seems to be correct but which proves, upon examination, not to be so" [@hansen2024b, § 2.10].
