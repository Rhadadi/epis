> **In short.** Arguments happen between people, and some of the most useful moves are about the exchange rather than the logic of a single argument: the counterexample, the parody that runs an opponent's reasoning on a different subject, the argument from an opponent's own concessions. Each has a precise structure and precise limits. Logicians have even rebuilt logic itself as a game between two players arguing over a thesis.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Parity of reasoning: Gaunilo's island

The oldest famous parody is Gaunilo's reply to Anselm's ontological argument. Replace "that than which no greater can be conceived" with "that island than which no greater island can be conceived", keep every step the same, and you seem to prove that a perfect island exists [@oppy-etal2025, § 11]. "Since the arguments have the same logical form, either they are both valid or they are both invalid. If they are both invalid, then the Proslogion II argument fails" [@oppy-etal2025, § 11]. If both are valid, the defender of the original must find a premise that is true in the original and false in the parody [@oppy-etal2025, § 11].

#### 2. A parody targets one formulation

Parodies "are tied to particular formulations of ontological arguments, and are intended only to raise questions about the probative value of those particular formulations" [@oppy-etal2025, § 11]. A good parody does not refute every version of a view, only the argument as stated. And it can be adjusted to block easy escapes: if islands seem unrankable, use "that score on this test than which no greater score on this test can be conceived" [@oppy-etal2025, § 11].

#### 3. Arguing from the other side's concessions

Locke's original *argumentum ad hominem* was a legitimate move: "to press a man with consequences drawn from his own principles or concessions", that is, "to argue that an opponent's view is inconsistent, logically or pragmatically, with other things he has said or to which he is committed" [@hansen2024b, § 2.4]. Whately drew its limit. From an opponent's concessions you can show that they are "committed to p", but presenting that as a proof of p itself "would be a fallacy" [@hansen2024b, § 2.7].

### Common confusions

- **Any tu quoque is a fallacy.** The fallacious tu quoque rejects advice "because the espouser him- or herself does not follow it": a neighbour's advice to exercise is not refuted by her not exercising, since "the value of advice is not wholly dependent on the integrity of the advisor" [@hansen2024b, § 1]. Pointing out that someone's principle undermines their own position is different: it tests the principle's consistency, Locke's legitimate ad hominem [@hansen2024b, § 2.4].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Logic as a game.** Dialogical logic goes back "to dialectics in Greek Antiquity, when problems were approached through dialogues in which opposing parties discussed a thesis through questions and answers". In its modern form, due to Paul Lorenzen and Kuno Lorenz, two players argue over a thesis under rules that "allow these players to challenge the other's statements or defend their own statements in such a way that one player wins and the other loses after a finite number of moves" [@clerbout-mcconaughey2022, preamble]. A valid thesis is one for which a player has a winning strategy "whatever the other player's moves may be" [@clerbout-mcconaughey2022, preamble].
- **Meaning from moves.** In this framework the meaning of "and", "or" and "not" is "provided by a set of interaction rules": what you may do when you attack or defend a statement containing them [@clerbout-mcconaughey2022, preamble]. Compare the inferentialist view of validity in [Validity and soundness](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/validity-and-soundness).
