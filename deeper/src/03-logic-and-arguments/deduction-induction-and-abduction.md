> **In short.** Deduction draws out what premises guarantee; induction generalizes from what has been observed; abduction infers the hypothesis that would best explain the evidence. Peirce showed how the three fit together with a bag of beans: each is the same syllogism with its parts rearranged. The division is useful, but it is less tidy than textbooks suggest. There is no agreed test for telling deductive from inductive arguments, "abduction" means two different things, and whether explaining well is a sign of being true is one of the live questions in the philosophy of science.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Necessary and non-necessary inference

"In deductive inferences, what is inferred is necessarily true if the premises from which it is inferred are true." Contrast inferring "John is rich" from "John lives in Chelsea" and "Most people living in Chelsea are rich": the premises make the conclusion likely but do not guarantee it [@douven2025, § 1.1]. "It is standard practice to group non-necessary inferences into inductive and abductive ones" [@douven2025, § 1.1].

#### 2. Induction and abduction are both ampliative

Both go beyond their premises, so the conclusion "goes beyond what is (logically) contained in the premises". The difference is the kind of support: "in abduction there is an implicit or explicit appeal to explanatory considerations, whereas in induction there is not; in induction, there is only an appeal to observed frequencies or statistics" [@douven2025, § 1.1]. So the same data can support either. Seeing many grey elephants and no others, you might infer that all elephants are grey "because that would provide the best explanation" of what you saw; that is abduction [@douven2025, § 1.1].

#### 3. Abduction in daily life

You know that Tim and Harry had a row that ended their friendship; now someone has seen them jogging together. "The best explanation for this that you can think of is that they made up." You have no useful statistics about rows and joggers; what leads you to the conclusion is that it "would, if true, best explain" what was seen [@douven2025, § 1].

#### 4. New information can undo induction and abduction

Abduction shares with induction a feature deduction lacks: "it violates monotonicity". Add the premise that Tim and Harry are former business partners with money matters to settle, and the inference that they are friends again may no longer be warranted [@douven2025, § 1.1].

### Common confusions

- **Deduction goes from general to particular.** The chapter already flags this as rough. The deeper problem is that there is no agreed criterion at all; see "The distinction is in chaos" below [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 10].
- **Holmes uses deduction.** He calls it "the Science of Deduction", but his reasoning about Watson's arm and tan is a chain of explanatory guesses: "Clearly an army doctor, then", "Clearly in Afghanistan" [@doyle1887, ch. 2]. Each "clearly" marks the best explanation he can think of, not a necessary consequence.

## The full story

### How the idea developed

::: timeline Three kinds of inference
- **4th century BCE** · Aristotle recognises two species of argument, deduction and induction (*epagôgê*), "argument from the particular to the universal" [@smith2022, § 3.1].
- **1620** · Bacon advertises induction "as the royal road to knowledge" in the *New Organon*; Descartes's *Discourse* (1637) favours deduction [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 1].
- **1878** · Peirce's "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis" adds a third kind, hypothesis, later called abduction [@peirce1878, § I].
- **1887** · Sherlock Holmes announces "the Science of Deduction" in *A Study in Scarlet* [@doyle1887, ch. 2].
- **1965** · Harman treats induction as a special type of abduction [@douven2025, § 1.1].
- **1989** · Van Fraassen's "best of a bad lot" objection to inference to the best explanation [@douven2025, § 2].
:::

Aristotle "has far less to say" about induction than about deduction, but it does essential work in his theory of science: induction, "or at any rate a cognitive process that moves from particulars to their generalizations", is "the basis of knowledge of the indemonstrable first principles of sciences" [@smith2022, § 3.1]. The early moderns took sides: Bacon promoted induction, while Descartes and the rationalists favoured deduction [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 1].

### Peirce's beans

Peirce's paper reduces all three kinds of inference to one syllogism. A deduction applies a rule to a case to reach a result. Induction and hypothesis invert that syllogism in two different ways.

::: original Peirce, "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis" (1878)
> Suppose I enter a room and there find a number of bags, containing different kinds of beans. On the table there is a handful of white beans; and, after some searching, I find one of the bags contains white beans only. I at once infer as a probability, or as a fair guess, that this handful was taken out of that bag. This sort of inference is called making an hypothesis. It is the inference of a case from a rule and result.

Peirce then lines up the three. **Deduction:** Rule, all the beans from this bag are white; Case, these beans are from this bag; so Result, these beans are white. **Induction:** from the Case and the Result, infer the Rule. **Hypothesis:** from the Rule and the Result, infer the Case. Induction "is the inference of the rule from the case and result"; hypothesis "is the inference of a case from a rule and result".
:::

[@peirce1878, § I]. His examples of hypothesis are vivid. Landing at a Turkish port, he met a man on horseback under a canopy held by four horsemen, and since the governor was "the only personage I could think of who would be so greatly honored", he inferred that it was the governor. Fossil fish far inland lead us to suppose "the sea once washed over this land". And "Numberless documents and monuments refer to a conqueror called Napoleon Bonaparte"; we cannot explain them "without supposing that he really existed" [@peirce1878, § I].

He was frank about its strength: "As a general rule, hypothesis is a weak kind of argument. It often inclines our judgment so slightly toward its conclusion that we cannot say that we believe the latter to be true; we only surmise that it may be so" [@peirce1878, § I]. Later Peirce placed abduction inside a cycle of inquiry: "abduction (making conjectures or creating hypotheses), deduction (inferring what should be the case if the hypotheses are the case), and induction (the testing of hypotheses)" [@burch2024, § 4].

### Two meanings of "abduction"

Here lies a trap for readers. "In the historically first sense, it refers to the place of explanatory reasoning in generating hypotheses, while in the sense in which it is used most frequently in the modern literature it refers to the place of explanatory reasoning in justifying hypotheses" [@douven2025, preamble]. Peirce's abduction is a way of *finding* a hypothesis worth testing; modern abduction, inference to the best explanation, is a reason to *believe* one. Douven notes that Peirce's own fairly precise statement "does not capture what most nowadays understand by abduction" [@douven2025, § 2].

### Holmes on Watson

::: original Arthur Conan Doyle, A Study in Scarlet (1887), chapter 2
> The train of reasoning ran, 'Here is a gentleman of a medical type, but with the air of a military man. Clearly an army doctor, then. He has just come from the tropics, for his face is dark, and that is not the natural tint of his skin, for his wrists are fair. He has undergone hardship and sickness, as his haggard face says clearly. His left arm has been injured. He holds it in a stiff and unnatural manner. Where in the tropics could an English army doctor have seen much hardship and got his arm wounded? Clearly in Afghanistan.'

Every step infers a hypothesis that would explain an observation. A dark face with fair wrists is best explained by a tan; a tan by the tropics; a stiff arm by a wound. Each could be wrong (Watson might have been a doctor who sailed for pleasure), so this is abduction, as the chapter says. Holmes adds that the train of thought "did not occupy a second", and that he arrived at the conclusion "without being conscious of intermediate steps".
:::

[@doyle1887, ch. 2]

### The distinction is in chaos

Textbooks present the deductive/inductive distinction as settled. Shanahan's survey of how it is actually drawn finds otherwise [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 10]. Some accounts define it by the arguer's psychology: an argument is deductive if the arguer believes the premises establish the conclusion definitely. That has an odd consequence: "what is ostensibly the very same argument may be equally both deductive and inductive", depending on who offers it [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 2]. Others appeal to what the argument purports, to evidential completeness, to necessity versus probability, or to formalization, and "All of these proposals entail problems of one sort or another" [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 10].

Some authors, Shanahan notes, acknowledge that the right way to draw the line is "controversial". "Yet, there seems to be remarkably little actual controversy about it. Instead, matters persist in a state of largely unacknowledged chaos" [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 10]. His recommendation is an evaluative approach: stop classifying arguments, and ask of any argument "Does the argument prove its conclusion, or does it only render it probable, or does it do neither?" [@deductive-inductive-arguments-iep, § 10]. That is also the spirit of the chapter's advice to evaluate arguments by the standard they can meet.

### Is the best explanation likely to be true?

The textbook rule for abduction is: "Given evidence E and candidate explanations H1,…, Hn of E, infer the truth of that Hi which best explains E" [@douven2025, § 2]. It faces three problems.

**What makes an explanation best?** Usually the answer appeals to theoretical virtues such as "simplicity, generality, and coherence with well-established theories", but "none of the said virtues is presently particularly well understood" [@douven2025, § 2].

**The bad lot.** The rule picks the best of the hypotheses we happened to think of. Unless the true explanation is typically among them, it "may well lead us to believe “the best of a bad lot”" [@douven2025, § 2]. One repair, due to Musgrave and Lipton, is to require the winner to be not only the best available but "satisfactory" or "good enough"; the catch is that "we are still lacking" a criterion for good enough [@douven2025, § 2].

::: argument Van Fraassen's argument against inference to the best explanation
1. A more explanatory theory must be more informative than its rival.
2. A more informative theory cannot be more likely to be true, since it has more ways of being false.
3. So explanatory superiority cannot be a mark of greater likelihood of truth.
:::

[@douven2025, § 3.1]. Douven grants premise 2 only in the "paradigm case" where one theory extends another. Elsewhere it is unclear: special relativity and Lorentz's ether theory make the same predictions, and it is "entirely unclear" in what sense relativity "has more ways of being false", yet it is regarded as the better explanation [@douven2025, § 3.1]. Van Fraassen's second objection is that a probabilistic abduction rule either agrees with Bayes' rule, and is redundant, or departs from it and is open to a Dutch book. Douven replies that a rule which reaches high probability for the truth faster might be worth that cost [@douven2025, § 3.1].

Defences of abduction are empirical rather than a priori. "Hardly anyone nowadays would want to subscribe to a conception of truth that posits a necessary connection between explanatory force and truth" [@douven2025, § 3.2]. And Bayesianism, now "the dominant view on confirmation", makes no reference to explanation at all. Lipton argues that the two are compatible and that "Bayesians should also be “explanationists”": explanatory considerations can guide the priors and likelihoods that Bayes' theorem needs [@douven2025, § 4].

::: positions Where abduction stands
| View | Who | Claim | Objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abduction as discovery | Peirce | Explanatory reasoning generates hypotheses to test | Says nothing about when to believe them |
| Inference to the best explanation | Harman, Lipton | Explaining best is a reason to believe | The bad lot; what makes an explanation best |
| Skepticism about IBE | van Fraassen | Explanatory power is no mark of truth | Relies on "more informative is less probable" |
| Explanationist Bayesianism | Lipton and others | Explanation guides the inputs to Bayes' rule | Unclear exactly how explanation should fix the priors |
:::

[@douven2025, preamble; @douven2025, § 2; @douven2025, § 3.1; @douven2025, § 4]

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Peirce on memory.** Peirce saw no difference "except one of degree" between a weak hypothesis and the inference "by which we are led to believe that we remember the occurrences of yesterday from our feeling as if we did so" [@peirce1878, § I]. Much of what we call knowledge rests on abduction. Compare [Memory](07-sources-of-knowledge.md#memory).
- **Explanations feel like evidence.** "Experimental studies have shown that when people are able to think of an explanation for some possible event, they tend to overestimate the likelihood that this event will actually occur" [@douven2025, § 3]. A good story is not yet a probable one.
- **Conductive arguments.** Some informal logicians add a third family of support: arguments that accumulate "non-decisive reasons" [@groarke2026, § 4.1]. See [Strength and cogency](deeper:03-logic-and-arguments/strength-and-cogency).

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"A term from Charles Sanders Peirce, often called inference to the best explanation."** These are two different ideas: Peirce's abduction generates hypotheses, inference to the best explanation justifies them [@douven2025, preamble].
- **The table's "Key figure" row.** Harman did more than name inference to the best explanation; he "conceives induction as a special type of abduction" [@douven2025, § 1.1].
