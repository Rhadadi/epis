> **In short.** Toulmin's six-part model describes an argument as a step from data to a claim, licensed by a warrant. The warrant is a general rule that usually holds but has exceptions, so the claim comes with a qualifier ("presumably") and a list of rebuttals ("unless..."). That makes the model a picture of defeasible reasoning, reasoning whose conclusions can be withdrawn when new facts arrive. It grew out of the way lawyers argue, and it has a serious rival: treat every argument as deductive and put the warrant in as a premise.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Where the model came from

Toulmin built on an observation from law. H. L. A. Hart had noted in 1949 that "judges must take into account exceptional circumstances in which a legal principle cannot be applied at all or must be applied in a weakened form" [@koons2025, § 1.1]. A contract can be defeated even when the standard definition of "contract" is met. "Toulmin, building on Hart's observations, argues for the importance of distinguishing between warrants and rebuttals" [@koons2025, § 1.1].

#### 2. The warrant is the hidden premise

In ordinary standardization, the assumptions that let a reasoner move from premises to conclusion are often left implicit; Groarke describes such implicit premises as providing "a “warrant” for the inference" [@groarke2026, § 3.1]. Toulmin's model gives that role a name and a place.

#### 3. Rebuttals are defeaters

Modern epistemology names two ways a defeasible inference can fail. Pollock distinguished "rebutting defeaters (which give one a prima facie reason for believing the denial of the original conclusion)" from "undercutting defeaters (which give one a reason for doubting that the usual relationship between the premises and the conclusion hold in the given case)" [@koons2025, § 1.1]. Toulmin's "unless his parents were aliens" is an undercutter: it does not show that Harry is not British, only that the Bermuda rule may not apply to him.

### Common confusions

- **Toulmin rejected logic.** Informal logic, for which *The Uses of Argument* became a "theoretical touchstone", still shares with formal logic "a premise, inference, and conclusion conception of argument" and the idea that a good argument has acceptable premises and a conclusion that follows from them [@groarke2026, § 1].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **The deductivist alternative.** Natural Language Deductivism keeps classical validity. It treats an argument that is not obviously deductive as relying on an implicit "associated" conditional, "If P, then C" [@groarke2026, § 4.3]. "Dr. Oz says it, so it must be true" becomes valid once you add "If Dr. Oz says this, it must be true"; the argument is then valid but unsound, "a valid argument with a problematic implicit premise" [@groarke2026, § 4.3]. Toulmin would call that conditional the warrant and ask for its backing. The two approaches put the weakness in the same place.
- **Deduction need not mean certainty.** Groarke notes that deductive validity ensures "only that the conclusion of an argument is as likely true (and certain) as its premises" [@groarke2026, § 4.3]. So Toulmin's qualifier is not a special feature of non-deductive arguments.
- **Argument schemes and critical questions.** Recurring patterns of argument (by sign, by analogy, by example, slippery slope) are now catalogued as schemes; Walton, Reed and Macagno list 96. The usual approach pairs each scheme with "a set of “critical questions”" [@groarke2026, § 4.4]. The critical questions do the work of Toulmin's backing and rebuttal.
- **The legal setting.** Hart's point was that a judge must decide "on the basis of an incomplete set of facts", those the two parties present [@koons2025, § 1.1]. That is the situation Toulmin's model was built for.
