> **In short.** An inductive argument is strong when its premises, if true, would make its conclusion probable, and cogent when it is strong and its premises are in fact acceptable. Strength comes in degrees and depends on things outside the argument: how the sample was drawn, how varied the evidence is, and what we already know about the kind of thing in question. That last point runs deep. On one influential view, every inductive inference rests on specific background facts rather than on one general rule.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Strong, not valid

"In an inductive argument, the premises are intended only to be so strong that, if they were true, then it would be unlikely, although possible, that the conclusion is false." So "It is inappropriate to criticize an inductively strong argument for being invalid" [@argument-iep, § 3]. A word like "probably" in the conclusion often signals that the arguer means it inductively [@argument-iep, § 3].

#### 2. Variety counts

Evidence from varied cases confirms more strongly than evidence from similar ones. If tigers and elephants share a trait, that is better evidence that all mammals have it than if tigers and lions do, because it is "a priori less likely that species as diverse as tigers and elephants share some unspecified genetic trait" [@crupi2025, § 3.6]. "The variety of evidence is a fairly central issue in the analysis of confirmation", and experiments show that most people, children included, judge this way too [@crupi2025, § 3.6].

#### 3. Background knowledge decides what to project

Not every regularity in a sample supports a prediction. "Suppose that on all my birthdays so far, I have been under 40 years old. This does not give me a reason to expect that I will be under 40 years old on my next birthday" [@henderson2024, § 4.2]. Whether to extrapolate depends on what we know about the property.

### Common confusions

- **Strength is a feature of the argument's form.** On the "material" view of Norton and Okasha, inductive inferences have no formal rule in common; "each inductive inference presupposes some more specific empirical presupposition" [@henderson2024, § 4.2]. Copper's conductivity can be generalized from a few samples because of what we know about metals, not because of the shape of the argument.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **A third kind of support.** Some informal logicians question the deductive/inductive "great divide". "Conductive" arguments "support their conclusions by accumulating non-decisive reasons in their favor"; they are good "when they collect enough reasons to warrant but not decisively prove their conclusions" [@groarke2026, § 4.1]. In conductive arguments the premises are convergent, so removing one leaves the others' support unchanged [@argument-iep, § 3].
- **Acceptability, relevance, sufficiency.** Many informal logics replace soundness and cogency with three criteria: premises must be acceptable, relevant ("provide some (i.e. any) support"), and sufficient ("provide enough support to warrant its acceptance") [@groarke2026, § 4.1].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Charity in classification.** Whether an argument is deductive or inductive often depends on how it is read. "Charity dictates that an invalid argument which is inductively strong be evaluated as an inductive argument unless there is clear evidence to the contrary" [@argument-iep, § 3].
