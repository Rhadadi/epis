> **In short.** A paradox is a small set of claims, each of which seems true, that cannot all be true together. That makes it a test: something we confidently believe, about truth, knowledge, rational belief or logic itself, has to go. The liar has been used to argue against classical logic; the preface paradox has convinced many philosophers that rational belief can be inconsistent. The way a theorist resolves a paradox tells you more about their view than almost anything else.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. A paradox is an inconsistent set

"A paradox is commonly defined as a set of propositions that are individually plausible but jointly inconsistent. The inconsistency is the itch that directs us to scratch out a member of the set" [@sorensen2024, § 4]. Sorensen's example is the regress of justification. Which is false: a belief can be justified only by another justified belief; there are no circular chains of justification; all chains are finite; some beliefs are justified? "Foundationalists reject (1)" [@sorensen2024, § 4]. Each theory of justification in [Chapter 6](06-justification.md#the-regress-problem) is a choice of which claim to drop.

#### 2. The preface paradox

Raymond Wilder apologised in his preface for the errors in his mathematics book, and the 1982 reprint "has three pages of errata that vindicate Wilder's humility". He rationally believes each assertion in the book, and, knowing himself fallible, rationally believes that not all of them are true [@sorensen2024, § 4]. If rational belief is closed under conjunction, he believes and disbelieves the same thing. "Many philosophers join Kyburg in rejecting agglomeration and conclude that it can be rational to have jointly inconsistent beliefs" [@sorensen2024, § 4].

#### 3. Moore's paradox

Moore's sentence is "I went to the pictures last Tuesday, but I don't believe that I did." The common explanation of its absurdity is that "the speaker has managed to contradict himself without uttering a contradiction". There is "no problem with third person counterparts" [@sorensen2024, § 5.3]: "He went to the pictures, but he doesn't believe he did" is fine.

### Common confusions

- **Paradoxes are puzzles for logicians only.** "Epistemic paradoxes have been discussed for twenty five hundred years", and they goad us "into correcting at least one deep error", about knowledge or "its kindred concepts such as justification, rational belief, and evidence" [@sorensen2024, preamble].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **What the liar shows.** Tarski "seems to have thought the Liar shows the ordinary notion of truth to be incoherent, and in need of replacement". The dominant view is that the principles governing truth "are more subtle than the T-schema reflects". The liar has also been the core of arguments for logics that are "paracomplete" (Kripke, Field) or "paraconsistent" (Priest) [@beall-etal2026, § 3].
- **The liar and the heap.** The sorites was long seen as unrelated to the paradoxes of self-reference, but McGee and Tappenden proposed a unified treatment "based on similarities between vague predicates and the truth predicate" [@raffman-hyde2026, § 4].
- **A Cartesian joke.** The scope ambiguity of Moore's sentence underlies a joke: Descartes is offered another drink, says "I think not", and disappears [@sorensen2024, § 5.3].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Exactly three options."** The chapter says you can reject a premise, reject the reasoning, or accept the conclusion. Kyburg's response to the preface paradox adds a fourth: accept that the set is inconsistent and believe all of it anyway. Sorensen notes the cost: it raises the question of "How can paradoxes change our minds if joint inconsistency is permitted?" [@sorensen2024, § 4].
