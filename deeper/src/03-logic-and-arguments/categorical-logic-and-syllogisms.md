> **In short.** Aristotle's syllogistic was the first formal logic, and for two thousand years it was simply "logic". It deals with four kinds of sentence ("Every A is B", "No A is B", "Some A is B", "Not every A is B") and asks which pairs of them force a conclusion. Aristotle answered that question completely and correctly for his system, proved invalidity with counterexamples, and showed that every valid form reduces to two basic ones. Its limits only became clear when Frege's logic arrived.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. Aristotle's definition of a deduction

"A deduction is speech (logos) in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so" (*Prior Analytics* I.2) [@smith2022, § 3]. "Results of necessity" corresponds to the modern idea that the conclusion could not be false while the premises are true [@smith2022, § 3].

#### 2. Four forms and their contradictories

In *On Interpretation* Aristotle sets out the contradictories for sentences with universal subjects: "Every A is B" against "Not every A is B", and "No A is B" against "Some A is B" [@smith2022, § 4.3.1]. That is the core of the square of opposition, and the reason a single black swan refutes "Every swan is white".

#### 3. A complete survey

Aristotle sorted premise pairs into three "figures" by the position of the shared middle term. He then "systematically investigates all possible combinations of two premises in each of the three figures", showing for each that a conclusion follows or that none does. "The results he states are correct" [@smith2022, § 5.1].

#### 4. Invalidity by counterexample

"Aristotle proves invalidity by constructing counterexamples", Smith notes, "very much in the spirit of modern logical theory". To show that a pair of premises yields no conclusion, he gives triples of terms that make the premises true while each candidate conclusion is false [@smith2022, § 5.3]. This is the chapter's [method of counterexample](03-logic-and-arguments.md#the-method-of-counterexample), in its original form.

### Common confusions

- **The rules are later textbook inventions.** Several come from Aristotle himself. Having found the valid forms, he drew metatheoretical conclusions, including: "No deduction has two negative premises" and "A deduction with a negative conclusion must have one negative premise" [@smith2022, § 5.5].
- **"Barbara" is Aristotle's name.** The mood names are a medieval mnemonic; Aristotle "does not use this expression and instead refers to “the arguments in the figures”" [@smith2022, § 5.4].

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Axioms and reduction.** Aristotle distinguished "perfect" deductions, which need no proof, from "imperfect" ones, which he proves by reducing them to the perfect forms. "We might compare the perfect deductions to the axioms or primitive rules of a deductive system" [@smith2022, § 5.2]. He even proved that "All deductions can be reduced to the two universal deductions in the first figure" [@smith2022, § 5.5].
- **Not quite modern validity.** Aristotle requires the conclusion to be "different from what is supposed", which rules out arguments whose conclusion repeats a premise; modern logic counts them as valid, if trivially. Some commentators also read his definition as excluding one-premise arguments and arguments whose premises are irrelevant to the conclusion [@smith2022, § 3.2].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Individuals and kinds.** Aristotle "gives the same analysis to “Socrates is an animal” and “Humans are animals”", unlike modern logic, which treats a name and a general term very differently [@smith2022, § 4.3].
- **The square is simple.** "Simple as it appears, this table raises important difficulties of interpretation" [@smith2022, § 4.3.1].
