> **In short.** Standard form lists an argument's premises, draws a line, and puts the conclusion underneath. It forces two decisions that ordinary prose lets you dodge: exactly what is being claimed, and how the claims fit together. Do the premises work only together, or does each support the conclusion on its own? Is something unstated doing the real work? Those answers tell you where an argument can be attacked.

## Re-learn

### Step by step

#### 1. The layout

"The premises are listed first and a solid line separates them from the conclusion, which is prefaced by “∴”. This symbol means “therefore”" [@argument-iep, § 1].

#### 2. Linked or convergent?

Premises are linked when "they do not support the conclusion independently of one another". "Tom is happy only if he is playing guitar" and "Tom is not playing guitar" support "Tom is not happy" only together [@argument-iep, § 1]. Premises are convergent when "each is a reason that supports [3] independently of the other": Tom said he didn't go to the party, and no one saw him there [@argument-iep, § 1]. The difference tells a critic what a successful objection achieves. Knock out one linked premise and the argument collapses; knock out one convergent premise and the others still stand.

#### 3. Extended arguments

An extended argument has "at least one premise that a reasoner attempts to support explicitly". In McKeon's example, "I did not find the keys in the kitchen" supports "the keys are not in the kitchen", which then serves as a premise for "the keys are in the bedroom" [@argument-iep, § 1].

#### 4. Missing premises

An enthymeme is "an argument which is presented with at least one component that is suppressed". "You should believe that abortion is immoral. You're a Catholic" relies on the unstated premise that all Catholics should believe it [@argument-iep, § 1]. A suppressed premise is "never a reason for a conclusion independent of another explicitly offered": it is always linked [@argument-iep, § 1].

### Common confusions

- **Standard form is just tidying up.** It is already interpretation. A newspaper report on a dog-fighting case contains one stated premise, and Groarke's standardization adds two implicit premises and an implicit conclusion [@groarke2026, § 3.1]. Every choice about what to add is a choice about what the arguer meant.

## Beyond the chapter

### What the chapter leaves out

- **Tables and diagrams.** A premise list "does not distinguish between different kinds of inferences an argument may contain". One remedy is to pair a key-component table, listing each claim, its role and its source, with an argument diagram that shows the inferences [@groarke2026, § 3.2]. See [Argument mapping](03-logic-and-arguments.md#argument-mapping).
- **Conductive arguments.** Some logicians treat arguments with convergent premises as a third category beside deduction and induction: "If one or more premises were removed from the argument, the degree of support offered by the remaining premises would stay the same" [@argument-iep, § 3].
