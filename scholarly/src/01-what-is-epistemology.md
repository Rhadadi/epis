## Why epistemology matters for critical thinking

### Critical thinking as applied epistemology

The chapter calls critical thinking "applied epistemology". The two fields overlap heavily, but the people who study critical thinking describe it more broadly. David Hitchcock's survey notes that the definition of critical thinking is contested, and that the competing definitions agree on a core: careful thinking directed to a goal [@hitchcock2024, preamble]. The literature he reviews treats critical thinking as a matter of **dispositions** as well as abilities. It lists attentiveness, a habit of inquiry, open-mindedness, willingness to suspend judgment, trust in reason, wanting evidence for one's beliefs, and seeking the truth [@hitchcock2024, § 8.1]. It adds dispositions that keep the work going once it has started: keeping focus on the question, and being willing to drop a strategy that is not working [@hitchcock2024, § 8.2].

Epistemology gives critical thinking its standards: what counts as evidence, when a belief is justified, when to trust a source. It does not by itself make anyone apply those standards. That is one reason the guide treats intellectual character separately, in Chapter 13. Contemporary epistemologists have also turned to character traits as a topic in their own right. Carter and Sosa point out that "theory of knowledge" is an inaccurate label for the field. Epistemologists also study justification, understanding and rationality, and virtue epistemologists study the character traits, such as open-mindedness, intellectual honesty and intellectual courage, that put us in a good position to gain knowledge [@carter-sosa2026, § 2.1].

### The word "epistemology"

The *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* says only that James Frederick Ferrier was "the first philosopher in English" to call the philosophy of knowledge "epistemology". It adds that his epistemology is the foundation of his whole system: he insisted on securing the laws of thought before saying anything about reality [@keefe-iep, “Idealist Metaphysics”]. In the *Institutes of Metaphysic* the word names one of three divisions of philosophy. Ferrier sets "The Epistemology, or theory of knowledge" beside "The Agnoiology, or theory of ignorance" and "The Ontology, or theory of being". He claims that the problems of philosophy can be taken up in no other order [@ferrier1856, § 61]. Agnoiology, a theory of what can and cannot be *ignorance*, never caught on. The other two words became standard.

### The dinner-table dialogue, more carefully

Two of the six problems the chapter finds in the dinner-table dialogue have a long history.

**Testimony.** Locke already asked when another person's word gives us reason to believe. He made testimony one of the two "grounds of probability", the other being agreement with our own experience. To weigh testimony, he said, we must consider "the number", "the integrity" and "the skill of the witnesses", the author's design, the consistency of the account, and any contrary testimony [@locke1690, IV.xv.4]. The modern debate is between two positions. **Reductionists**, following Hume, hold that we are justified in believing a speaker only if we have positive reasons, not themselves drawn from testimony, to think the speaker reliable. **Non-reductionists**, following Thomas Reid, hold that we have a defeasible right to believe what we are told unless we have reason for doubt [@leonard2023, §§ 1.1–1.2]. Each side has a standard objection. Against reductionism: most of us could never check the reliability of the people we learn from, so it leads to skepticism (C. A. J. Coady's argument). Against non-reductionism: it seems to license gullibility, for example toward an anonymous blogger [@oconnor-etal2026, § 3.1; @leonard2023, § 1.2]. So "My cousin told me" and "Anecdotes aren't evidence" are positions in a live philosophical dispute.

**Proportioning belief.** The chapter's call to hold beliefs "with the right degree of confidence" echoes Hume: "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" [@hume1748, X.i]. Hume links this to the uneven regularity of experience. Some events have been "constantly conjoined", others only usually, and so there are "all imaginable degrees of assurance". Thomas Kelly makes the sentence the epigraph of his discussion of how rational thinkers "respect their evidence". He adds a warning the chapter leaves implicit: rationality is no guarantee of being right, because evidence can mislead [@kelly2016, § 2].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Anecdotes are weak evidence, not zero evidence."** This is right on the standard probabilistic account. Evidence *E* confirms hypothesis *H* to the degree that it raises *H*'s probability: *c(H, E) = p(H | E) − p(H)* [@weisberg2021, § 1.2]. The chapter's test, that *E* is more probable if *H* is true than if it is false, picks out the same cases whenever *H* is neither certain nor impossible. But how strong an anecdote is depends on how it reached you. If you would have heard about the cousin's weight loss whether or not the diet works (people pass on successes and forget failures), then hearing it is almost as likely either way, and it supports the hypothesis very little. A bare anecdote is weak evidence, and an anecdote that reached you through a selective filter can be weaker still.
- **Critical thinking is more than epistemology.** As the literature above shows, much of it is about dispositions and practice, which is why educators assess it with tests of abilities *and* attitudes [@hitchcock2024, preamble].
- **"Ferrier coined the term."** The reference works consulted here go no further than saying that Ferrier was the first philosopher writing in English to use the word for the theory of knowledge [@keefe-iep, “Idealist Metaphysics”]. Whether anyone printed the word before him is a question for historical lexicography, and this companion does not settle it.

## The central questions

### The problem of the criterion

The chapter's two prior questions, "What do we know?" and "How do we know?", are the classic form of **the problem of the criterion**. Roderick Chisholm called it "one of the most important and one of the most difficult of all the problems of philosophy" [@chisholm1973, 1; @mccain-iep, introduction]. The problem is ancient. Sextus Empiricus presents it in Book 2 of the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* as the "wheel" or *diallelus*, in the context of the debate between the Academic Skeptics and the Stoics [@mccain-iep, § 1]. The idea of a criterion itself goes back to Epicurus. He spoke of a *kanôn* ("measuring stick") and a *kritêrion*: the evident content of perception, against which claims about the non-evident are tested. The Stoic Zeno proposed instead the "cognitive impression" as the criterion of truth [@vogt2026, § 3.1].

Chisholm recognised three responses:

- **Particularism** starts from particular cases of knowledge and derives criteria from them.
- **Methodism** starts from criteria and uses them to sort the cases.
- **Skepticism** says that neither question can be answered without first answering the other, so neither can be answered.

Chisholm argued that each response begs the question against the others. He defended particularism anyway [@mccain-iep, § 2]. Robert Amico objected that Chisholm gives no independent reason to prefer particularism. Chisholm replied that only particularism allows any progress in epistemology. He was, in the end, frank: "the problem of the criterion has no solution" [@mccain-iep, § 2].

Carter and Sosa explain why the choice matters beyond this one puzzle. Descartes and Hume both start as methodists, and that starting point commits them to **foundationalism** about the structure of justified belief [@carter-sosa2026, § 3.1]. The same choice of starting point returns wherever epistemologists theorize: about justification, understanding and the intellectual virtues, not only knowledge [@carter-sosa2026, § 3.1].

### Is it one problem or several?

Kevin McCain notes an ambiguity in Chisholm. Sometimes the problem concerns how to tell when we *have knowledge* (a meta-epistemological question). At other times, as in Montaigne's version, it concerns how to tell *true from false appearances* (a first-order question). He suggests that "the Problem of the Criterion" may be a family of related problems rather than one [@mccain-iep, § 1].

### The table of questions

The chapter's table is a fair map of the questions epistemologists ask. Carter and Sosa note that the broad description of epistemology as the "theory of knowledge" is "an oversimplified picture". The field's two traditional questions, about the *nature* and the *extent* of knowledge, sit beside questions about justification, rationality, understanding and intellectual character [@carter-sosa2026, §§ 2.1, 3.1].

The first question in the table, *what is knowledge?*, has a classic ancestor in Plato's *Theaetetus*. Theaetetus first suggests that knowledge is true opinion. Socrates replies with the jurors who reach a true verdict from a persuasive orator without having seen the crime: they have true opinion but not knowledge. Theaetetus then offers that knowledge is "true opinion, combined with definition or rational explanation" [@plato-theaetetus, 201a–d]. This is the forerunner of the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, discussed in Chapter 5.

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Philosophers have argued about their order for 2,500 years."** The problem is that old, but the argument was not continuous. After Sextus, interest largely lapsed until Montaigne's *Apology for Raymond Sebond* in the late 1500s. It lapsed again until Hegel (1807), and was revived by Cardinal Mercier (1884) and P. Coffey (1917), well before Chisholm [@mccain-iep, § 1].
- **"A circle".** In Chisholm's framing the circle does not show that knowledge is impossible. It shows that every answer begins with an assumption the other side rejects. Chisholm's own conclusion was that we must choose a starting point and accept that we cannot prove it to an opponent who starts elsewhere [@mccain-iep, § 2].

## Three kinds of knowing

### Words for knowing

The chapter's contrast between *savoir* and *connaître* is standard. Steup and Neta add Latin *cognitio* and *scientia*. They caution that how to individuate kinds of cognitive success "is not something that can be determined solely by appeal to the lexicon of any particular natural language" [@steup-neta2025, § 2]. Two languages that both mark a distinction may draw the line in different places, and a language that marks none may still recognise the difference.

### Knowledge-how: Ryle and the intellectualists

Ryle's regress argument is, in his words: "The consideration of propositions is itself an operation the execution of which can be more or less intelligent, less or more stupid". If every intelligent act required a prior intelligent act of theorizing, "it would be a logical impossibility for anyone ever to break into the circle". He concluded that "knowing how" cannot be defined in terms of "knowing that" [@ryle1949, 19–20; @pavese2022, § 1]. The argument first appears in his 1946 presidential address to the Aristotelian Society [@ryle1946].

Two points about the later debate are worth knowing:

1. **How to reconstruct the regress is itself disputed.** Stanley argued that if the regress worked against intellectualism, a parallel regress would work against Ryle: employing knowledge-how is also an action, which on Ryle's terms would need some prior capacity to guide it [@pavese2022, §§ 1, 1.2].
2. **Intellectualism has to explain the cases where someone knows the relevant fact but cannot do the thing.** A non-swimmer might know a true proposition about how to swim and still drown when thrown in the pool. Stanley and Williamson's answer is that knowing how requires grasping the way under a **practical mode of presentation** [@stanley-williamson2001; @pavese2022, § 2]. Critics have called this notion obscure or even question-begging. Koethe worries that it smuggles in a prior notion of knowing how [@pavese2022, § 2].

Anti-intellectualism has its own difficulties. It is often read as identifying knowing how with an *ability*, but "few theorists nowadays identify knowledge-how with bare abilities" [@pavese2022, § 8.2]. Long before the recent revival, psychologists and neuroscientists had already used Ryle's distinction to describe their findings [@pavese2022, preamble].

### Knowledge by acquaintance: what Russell meant

Russell's definition is narrower than the chapter's examples suggest. We have acquaintance with "anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths" [@russell1912, ch. V; @hasan-fumerton2024, § 1]. For Russell, we are acquainted with the data of the senses, with our own thoughts, feelings and desires through introspection, with past data through memory, probably with the self, and with universals such as *whiteness* [@russell1912, ch. V]. **Physical objects and other people are known only by description.** In his example, even a friend of Bismarck's was directly acquainted only with certain sense-data, which he connected with Bismarck's body; the body and mind themselves "were only known as the body and the mind connected with these sense-data", that is, by description [@russell1912, ch. V]. Russell's governing principle is: "Every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted" [@russell1912, ch. V]. His earlier paper sets out the same distinction [@russell1911].

Hasan and Fumerton point out a puzzle of interpretation. Russell says that knowledge of things by acquaintance is "logically independent of knowledge of truths". So it may not be propositional knowledge at all, but knowledge *of* something rather than knowledge *that* something is so [@hasan-fumerton2024, § 1]. Traditional acquaintance theorists take states of consciousness and their properties as the clearest objects of acquaintance. Some recent writers defend acquaintance with physical objects in perception [@hasan-fumerton2024, § 1].

### Mary and the knowledge argument

Frank Jackson's case is usually discussed in philosophy of mind, but the main responses are epistemological, because they turn on what *kind* of knowledge Mary gains [@jackson1982]:

- **The ability hypothesis** (David Lewis, Laurence Nemirow): Mary gains no new propositional knowledge, only abilities, such as the ability to imagine, remember and recognise colour experiences. For Nemirow, "knowing what an experience is like is the same as knowing how to imagine having the experience" [@nidarumelin-oconaill2026, § 4.3].
- **The acquaintance hypothesis** (Earl Conee): what Mary gains is acquaintance knowledge, which is neither propositional knowledge nor a bundle of abilities [@nidarumelin-oconaill2026, § 4.3].

So Mary's case does not merely *illustrate* the three kinds of knowing. It is a test of whether they are really distinct. Non-philosophers seem to side with the intuition: in one survey, 82% of respondents said Mary in her room would not know what it is like to see red [@nidarumelin-oconaill2026, § 4.1]. Jackson himself later rejected the argument. He came to think that it presupposes a false view of sensory experience, and that it can be answered by a representationalist account that is physicalist [@nidarumelin-oconaill2026, § 5].

### Knowledge by presence

The Islamic distinction the chapter mentions has a precise source in Suhrawardi's "philosophy of illumination". He rejected the Peripatetic, broadly Avicennian, model on which knowing is having a representation or form of the object in the knower. In its place he set **knowledge by presence** (*ʿilm ḥuḍūrī*), modelled on self-awareness, in which the thing known is itself present to the knower [@marcotte2025, § 3.2]. Mehdi Ha'iri Yazdi's study is the standard book-length treatment in English [@haeriyazdi1992].

### Knowledge-wh and understanding

On the standard view, to know whether, who or where is to know the proposition that answers the embedded question. Sooyung knows whether the shop is open by knowing that it is not open [@ichikawa-steup2026, preamble]. Stanley and Williamson extended this treatment to knowing how [@steup-neta2025, § 2.2].

Understanding raises questions of its own. Philosophers disagree about whether understanding, unlike knowledge, can survive certain kinds of luck. Kvanvig thinks it can; Grimm and Pritchard think it needs a reliable source, though Pritchard and Hills allow some environmental luck [@grimm2025, § 2.3; @pritchard-etal2025, § 4]. They also disagree about whether understanding can be passed on by testimony. Zagzebski and Hills doubt it, because grasping is first-hand or a skill; Grimm thinks it often can [@grimm2025, § 3.2].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"I know Tehran" and "I know my sister" are ordinary-language examples of knowing things and people.** They are not examples of acquaintance in Russell's technical sense: for Russell, cities and sisters are known by description.
- **The three-way division is a starting point.** The chapter says as much in its exercise answers. The live questions are whether knowledge-how reduces to knowledge-that, and whether acquaintance is knowledge of truths at all. Ordinary language does not settle either.

## Belief, credence, and acceptance

### Dispositional belief, and what belief is

The chapter says that beliefs are "commonly understood as dispositional". Two different claims should be kept apart here.

- **The occurrent/dispositional distinction** is uncontroversial. Harry believes plaid ties are hideous even while asleep. The belief is occurrent only when the thought comes to mind [@schwitzgebel2024, § 2.1].
- **Dispositionalism** is a contested theory of what belief *is*. It says that to believe *p* is to have a cluster of dispositions: to assent to *p*, to be surprised if *p* turns out false, to rely on *p* in planning [@schwitzgebel2024, § 1.2]. Its main rival, **representationalism**, says that to believe *p* is to have a stored representation with the content *p*: the "belief box" picture [@schwitzgebel2024, § 2.1].

Schwitzgebel notes that a strict dispositionalism may make occurrent belief impossible. A passing thought is a manifestation of the disposition, not the disposition itself [@schwitzgebel2024, § 2.1].

### Credence, the Lockean thesis and its paradoxes

Bayesian epistemology adds two core norms on credences. **Probabilism** says your credences should obey the probability calculus. **Conditionalization** says how they should change when you learn new evidence [@lin2024, § 1.1].

The **Lockean thesis** takes its name from passages in Locke that suggest belief is nothing more than sufficiently high credence. Richard Foley first formulated it explicitly. It is really a family of norms, one for each threshold [@pettigrew2024, § 4.2]. It meets two paradoxes:

- **The lottery paradox** (Henry Kyburg). Suppose a fair lottery has 100 tickets and the threshold is 0.99. Then you should believe of each ticket that it will lose, and also believe that some ticket will win. If rational beliefs can be combined ("agglomeration"), you end up rationally believing a contradiction. Kyburg's response was to give up agglomeration and tolerate beliefs that cannot all be true together [@sorensen2024, § 3; @geninhuber2026, § 4.2.2].
- **The preface paradox** (D. C. Makinson, using Raymond Wilder's 1952 apology for the errors in his book). An author rationally believes each claim in the book, and also rationally believes, from experience of his own fallibility, that at least one of them is false. This paradox does not rest on any probability threshold, so restricting the Lockean thesis does not dissolve it [@sorensen2024, § 4].

Attempts to repair the thesis add exception clauses, for example Douven's rule against believing members of "probabilistically self-undermining" sets. Kevin Korb produced a counterexample that tells against all proposals of this kind [@geninhuber2026, § 4.2.2].

The Lockean thesis can also be *derived* from a view about the goals of belief. That derivation connects this section with "Epistemology is normative"; see that section below.

### Acceptance

Schwitzgebel's survey describes acceptance as more voluntary than belief and tied to action in a context. Accepting something typically means deciding to stop inquiring and to act as if the matter were settled [@schwitzgebel2024, § 2.4]. Two cases show the two attitudes coming apart:

- **Acceptance without belief.** Van Fraassen argues that scientists often accept a theory as an adequate basis for research without believing that it is literally true [@schwitzgebel2024, § 2.4].
- **Belief without acceptance.** Bratman's ladder: you may believe the ladder is stable but, because so much depends on it, not accept that it is until you have checked [@schwitzgebel2024, § 2.4; @bratman1992].

L. Jonathan Cohen defines accepting that *p* as "to have or adopt a policy of deeming, positing, or postulating that p", and so of using it as a premise in some context, "whether or not one feels it to be true that p" [@cohen1992, 4; @bishop-mckaughan2023, § 8]. Chignell warns that acceptance is "typically a technical notion" and that "characterizations of its nature and ethics differ radically in the literature" [@chignell2018, § 7].

### Neighbouring attitudes

- **Supposition and imagination.** Some philosophers distinguish them by content: imaginings are richer and more sensory. Others distinguish them by function: imagination produces emotional responses, prompts pretence and meets "imaginative resistance" to morally repugnant scenarios, while supposition does not. Others again doubt that they differ in kind at all [@munro-etal2026, § 2.5]. Tamar Gendler's idea of "imaginative quarantining" sums up how imaginings, unlike beliefs, are kept from spreading into action and emotion [@munro-etal2026, § 2.1].
- **Faith.** "Non-doxastic" accounts of faith do not require belief: faith is a practical commitment, a venture that treats the hoped-for as real. William Alston suggested that faith may involve acceptance rather than belief [@bishop-mckaughan2023, § 8].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **The three doxastic attitudes.** That suspension of judgment is a genuine attitude, not merely the absence of belief, is a substantive view. So is the question of whether we can suspend at will; Booth argues that we cannot, for the same contingent reasons that we cannot believe at will [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 3.1].
- **"Belief" in the definition of knowledge.** Gettier's famous paper stated three versions of the justified-true-belief analysis: one with "believes", one with "accepts" and one with "is sure that". Epistemologists have generally treated these as equivalent [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 1.2; @gettier1963]. The distinction this chapter draws between belief and acceptance was not in view.

## The basic vocabulary

### Proposition

McGrath and Frank list four jobs the word "proposition" is asked to do: the primary bearers of truth and falsity, the objects of belief and other attitudes, the referents of that-clauses, and the meanings of sentences. They quote David Lewis's worry that the concept may be "a jumble of conflicting desiderata". Their stipulation is the one the chapter uses: propositions are "the sharable objects of the attitudes and the primary bearers of truth and falsity" [@mcgrath-frank2024, preamble]. One open question is whether propositions have structure, so that *a loves b* and *b loves a* are made of the same parts in a different order [@mcgrath-frank2024, § 8].

### Truth

Aristotle's formula, quoted in the chapter, is often treated as the founding statement of the correspondence theory. Marian David points out that it is a "muted, relatively minimal" version. It does not say what in reality makes a statement true, which is why it has also been claimed as a precursor of **deflationary** theories. Plato gives almost identical formulations (*Cratylus* 385b, *Sophist* 263b). Aristotle sounds more like a full correspondence theorist elsewhere, when he speaks of underlying things that make statements true [@david2025, § 1]. Michael Glanzberg adds that Tarski used the same Aristotelian slogan, and that without a metaphysics of facts the notion of correspondence "loses substance" [@glanzberg2025, § 3].

### Evidence

Philosophers have held very different views of what evidence is:

- Russell: sense-data.
- Quine: the stimulation of sensory receptors.
- The logical positivists: observation statements.
- Williamson: everything one knows ("E = K").
- Conee and Feldman: one's current mental states.

[@kelly2016, preamble; @williamson2000]. The choice matters. If evidence is knowledge, a body of evidence can never rule out a truth. If evidence is experiences or beliefs, it can [@kelly2016, § 3]. There is also a root idea: evidence is a reliable *sign* of what it is evidence for, as smoke is of fire, in Ian Hacking's phrase "the evidence of one thing that points beyond itself" [@kelly2016, § 3].

### Justification: internal and external

The chapter's definition, "held for good reasons, or formed in the right way", covers two camps.

- **Internalists** hold that justification depends only on factors internal to the believer. A common test: a brain in a vat with your experiences would be exactly as justified as you are [@steup-neta2025, § 3.3].
- **Externalists** deny this. They want from justification the kind of likelihood of truth that knowledge needs, and a deceived brain lacks it [@steup-neta2025, § 3.3].

"Internal" itself has two senses. *Accessibility* internalism requires that the justifiers be accessible to the believer on reflection. *Mentalist* internalism requires that they be mental states. Evidentialism is usually internalist, but Williamson's version is not, because on his view a brain in a vat does not share your evidence [@steup-neta2025, § 3.3].

### Certainty and fallibilism

Baron Reed separates psychological from epistemic certainty, as the chapter does. He adds that someone can have epistemic certainty without knowing that they have it: certainty need not "iterate" [@reed2022, § 1]. Russell defined the epistemic sense: a proposition is certain "when it has the highest degree of credibility" [@reed2022, § 2.3].

Fallibilism is the majority view, but it has a well-known awkwardness. **Concessive knowledge attributions** ("I know it's raining, though I could be wrong") sound odd. Lewis said that to speak of fallible knowledge "just sounds contradictory" [@rysiew2026, § 5.1]. Stephen Hetherington argues that the oddity does not show that knowledge must be infallible. Most epistemologists, he notes, accept that knowledge is usually, perhaps always, fallible [@hetherington-iep, §§ 8–9].

### Defeaters

John Pollock distinguished two kinds of defeater.

- A **rebutting** defeater is a reason to believe the conclusion is false.
- An **undercutting** defeater is a reason to doubt that the evidence supports the conclusion in this case.

Kelly gives an example. Your testimony that your name is Fritz is evidence that it is. Credible testimony that your name is really Leopold *rebuts* it. Evidence that you are a pathological liar *undercuts* it [@kelly2016, § 1]. Defeaters can themselves be defeated, which restores the original justification. "In principle, there is no limit to the complexity of the relations of defeat" [@kelly2016, § 1; @koons2025, § 1.1].

### Reasons

The threefold distinction in the chapter comes from the theory of practical reasons. It is now accepted by most writers on the topic. A **normative** reason is, in Scanlon's phrase, "a consideration that counts in favour of" something. A **motivating** reason is a reason for which someone acts. Recent authors distinguish motivating from **explanatory** reasons, though older usage ran them together [@alvarez-way2024, preamble, §§ 1, 3].

### Warrant

Plantinga uses "warrant" for whatever turns true belief into knowledge [@plantinga1993]. Peter Forrest notes the potential for confusion: elsewhere in philosophy "warrant" is a synonym for "justified" (as in "warranted assertibility") [@forrest2025, § 6]. Plantinga's own candidate is a proper-function condition: a belief has warrant when it is produced by cognitive faculties working properly in an appropriate environment. Linda Zagzebski argued that this condition, like any condition that does not guarantee truth, falls to a Gettier case [@ichikawa-steup2026, § 7].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"On the most common view, a proposition is true when things are as it says they are."** Stated this minimally, the view is one that rival theories can share: Aristotle's formula has been claimed as a precursor by deflationists as well as by correspondence theorists. The disputes concern what more, if anything, there is to say about truth [@david2025, § 1; @glanzberg2025, § 3].
- **The evidence slogan.** "*E* is evidence for *H* when *E* is more probable if *H* is true than if *H* is false" is one standard probabilistic gloss. It does not settle what *E* can be: an experience, a belief or a known fact [@kelly2016, preamble].

## Three great distinctions

> **In short.** The chapter introduces three pairs of words that sound alike but answer three different questions about a statement. *A priori / a posteriori* asks **how you could come to know it**: by thinking, or by looking at the world. *Analytic / synthetic* asks **what makes it true**: the meanings of its words alone, or also how the world is. *Necessary / contingent* asks **whether it could have been false**. For a long time philosophers assumed the three always give the same answer. The most interesting work in this area comes from the cases where they don't.

### Why this section is hard

Most people find this section hard for three reasons. The examples the chapter starts with ("All bachelors are unmarried", "7 + 5 = 12") come out the same way on all three tests, which hides how different the tests are. The words are Latin and Kantian, not everyday English. And each word is ordinary in other senses: "necessary" also means "required", and "a priori" is used loosely to mean "assumed in advance". It helps to keep one question attached to each pair:

- **A priori / a posteriori** asks *how someone could know it*. Quick test: could you know it in an armchair, once you understand the words, without checking anything?
- **Analytic / synthetic** asks *what makes it true*. Quick test: is it true just because of what the words mean?
- **Necessary / contingent** asks *whether it could have been otherwise*. Quick test: is there any way the world could have gone in which it is false?

The first is about **knowledge**, the second about **meaning**, the third about **reality and possibility**. Different questions, so in principle different answers.

### The idea, step by step

#### 1. A priori and a posteriori: how could you know it?

Try the **armchair test**. Sit with your eyes closed, understand the sentence, and ask whether you could know it is true without any further observation.

- "If Ana is taller than Ben and Ben is taller than Carl, then Ana is taller than Carl." You can know this in the armchair. **A priori.**
- "Ana is taller than Ben." You would have to look, or ask. **A posteriori.**

Two clarifications prevent most mistakes.

**Experience can be needed to understand a sentence without being needed to justify it.** You needed experience to learn the word "taller". But once you have it, nothing further has to be observed. Steup and Neta define a priori justification as justification that "does not depend on any experience", and note that on the strict use of the term, introspection and memory count as experience too: knowing that you are thirsty is not a priori [@steup-neta2025, § 5.4].

**Kant distinguished pure from impure a priori knowledge.** "Every change has a cause" is, for Kant, a priori but *impure*, because the concept of change can only be acquired from experience [@kant1787, Introduction, B 3]. The chapter's own example, "every event has a cause", belongs here. Hume disagreed sharply about such principles. He argued that no reasoning "*a priori*" could ever reveal what causes what: "A man must be very sagacious who could discover by reasoning that crystal is the effect of heat, and ice of cold" [@hume1748, IV.i.23–25].

#### 2. Analytic and synthetic: what makes it true?

Try the **dictionary test**: is the sentence true just because of what its words mean?

Kant's own pair of examples is the clearest. "All bodies are extended" is analytic: to find the predicate, I only have to unpack my concept of a body, which already includes extension, shape and impenetrability. "All bodies are heavy" is synthetic: weight is "something totally different from that which I think in the mere conception of a body", and I learn it by observation [@kant1787, Introduction, B 11–12].

Notice a trap here. Kant says analytic judgments are known a priori, "a necessity which I could never learn from experience" [@kant1787, Introduction, B 11–12]. So for Kant, analytic truths automatically pass the armchair test too. That is one reason the pairs get confused. The tests are different even when they agree.

The dictionary test is harder to apply than it looks. Georges Rey points to sentences that seem as "true by meaning" as any, but where nothing is obviously "contained" in anything: "If Bob is married to Sue, then Sue is married to Bob", or "If something is red, then it's colored". What does *red* contain besides *colored*? "It is hard to see what else to 'add' – except red itself!" [@rey2026, § 1.2]. Frege's response was to build modern symbolic logic and then count as analytic the truths that turn into truths of logic once definitions are substituted for the words they define. This replaced Kant's picture of concepts contained in concepts [@rey2026, § 1.2].

#### 3. Necessary and contingent: could it have been otherwise?

Try the **story test**: can you tell a consistent story about how the world might have gone in which the sentence is false?

- "Napoleon lost at Waterloo." Easy story: the Prussians arrive late. **Contingent.**
- "7 + 5 = 12." No story makes it false. **Necessary.**

Philosophers talk about this using **possible worlds**: a necessary truth is true in every possible world; a contingent truth is true in ours but false in some other.

The chapter's "necessary" means *metaphysically* necessary. There are several kinds of necessity, and confusing them causes trouble:

- **Logical or conceptual** necessity: denying it is a contradiction.
- **Metaphysical** necessity: it could not have been otherwise, whatever the laws of nature were.
- **Physical** (or nomological) necessity: it follows from the laws of nature, but the laws themselves might have been different.

Philosophers disagree about how these relate. Some think metaphysical and logical necessity coincide, others that metaphysical and physical necessity coincide, and some doubt that metaphysical necessity is a separate notion at all [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1].

**Necessity is not the "must" of "it must be raining".** That "must" is *epistemic*: it says what follows from what you know, and philosophers studying necessity usually set it aside, since it depends on the knower [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1].

#### 4. Why the three come apart

Here is the chapter's table again, cell by cell, with the reason each case is placed where it is.

**Necessary and a priori ("7 + 5 = 12").** The traditional case. Kant's twist is that it is *synthetic*. However long you analyse "the sum of seven and five", the concept *twelve* is not in it; to get there you must count, for instance on your fingers, adding units one at a time [@kant1787, Introduction, B 15–16; @shabel2025, § 2.2]. He says the same about geometry: "A straight line between two points is the shortest" is synthetic, because *straight* is a quality and *shortest* is a quantity [@kant1787, Introduction, B 16]. If Kant is right, there is **synthetic a priori** knowledge: substantial truths about the world, knowable by reason. Empiricists deny this is possible. Rationalists offer further candidates, such as "If a ball is green all over, then it doesn't have black spots" [@steup-neta2025, § 5.4].

**Necessary but a posteriori ("Water is H₂O").** How can something be necessary yet only discoverable by experiment? The standard answer has two steps. First, the word "water" is introduced to refer to *the stuff, whatever it is,* that fills the lakes, falls as rain and quenches thirst. That much we know by understanding the word. Second, chemistry discovers what that stuff essentially is. The meaning is knowable a priori, "the essence of water must be discovered empirically" [@russell-bruce2024, § 2]. Since the essence could not have been different, the truth is necessary; since it had to be discovered, it is a posteriori. Kripke made the pattern explicit: we know a priori that *if* water is H₂O, it is necessarily H₂O, and we learn by experiment that it *is* [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 2.3; @kripke1980].

There is a further way to see why the case feels puzzling. In one sense it is conceivable that water is not H₂O: before chemistry, it *might have turned out* that the watery stuff was something else. In another sense it is not: given that the watery stuff is H₂O, there is no possible world in which *water* is something else. David Chalmers calls these **primary** and **secondary** conceivability, and builds his "two-dimensional" semantics on the difference [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.1; @schroeter2021, preamble].

**Contingent but a priori ("Stick S is one metre long at t₀").** Suppose "one metre" is *defined* as the length of stick S at time t₀. Then you know without measuring that S is one metre long at t₀. But the stick could have been longer, if it had been heated, so the truth is contingent [@russell-bruce2024, § 2]. This case is more controversial than the water case. Many deny that it is genuinely a priori, and even those who accept it find it of little consequence [@laporte2026, § 3.2].

**Contingent and a posteriori ("Paris is the capital of France").** The ordinary case: discovered by looking, and could have been otherwise.

### Common confusions

- **"A priori" means "innate" or "known before birth".** No. Russell made the point in 1912: knowledge that is *logically* independent of experience is still "elicited and caused by experience". It would be absurd to think "babies are born with a knowledge of everything which men know". That is why philosophers stopped calling such knowledge "innate" and said "*a priori*" instead [@russell1912, ch. VII].
- **"A priori" means certain, or can never be revised.** No. Traditionally, a priori meant *justified independently of experience*. Rey notes that Quine and the positivists before him "casually" changed this to *unrevisable in the light of experience*, which would make anyone who calls something a priori claim to be infallible about it [@rey2026, § 3.6.1]. You can make a mistake in an a priori subject; a long sum can be added wrongly.
- **Analytic means obvious, and synthetic means surprising.** No. Some analyses are long and informative; that they can be informative at all is the old "paradox of analysis" [@rey2026, § 3.1]. And some synthetic claims are as safe as anything: "There have been black dogs" is not analytic, but no one would give it up [@rey2026, § 3.6.1].
- **Necessary means "certain", and contingent means "uncertain" or "random".** No. These are claims about the world, not about our confidence. You are certain that Paris is the capital of France, yet it is contingent. A necessary mathematical truth can be unproven and unknown to everyone.
- **"True by definition" ends an argument.** Only about words. A definition can make "All bachelors are unmarried" true. It cannot make any claim about the world true; it can only decide what a word will mean. When someone says a contested claim is "true by definition", ask whether they are defining a word or smuggling in a claim. The chapter's link to [persuasive definition](04-language-concepts-and-definitions.md#kinds-of-definition) is the place to follow this up.

### What the chapter leaves out

- **How we know what is necessary.** The chapter tells you which truths are necessary, but not how anyone could know that a truth holds in *every* possible world. The main proposal is that **conceivability** guides us: if you can coherently conceive a situation, that is evidence that it is possible. The central problem is explaining how a fact about our minds can tell us about mind-independent possibility [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.1].
- **The kinds of necessity** described above, and the disputes about whether "metaphysical" necessity is a separate kind at all [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 1].
- **Frege's revision of "analytic"** in terms of logic and definitions, and the hopes, centred on Frege's program, of reducing mathematics to logic. That program was damaged by Russell's paradox [@rey2026, §§ 1.2, 3.2].
- **The reply to Quine.** Grice and Strawson (1956), and later Putnam, argued that a distinction which ordinary speakers draw so readily and agree about so widely, "Lawyers are attorneys", "A fortnight is a period of fourteen days", is unlikely to have "no basis at all in fact" [@rey2026, § 3.5]. Since then, the debate has been mostly about the epistemology of analyticity: how we justify beliefs in necessary truths [@rey2026, § 2].
- **Non-Euclidean geometry and Kant.** It is often said that the discovery of non-Euclidean geometries refuted Kant's view that geometry is synthetic a priori. Contemporary Kant scholars broadly oppose that "long-standard story" [@shabel2025, § 3.1]. Rey adds that the usual example misreads the history: non-Euclidean geometries were developed as pure mathematics in the 19th century, and Einstein argued that physics supported one of them [@rey2026, § 3.4].

### Where philosophers disagree

- **What a priori justification is.** Bruce Russell lists three views: that it is not very different from empirical justification; that it rests on rational intuition or insight; and that it is an entitlement to believe without evidence [@russell-bruce2024, § 3]. Timothy Williamson argues that much knowledge, such as knowledge of counterfactuals, is "armchair" knowledge that fits neither side, and that the distinction "doesn't cut at the epistemic joints" [@mallozzi-etal2024, § 4.2].
- **Whether the analytic/synthetic distinction survives Quine.** Quine argued that the notions of analyticity, synonymy and meaning only explain one another, "bringing in each other's laundry" in Rey's phrase. He also argued that "our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually, but only as a corporate body" [@quine1951; @rey2026, §§ 3.4–3.5]. Rey points out that Quine offered no argument for this holism, and that mathematics has virtually never been revised on empirical grounds [@rey2026, § 3.4].
- **Whether Kripke's examples work.** Some philosophers (Salmon, Soames) argue that the *proposition* that Hesperus is Phosphorus is the same as the proposition that Hesperus is Hesperus, and so is knowable a priori; only the *sentence* is learned a posteriori. LaPorte calls the usual claim "controversial, though common" [@laporte2026, § 3.1]. Others hold that "Water is H₂O" is contingent, and that the necessary truth is the more general one that a kind of matter's essence is its composition [@russell-bruce2024, § 2].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Kant introduced the distinction."** Kant gave it its modern form, but it has precursors in Leibniz, and in Locke's and Hume's "relations of ideas" [@rey2026, § 1.1]. Hume's fork divides the objects of reason into **relations of ideas**, "discoverable by the mere operation of thought", and **matters of fact** [@hume1748, IV.i.20–21]. It leaves no room for the synthetic a priori.
- **The table.** Placing "Water is H₂O" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus" in the necessary a posteriori cell is standard, but contested as noted above. The contingent a priori cell is more contested still [@laporte2026, §§ 3.1–3.2].

## Epistemic reasons versus practical reasons

### Can we believe at will?

The billionaire case relies on **doxastic involuntarism**, the view that we cannot believe at will. There are two kinds of argument for it [@boespflug-jackson2024, §§ 3.1–3.2]:

- **Psychological.** William Alston simply asks the reader to try: "Can you, at this moment, start to believe that the U.S. is still a colony of Great Britain, just by deciding to do so". His target was the deontological conception of justification, which, if "ought implies can", needs beliefs to be under our control [@alston1988; @boespflug-jackson2024, § 3.1].
- **Conceptual.** Bernard Williams argued that belief at will is *impossible*, not just difficult. Believing at will would mean believing *p* regardless of its truth, and knowing that you had done so. You would then take yourself to believe something you regard as unconnected to the truth, which conflicts with belief's aim at truth [@williams1973, ch. 9; @boespflug-jackson2024, § 3.2].

Williams's argument is widely thought to fail as it stands:

- Barbara Winters pointed out that one could have the ability without knowing it.
- Jonathan Bennett imagined the "Credamites", who can will themselves to believe but immediately forget that they did so [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 3.2].
- More recently, Rik Peels and Andrew Reisner have described cases in which the truth depends on the belief. A perfectly reliable mind-reader will pay you $10 if, and only if, you come to believe that you will be paid. In such a case the practical reason and the evidence point the same way [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 4.4].

The idea that belief has a constitutive aim or norm of truth underlies most of these arguments, and Nishi Shah's "How Truth Governs Belief" is one of its central statements [@shah2003; @boespflug-jackson2024, § 3.2].

### Pascal

Alan Hájek notes that the name "Pascal's Wager" is misleading. In a single section of the *Pensées* Pascal presents four arguments, and only the third is traditionally called "the Wager" [@hajek2024, preamble]. The argument concludes that we *ought to believe* in God, not that God exists. Its reasons are prudential, not evidential [@hajek2024, § 1]. What is new is its decision-theoretic form. Ian Hacking called it "the first well-understood contribution to decision theory" [@hajek2024, § 1]. Similar pragmatic arguments occur in al-Ghazālī and in the Buddhist discourse to the Kālāmas [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 7].

Pascal saw the voluntarism problem himself. To someone who cannot simply decide to believe, he recommends acting as believers do, "taking the holy water, having masses said", so that belief may follow [@pascal1670, fr. 233; @boespflug-jackson2024, § 7]. Boespflug and Jackson note that pragmatic arguments can be recast as reasons to make a commitment, or to *accept* that God exists, which avoids the voluntarism problem [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 7].

A well-known objection concerns infinite utilities. Any strategy that gives you some finite positive probability of ending up a believer, even having a hamburger instead, has infinite expected utility too. So the Wager fails to single out wagering for God [@hajek2024, § 5.4].

### Clifford and James

The chapter discusses the Clifford–James debate in Chapter 13, but it belongs here as well.

**Clifford.** Clifford's shipowner silences his doubts about an old ship, and it sinks with all aboard. Clifford judges him "verily guilty", and holds that he would be just as guilty if the ship had arrived safely: he "had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him" [@chignell2018, § 1.1]. The principle reads: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" [@clifford1879, 186]. Clifford adds a duty over time. Whoever avoids "the reading of books and the company of men that call in question" a cherished belief lives a life that "is one long sin against mankind" [@clifford1879, 186–87]. Chignell notes that the later parts of the essay give a more moderate account of what makes evidence "sufficient" [@chignell2018, § 1.1].

**James.** James's reply turns on a carefully restricted kind of choice. He calls an option **genuine** when it is:

- **living**: both hypotheses make some appeal to you;
- **forced**: there is no third way out;
- **momentous**: something important and unrepeatable is at stake.

[@james1897, 3–4]. His thesis: "Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds". He adds that refusing to decide "is itself a passional decision" [@james1897, 11]. James did not take Pascal's side. A belief in masses and holy water adopted after "such a mechanical calculation" would lack "the inner soul of faith's reality". And for anyone without a prior tendency to believe, the option Pascal offers is not a living one [@james1897, 6].

Chignell groups the pragmatist line of Pascal and James together with Kant's moral grounds for faith (*Glaube*) under the label "practical non-evidentialism" [@chignell2018, § 6.1].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"You probably *cannot* believe it at will."** Probably not. But whether this is a psychological fact or a conceptual truth is disputed, and there are contrived cases in which believing at will looks possible and even rational [@boespflug-jackson2024, §§ 3, 4.4].
- **"Practical reasons for belief."** Many defenders of pragmatic arguments recast them as reasons for *acceptance*, commitment or action, not belief, which links this section to "Belief, credence, and acceptance" above [@boespflug-jackson2024, § 7; @chignell2018, § 7].

## Epistemology is normative

### Kim's challenge and Quine's reply

The clearest statement of the claim that epistemology is essentially normative came as an objection to Quine. In "Epistemology Naturalized" Quine proposed that epistemology "falls into place as a chapter of psychology", one that studies how "meager input" produces a "torrential output" of theory [@rysiew2021, § 2]. Jaegwon Kim objected that this abandons justification, and with it epistemology: "For epistemology to go out of the business of justification is for it to go out of business" [@kim1988, 391; @rysiew2021, § 3.1]. Kim also wrote: "A strictly nonnormative concept of evidence is not our concept of evidence; it is something that we do not understand" [@kelly2016, § 1].

In later work Quine denied that he had dropped normativity: "The normative is naturalized, not dropped". For him normative epistemology became "a branch of engineering", "the technology of truth-seeking" [@rysiew2021, § 3.2]. So the question is not *whether* epistemology is normative. It is where its norms come from, and whether empirical science can supply them.

### Deontology and its rivals

The chapter's table pairs "deontology" with "deontological justification". Steup and Neta set out the two standard objections to that view:

- **Control.** It presupposes control over our beliefs that we lack. This is Alston's argument above.
- **Insufficiency.** Members of an isolated culture, or people with cognitive deficits, might violate no obligation and still hold beliefs that are not justified.

[@steup-neta2025, § 3.1; @alston1988]. The rival conception defines justification as believing "in a way that makes it sufficiently likely that [the] belief is true" [@steup-neta2025, § 3.1].

**Epistemic consequentialism** is the view that a way of forming beliefs is permissible insofar as it promotes true belief and the avoidance of false belief, or, for credences, overall accuracy. Selim Berker, developing an argument of Roderick Firth's, argues at length that it is false [@steup-neta2025, § 1.2; @berker2013]. A related debate concerns **permissivism**: whether the same evidence can permit more than one rational response [@steup-neta2025, § 1.2].

### The aim of belief

"Veritism" is the view that truth is the fundamental epistemic good. It is one form of **value monism**. Other candidates for the aim of belief are knowledge, justification, virtue and understanding [@chignell2018, §§ 2.4, 3.2]. Chignell notes a limit to the parallel with ethics: a belief is usually assessed at the moment it is formed, while an action's rightness may depend on consequences known only later [@chignell2018, § 3.2].

The value problem goes back to Plato's *Meno*: why is knowledge more valuable than true belief? If a reliable process is valuable only because it produces true beliefs, then the value of the true belief "swamps" the value of how it was produced. This is the **swamping problem** [@pritchard-etal2025, preamble, § 2]. The chapter's example of counting grains of sand has a counterpart in this literature. No rational person is curious about "every grain of sand on a given beach". So some argue only that true belief is *pro tanto* good, not that every truth is worth having [@pritchard-etal2025, § 8].

### James's two commandments, made precise

James's passage reads: "*We must know the truth*; and *we must avoid error*". These, he says, "are not two ways of stating an identical commandment, they are two separable laws" [@james1897, 17–18]. He thought Clifford gave the second absolute priority, and he could not "go with Clifford" [@james1897, 18].

Recent epistemology has turned this into a theorem. Assign a value *R* to believing a truth and a cost *W* to believing a falsehood. Then believing a proposition maximizes expected epistemic value exactly when your credence in it exceeds *W* / (*R* + *W*) [@pettigrew2024, § 4.2]. Richard Pettigrew calls the assumption "Jamesian" because it lets the two commandments be weighted differently. The result yields the Lockean thesis, with a threshold set by those weights:

- If error is three times as bad as truth is good (*W* = 3*R*), believe when your credence exceeds 0.75.
- If the two are equal (*W* = *R*), believe when it exceeds 0.5.

[@pettigrew2024, § 4.2]. The chapter's claim that "every epistemic standard is a trade-off" is, on this model, literally true: the threshold for belief *is* the exchange rate between the two commandments.

### What the chapter simplifies

- **"Consequentialism ↔ Veritism."** Veritism is a view about what is valuable. Consequentialism is a view about how what is valuable determines what is permissible. One can hold either without the other, and Berker's objection targets the second [@steup-neta2025, § 1.2; @chignell2018, § 2.4].
- **The trade-off.** The Jamesian model shows the trade-off for outright belief. For credences, accuracy-based theories score how high your confidence is in truths and how low it is in falsehoods, so the trade-off takes a graded form [@steup-neta2025, § 1.2].

## The map of the field

### Other ways to draw the map

Any map of the field reflects choices. Two examples:

- **Formal and informal.** It is tempting to identify formal epistemology with the study of credences and informal epistemology with the study of belief. Scott Sturgeon proposes a two-by-two grid instead: formal and informal methods, crossed with coarse-grained belief and fine-grained confidence, gives four kinds of project. Lehrer's informal work on comparative confidence and Pollock's formal work on belief show that the off-diagonal cells are occupied [@carter-sosa2026, § 2.2].
- **Formal epistemology's own scope.** Its questions are often those of traditional epistemology, but its tools also take it into decision-making and the meaning of conditionals [@weisberg2021, preamble].

### Branches in more detail

- **Social epistemology.** It began as a correction to epistemology's individualism [@oconnor-etal2026, preamble]. Alvin Goldman's *Knowledge in a Social World* (1999) set the agenda for its "veritistic" wing, which assesses social practices by how reliably they produce true beliefs [@goldman1999; @oconnor-etal2026, § 2]. Other strands include testimony, the social structure of science (Kitcher, Longino, Solomon) and Margaret Gilbert's "plural subjects", which ground the epistemology of groups [@oconnor-etal2026, § 2].
- **Epistemic injustice.** Miranda Fricker distinguished two kinds [@fricker2007; @oconnor-etal2026, § 5.3]:
  - **Testimonial** injustice: prejudice deflates a speaker's credibility. Her example is the jury's treatment of Tom Robinson in *To Kill a Mockingbird*.
  - **Hermeneutical** injustice: a group lacks the concepts to make sense of its own experience. Her example is sexual harassment before the term existed.
- **Virtue epistemology.** It divides into **virtue reliabilists** (Sosa, Greco, Goldman), for whom virtues include faculties such as perception and memory, and **virtue responsibilists** (Zagzebski, Code, Montmarquet, Battaly), for whom they are cultivated character traits. Some critics argue that a complete epistemology needs both [@turri-etal2021, § 3]. On Sosa's influential account, knowledge is "apt" belief: belief that is true *because* it manifests competence, as an archer's shot hits because it is skilful [@turri-etal2021, § 5].
- **Feminist epistemology.** Its central concept is **situated knowledge**, knowledge that reflects the particular perspective of the knower. Its three main approaches, standpoint theory, postmodernism and feminist empiricism, have converged over time [@anderson2024, preamble, § 1].
- **Moral and religious epistemology.** Moral epistemology must explain how moral knowledge is possible, given deep moral disagreement and the motivating force of moral judgment [@campbell2024, preamble]. In the epistemology of religion, "reformed epistemology" can be seen as widening what counts as evidence. Alston, for example, argued that some religious experience can be assimilated to perception [@forrest2025, § 7].

### Beyond the European tradition

The chapter's map is drawn from contemporary Anglophone philosophy. Two other traditions have their own developed theories of knowledge.

**Classical Indian philosophy.** Theory of knowledge, *pramāṇa-śāstra*, is a genre of Sanskrit literature spanning almost twenty centuries, and every major school takes a position in it [@phillips-vaidya2026, preamble]. Its debates parallel some in this chapter:

- **Self-certification.** Nāgārjuna argued that if every knowledge source needed certifying by another, there would be an infinite regress. Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta philosophers replied that knowledge is self-certifying (*svataḥ prāmāṇya*) [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 3].
- **Testimony.** Nyāya treats testimony as a distinct source of knowledge. Vaiśeṣika and Yogācāra reduce it to inference from the speaker's trustworthiness, a close analogue of the reductionist debate described above [@phillips-vaidya2026, § 6].

**Chinese philosophy.** Knowledge has traditionally been understood more broadly than in European epistemology, as stemming also, or primarily, from moral training. The study of knowing has been bound up with the study of being [@rosker2025, preamble, § 2.3].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **Separate branches.** They overlap more than a tree diagram suggests: feminist epistemology "is thus a branch of social epistemology" [@anderson2024, § 1]. Formal methods are used on questions from every branch [@weisberg2021, preamble].
- **"Naturalized epistemology: psychology."** Quine's original proposal named psychology, but in later writings he counted other sciences as relevant too [@rysiew2021, § 2].
- **Non-Western epistemology.** The map omits it. The chapter mentions the Islamic tradition, and the guide returns to knowledge by presence in Chapter 7.

## How the pieces connect: one news story, twelve questions

### The chain of testimony, measured

The chapter's second question, *Who is telling me?*, describes a chain: a journalist summarizing a press release summarizing a paper. Steup and Neta count "articles by journalists" and broadcast information as testimony in the epistemologist's sense [@steup-neta2025, § 5.5]. One philosophical question is whether testimony can only *transmit* knowledge or can sometimes *generate* it. Can a hearer come to know something from a speaker who does not know it? [@leonard2023, § 2].

Empirical research has measured how much such chains distort.

**Sumner and colleagues (2014)** studied 462 press releases on health-related research issued by twenty leading UK universities in 2011, together with the papers and 668 news stories based on them [@sumner2014, abstract]:

- Between a third and two-fifths of the press releases already contained an exaggeration. 40% gave exaggerated advice, 33% made exaggerated causal claims from correlational results, and 36% made exaggerated inferences from animal research to humans.
- When a press release exaggerated, most of the related news did too: 58%, 81% and 86% in the three categories. When the press release did not exaggerate, the news exaggerated at rates of 17%, 18% and 10%.
- There was "little evidence that exaggeration in press releases increased the uptake of news".

**Schwartz and colleagues (2012)** studied press releases from medical journals. When a journal's press release quantified the main result with absolute risks, 53% of newspaper stories did so. When it did not, only 9% did [@schwartz2012, abstract]. The same pattern held for mentioning harms and limitations. They concluded that high-quality press releases "seem to make the quality of associated newspaper stories better" [@schwartz2012, abstract].

Both studies are observational: Sumner's is a retrospective content analysis, Schwartz's a retrospective cohort. They show strong *associations*, and their authors word their causal conclusions cautiously. That is question 4 of the chapter's list applied to the evidence about question 2.

### Why a single study should move you little

Questions 6, 8 and 10 rest on an argument made famous by John Ioannidis. The probability that a claimed finding is true, its **positive predictive value**, depends on three things:

- **R**, the field's ratio of true to false relationships among those tested;
- **1 − β**, the study's statistical power;
- **α**, the significance threshold.

In the simplest case, without bias, PPV = (1 − β)*R* / (*R* − β*R* + α), and a finding is more likely true than false only if (1 − β)*R* > α [@ioannidis2005, “Modeling the Framework”]. Ioannidis drew corollaries from the model:

- Findings are less likely to be true when studies are small.
- They are less likely to be true when designs, definitions and analyses are flexible.
- They are less likely to be true in "hot" fields with many competing teams.
- They are less likely to be true where financial and other interests are strong.

[@ioannidis2005, corollaries 1–6]. Flexible analysis includes what is now called "p-hacking": dropping outliers, reporting selectively, or restricting the analysis to a subgroup. Reiss and Sprenger note that such practices push the real rate of false positives well above its nominal 5% [@reiss-sprenger2020, § 4.2.2].

### Expertise and disagreement

Questions 6 and 7 involve evidence about evidence. Learning that a peer or an expert disagrees with you is **higher-order evidence** about your own first-order belief. How it should combine with first-order evidence is an open question [@chignell2018, § 4.3]. Kelly adds that evidence is not a perfectly neutral arbiter. Its bearing on a hypothesis depends on background theory, so people with different background beliefs can rationally read the same evidence differently [@kelly2016, § 4].

### What the chapter simplifies

- **The individual reader.** The twelve questions are addressed to one reader evaluating one story. The research above suggests that some of the most effective improvements are institutional. Sumner and colleagues conclude that "improving the accuracy of academic press releases could represent a key opportunity for reducing misleading health related news" [@sumner2014, abstract]. Social epistemology studies exactly these systems [@oconnor-etal2026, preamble].
- **"It can be proven that most claimed research findings are false."** That is Ioannidis's own wording [@ioannidis2005, introduction]. What the model proves is conditional: given the assumed values of *R*, power and bias, the PPV follows. Whether "most" findings are false in a particular field depends on whether those values hold there.
- **A checklist, not a calculation.** The questions do not come with weights. Combining their answers into a single degree of confidence is the job of the probabilistic tools introduced in Chapter 9.
