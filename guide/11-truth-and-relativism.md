> "To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not, is true."
> — Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Γ 7, 1011b25

> "The ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but people for whom the distinction between fact and fiction (i.e., the reality of experience) and the distinction between true and false (i.e., the standards of thought) no longer exist."
> — Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951)

Truth is the goal of belief. It is what we aim at when we inquire, what we promise when we assert, and what we lose when we are deceived. But what *is* truth? Is it correspondence with reality? Coherence among beliefs? Usefulness? Or is "true" just a device for agreement, with nothing substantial to say about it?

These questions might seem abstract, but claims about truth come up constantly in public argument: "That's true for you, but not for me." "There's no such thing as objective truth." "Everyone has their own truth." "We live in a post-truth era." A critical thinker needs to be able to say what such claims mean, whether they are coherent, and what grain of insight they may contain.

## Why truth matters

Bernard Williams (*Truth and Truthfulness*, 2002) identified two basic **virtues of truth**:

- **Accuracy**: taking care to get things right, doing the work needed to find out.
- **Sincerity**: saying what you actually believe.

A society in which these virtues decay cannot function. Trade, science, law, medicine, friendship, and democratic self-government all depend on people generally trying to find the truth and generally telling it. Williams also noted a tension in modern culture: an intense demand for truthfulness (we want to see through deception and expose hidden agendas) combined with suspicion of truth itself (the idea that "truth" is just what the powerful say). If the suspicion wins, the demand for truthfulness has nothing left to demand.

Harry Frankfurt (*On Truth*, 2006) made a simpler point: people who say they don't care about truth rely on it every time they cross a road, take a medicine, or check their bank balance.

## Truth-bearers

What kinds of things are true or false? Candidates:

- **Sentences** ("Snow is white"). But the same sentence can be true in one context and false in another ("I am hungry"), and sentences in different languages can say the same thing.
- **Statements** or **utterances**: sentences as used on particular occasions.
- **Propositions**: the contents expressed by sentences, what is said. "Snow is white" and "La neige est blanche" express the same proposition. Most philosophers treat propositions as the primary truth-bearers.
- **Beliefs**: true when their content is true.

This matters for the relativist slogan "true for me." A proposition like "I am hungry" is not true for me and false for you. The sentence expresses *different* propositions when we each say it ("Ali is hungry," "Sara is hungry"), and each proposition is simply true or false. Much apparent relativity dissolves once we specify the proposition fully.

## Theories of truth

### The correspondence theory

The oldest and most intuitive theory: **a proposition is true if and only if it corresponds to the facts**, to the way the world is.

Aristotle's definition (quoted above) is often read this way. Thomas Aquinas defined truth as *adaequatio rei et intellectus*, the "adequation" (conformity) of thing and intellect. In the 20th century, Bertrand Russell and the early Wittgenstein developed detailed versions. Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* (1921) held that a proposition is a **picture** of a possible state of affairs, true when its elements are arranged as the objects in the world are.

**Objections.**
- **What is correspondence?** What relation holds between a sentence (or a thought) and a fact? A picture resembles what it depicts, but "The cat is on the mat" doesn't resemble a cat on a mat.
- **What are facts?** If facts are just "true propositions," the theory is circular. And what facts correspond to negative truths ("There is no elephant in this room"), general truths ("All ravens are black"), or truths about the future, about possibility, or about morality?
- **Access.** We can never step outside our beliefs to compare them with the facts. All we can ever compare a belief with is another belief or experience. This objection led some to the coherence theory.

Defenders reply that we don't need to step outside our beliefs to *have* true beliefs, only to *check* them in some absolute way, and nobody needs that. **Truthmaker theory** (David Armstrong, *Truth and Truthmakers*, 2004) develops a modern version: every truth is made true by something in the world.

### The coherence theory

**A proposition is true if and only if it coheres with a system of propositions.** Idealists such as F. H. Bradley and Brand Blanshard (*The Nature of Thought*, 1939) held versions of this view, as did some logical positivists (Otto Neurath).

The motivation is the access objection above: since we can only compare beliefs with beliefs, truth must be a relation among beliefs.

**Objections.**
- **Which system?** A consistent fairy tale is a coherent system. So is a consistent set of false beliefs. Many incompatible systems can each be coherent. If truth is coherence with *a* system, contradictory propositions could both be true. Defenders specify "the" system, perhaps the one that would be accepted by an ideally complete inquiry, but that moves toward the pragmatist view.
- **The confusion of truth and justification.** Coherence is a plausible theory of **justification** (see [Coherentism](06-justification.md#coherentism)). But justification and truth come apart: a belief can be justified and false. The coherence theory of truth seems to confuse the test of truth with its nature.

### Pragmatist theories

The pragmatists connected truth to inquiry and practice.

**Charles Sanders Peirce** ("How to Make Our Ideas Clear," 1878): "The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real." Truth is the **end of inquiry**: what inquirers would agree on if inquiry were pursued indefinitely.

**William James** (*Pragmatism*, 1907) was more provocative: "The true is only the expedient in our way of thinking." True ideas are those we can "assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify." Critics (including Russell and G. E. Moore) accused James of saying that whatever is useful to believe is true, which would make "Santa Claus exists" true for children. James protested that he meant usefulness *in the long run and on the whole*, including usefulness in dealing with reality, but the charge stuck.

**John Dewey** preferred to talk of **warranted assertibility** rather than truth: what inquiry, properly conducted, entitles us to assert.

**Later pragmatists**: Hilary Putnam's "internal realism" (*Reason, Truth and History*, 1981) identified truth with **idealized rational acceptability**: what would be justified under epistemically ideal conditions. Richard Rorty (*Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, 1979; *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*, 1989) went further, suggesting that "truth" is merely a compliment we pay to beliefs that work for us, and that we should stop trying to have a theory of it at all.

**Objections.** It seems possible for there to be truths that no inquiry could ever discover: the exact number of hairs on Julius Caesar's head on the morning of the Ides of March. The pragmatist theory seems to imply that such propositions are neither true nor false. And if truth is what inquirers would agree on, whose inquiry, and under what conditions? Specifying "ideal conditions" without appealing to truth is difficult.

The lasting pragmatist insight is Peirce's link between truth and **inquiry**: the point of the concept of truth is to mark what we are trying to get right, and what could show us to be wrong. See also [Peirce, James, and Dewey](02-history-of-epistemology.md#peirce-james-and-dewey-pragmatism).

### Tarski's semantic theory

The Polish logician Alfred Tarski ("The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages," 1933; "The Semantic Conception of Truth," 1944) gave the first mathematically precise definition of truth, for formal languages. Its core is the **T-schema** (or **Convention T**):

> "S" is true if and only if S.
>
> *"Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white.*

Any adequate definition of truth for a language must imply every instance of this schema. Tarski showed how to define truth for a formal language recursively, in terms of a relation he called **satisfaction** (roughly, of predicates by objects). To avoid the [liar paradox](#the-liar-paradox), he required that truth for a language be defined in a **metalanguage**, not in the language itself.

Tarski's theory is sometimes seen as a precise version of the correspondence theory, and sometimes as a precursor of deflationism.

### Deflationism

**Deflationary** theories hold that there is nothing substantial to say about the *nature* of truth. The T-schema says almost everything there is to say.

- **The redundancy theory** (Frank Ramsey, "Facts and Propositions," 1927): "It is true that Caesar was murdered" means no more than "Caesar was murdered." Adding "it is true that" adds nothing.
- **The disquotational theory** (W. V. O. Quine): "true" is a device for **disquotation**, for moving from talking about a sentence to talking about the world. Saying "'Snow is white' is true" is a roundabout way of saying snow is white.
- **Minimalism** (Paul Horwich, *Truth*, 1990): the whole theory of truth consists of the infinitely many instances of the schema "The proposition that p is true if and only if p."

Why have the word "true" at all, if it adds nothing? Because it allows **generalization** and **blind endorsement**. You cannot say "Everything the Pope says is so" without a truth predicate, since you don't know what all the Pope's statements are. "Everything the Pope says is true" does the job. Likewise: "What she said was true," "Most of the book is true," "Always tell the truth."

**Objections.** Critics argue that deflationism cannot explain why truth is the aim of belief and assertion, or why true beliefs tend to help us achieve our goals, since those explanations seem to need a substantive connection between truth and the world. Deflationists reply that the explanations can be given in terms of the T-schema instances ("If you believe the bridge will hold, and it will hold, then you'll get across").

### Truth pluralism

**Alethic pluralism** (Crispin Wright, *Truth and Objectivity*, 1992; Michael Lynch, *Truth as One and Many*, 2009) proposes that truth may be realized differently in different domains. In the domain of physical objects, truth may consist in correspondence; in mathematics, in coherence with axioms or provability; in morality or comedy, in something like superwarranted assertibility (what would remain warranted however inquiry proceeds). What all these have in common is that they play the same **functional role**: truth is what we aim at in belief, what is preserved by valid inference, what is objective in the sense that believing doesn't make it so.

### Comparing the theories

| Theory | Truth is... | Main strength | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Correspondence** | Agreement with facts | Captures the core intuition: truth depends on the world | What are correspondence and facts? |
| **Coherence** | Coherence with a system of beliefs | We can only compare beliefs with beliefs | Many coherent systems; confuses truth with justification |
| **Pragmatist** | What inquiry would ultimately converge on; what works | Links truth to inquiry and practice | Undiscoverable truths; usefulness isn't truth |
| **Semantic (Tarski)** | Defined recursively via satisfaction, meeting Convention T | Precise; avoids paradox | Only for formal languages; is it a theory of *what truth is*? |
| **Deflationary** | Nothing substantial: a device of disquotation and generalization | Simple; explains the uses of "true" | May not explain truth's role in success and inquiry |
| **Pluralist** | Different properties in different domains, playing one role | Handles mathematics, morals, and physics differently | Is it really one concept? |

> **What matters for critical thinking.** You don't need to settle which theory is right to use the concept well. Almost every theory accepts the **T-schema**: "p" is true if and only if p. This alone refutes the most common relativist slogans. If "the Earth is round" is true if and only if the Earth is round, then its truth depends on the shape of the Earth, not on who believes it. Believing doesn't make it so.

## The liar paradox

The most famous paradox about truth is attributed to the ancient philosopher **Eubulides of Miletus** (4th century BCE). A version is also associated with **Epimenides** the Cretan, who is said to have claimed that "all Cretans are liars." The strengthened form is:

> **(L)** This sentence is false.

If L is true, then what it says is the case, so it is false. If L is false, then what it says is not the case, so it is not false, so it is true. L is true if and only if it is false. Contradiction.

The liar paradox is not a trick. It shows that the T-schema, which almost every theory of truth accepts, leads to contradiction when applied to a language that can talk about its own sentences. Responses include:

- **Tarski's hierarchy**: a language cannot contain its own truth predicate. Truth for language L₀ is defined in a metalanguage L₁, truth for L₁ in L₂, and so on. L is not a well-formed sentence. Objection: natural languages do seem to contain their own truth predicates.
- **Truth-value gaps** (Saul Kripke, "Outline of a Theory of Truth," 1975): some sentences, including L, are neither true nor false; they are "ungrounded." Objection: the **strengthened liar**: "This sentence is not true." If it is neither true nor false, then it is not true, which is what it says, so it seems to be true.
- **Dialetheism** (Graham Priest, *In Contradiction*, 1987): some contradictions are true. L is both true and false. This requires a **paraconsistent logic**, in which a contradiction does not imply everything (in classical logic, from a contradiction you can derive any statement whatsoever, a principle called *ex falso quodlibet*).
- **Contextualist** and **revision theories** (Anil Gupta and Nuel Belnap, *The Revision Theory of Truth*, 1993).

No solution commands general agreement. Related paradoxes, such as **Russell's paradox** in set theory, **Curry's paradox**, and **Grelling's paradox** (is "heterological," meaning "not true of itself," heterological?), led to some of the deepest results of 20th-century logic, including Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) and Tarski's theorem on the undefinability of truth.

## Realism and anti-realism

Theories of truth connect to a larger debate about **realism**.

**Realism** about a domain (the external world, the past, mathematics, morality) holds that statements in that domain are true or false independently of our ability to know which. There is a fact of the matter about the number of hairs on Caesar's head, whether or not anyone can find out.

**Anti-realism**, in Michael Dummett's influential version ("Truth," 1959; *Truth and Other Enigmas*, 1978), holds that the meaning of a statement is given by the conditions under which we could **recognize** it as true (its assertibility conditions), not by conditions that might obtain undetectably. For statements whose truth we could never in principle verify, the principle of **bivalence** (every statement is either true or false) may fail. Dummett's view was motivated partly by **intuitionism** in mathematics, which holds that a mathematical statement is true only if it can be proved.

Scientific realism and anti-realism are discussed in [Chapter 10](10-science-and-evidence.md#scientific-realism-and-anti-realism).

## Relativism

**Relativism** is the view that truth, knowledge, justification, or values are relative to some framework (a culture, a person, a language, a conceptual scheme, a historical period), so that there is no framework-independent standard.

### Protagoras and the self-refutation argument

The earliest known relativist was **Protagoras** (see [Chapter 2](02-history-of-epistemology.md#the-presocratics-and-the-sophists)): "Man is the measure of all things." In the *Theaetetus* (171a–c), Plato has Socrates develop a famous objection, the **peritrope** ("turning around"):

1. Protagoras says that whatever seems true to anyone is true for them.
2. Most people believe that Protagoras's doctrine is false.
3. So, by Protagoras's own doctrine, his doctrine is false for them, and since they are the majority, it is false for more people than it is true for.
4. Worse: Protagoras must admit that his opponents' belief (that his doctrine is false) is true. So he must grant that his own doctrine is false.

In modern form, the **self-refutation argument** runs: the claim "All truth is relative" is either absolutely true or only relatively true.
- If it is absolutely true, then at least one truth is not relative, and the claim is false.
- If it is only relatively true (true for relativists), then it is compatible with the claim being false for everyone else, and the relativist has given the non-relativist no reason to accept it.

Sophisticated relativists have replies. They can restrict their relativism to particular domains (morality, taste, aesthetics), avoiding global self-refutation. Or they can argue that the self-refutation argument begs the question by assuming a non-relative concept of truth.

### Varieties of relativism

It helps to separate different claims that are all called "relativism":

| Variety | Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive cultural relativism** | Different cultures have different beliefs and values. | True, and an empirical fact of great importance. But it says nothing about whether any of those beliefs are true. |
| **Relativism about taste** | "Cilantro is delicious" is true relative to a standard of taste. | Plausible. There may be no fact about deliciousness independent of tasters. This is the strongest case for relativism. |
| **Moral relativism** | Moral claims are true or false only relative to a culture or individual. | Hotly debated. Faces the problem that it seems to make moral reformers (abolitionists, suffragists) automatically wrong by the standards of their own societies, and that it makes cross-cultural moral criticism impossible. |
| **Epistemic relativism** | What counts as good evidence or justification is relative to a framework, with no framework-neutral way to compare frameworks. | Serious and much discussed (see below). |
| **Alethic (truth) relativism** | Truth itself is relative. "The Earth is round" is true for some people and false for others. | Faces the self-refutation argument and conflicts with the T-schema. Very few philosophers defend global truth relativism. |
| **Conceptual relativism** | Different languages or conceptual schemes carve up the world differently. | Contains insight but is limited: Donald Davidson ("On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme," 1974) argued that a truly untranslatable conceptual scheme could not even be recognized as a scheme. |

### Boghossian's critique of epistemic relativism

Paul Boghossian (*Fear of Knowledge: Against Relativism and Constructivism*, 2006) gave an influential analysis of epistemic relativism. His example: in the early 17th century, Cardinal Bellarmine refused to accept Galileo's telescopic observations as evidence about the heavens, holding that Scripture was a better source of knowledge about the celestial realm. Was Bellarmine's epistemic system simply different from Galileo's, with no fact about which was better?

The epistemic relativist says that claims like "Observation justifies beliefs about the heavens" are not true or false absolutely, but only relative to an epistemic system. So we should say: "According to Galileo's system, observation justifies...; according to Bellarmine's system, Scripture justifies..."

Boghossian argues that this position is unstable. If no epistemic system is more correct than another, then the relativist cannot say that relativism itself is *better supported* than absolutism. Moreover, when we try to state what an epistemic system is, we find it consists of general principles ("If a telescope shows p, you are justified in believing p"), and these seem to be either true or false. If they are false in general, then the relativist's claim that each person's system is "right for them" collapses; if some are true, relativism is false.

The relativist's strongest argument is the **no neutral justification** argument: to show that one epistemic system is better than another, you must use some epistemic system, which begs the question against the other. Boghossian replies that this form of circularity afflicts all fundamental justification (compare the [problem of the criterion](06-justification.md#the-problem-of-the-criterion)), and that it doesn't follow that no system is objectively better. We can still note that one system has led to far more successful predictions and technologies than another, and that even the defender of the rival system typically relies on the first when it matters (Bellarmine, presumably, used his eyes to cross the street).

### What relativism gets right

Relativism is usually wrong as a *general theory of truth*, but it is motivated by real insights that a critical thinker should keep.

1. **Many claims are implicitly relative, and making the relativity explicit resolves disputes.** "It's cold" is relative to a place and person. "Tea is better than coffee" is relative to taste. "The law requires X" is relative to a jurisdiction. "Motion" is relative to a frame of reference (as physics teaches), but that doesn't make physics relativist: the laws relating the frames are absolute.
2. **Cultural humility.** What seems obvious in one culture may be parochial. Before condemning an unfamiliar practice, understand it, including the reasons its participants give for it.
3. **Background assumptions shape judgment.** People from different backgrounds may weigh the same evidence differently because of different prior beliefs. That is a fact about *justification*, not about truth. Two people with different evidence can both be justified in holding incompatible beliefs; they cannot both be right.
4. **Power shapes what is accepted as knowledge.** Who gets believed, whose testimony is taken seriously, and which questions get investigated are affected by social power. See [Epistemic injustice](12-social-epistemology.md#epistemic-injustice). Again, this is a fact about the social production and recognition of knowledge, not a proof that truth is relative.

> **Diagnosing "That's true for you."** When someone says this, ask which of the following they mean:
> - *"That's your opinion, and you're entitled to it."* (A claim about rights of expression, not about truth.)
> - *"You have your reasons, and I have mine."* (A claim about justification. Both of you might be justified by your own evidence; at most one of you is right.)
> - *"This is a matter of taste or perspective."* (Possibly correct, for questions of taste.)
> - *"There is no fact of the matter."* (A strong claim that needs argument, and it is implausible for factual questions such as "Did the vaccine trial show a reduction in hospitalizations?")
> - *"I don't want to argue about it."* (Often the real meaning, and a reasonable one sometimes. But say so directly.)

## Social constructionism

**Social constructionism** holds that some things we take to be natural or inevitable are in fact products of human social practices, and could have been otherwise.

Ian Hacking (*The Social Construction of What?*, 1999) brought clarity to a heated debate by asking: *what* is said to be socially constructed? He distinguished:

- **Ideas and classifications**: our *concept* of "child abuse," "refugee," or "attention deficit disorder" was shaped by historical and social forces. This is nearly always true.
- **Objects**: the things classified. Some objects are socially constructed in a strong sense: money, marriage, borders, corporations, and laws exist only because people collectively treat them as existing. The philosopher John Searle (*The Construction of Social Reality*, 1995) called these **institutional facts**, created by collective acceptance of rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C" (this piece of paper counts as a 20-euro note in the eurozone).
- **Facts and knowledge themselves**: the strong constructionist claim that scientific facts (quarks, DNA) are constructed by social negotiation rather than discovered. This is much more controversial.

Hacking also observed that classifications of **people** can have **looping effects**: once people are classified (as having a certain disorder, identity, or status), they may change their behavior in response to the classification, which changes the kind itself. Classifications of quarks don't affect quarks. Classifications of people affect people.

Social constructionist claims are often made to argue that something is **not inevitable** and can be changed. Hacking noted that constructionist arguments typically have the form: (1) X need not have existed, or need not be as it is; (2) X is bad as it is; (3) we would be better off if X were done away with or radically transformed. Critical thinking requires keeping these steps separate. That a classification has a social history does not show it is arbitrary or false; that money is socially constructed does not mean your debts aren't real.

**The science wars and the Sokal hoax.** In the 1990s, disputes over strong constructionism about science became heated. In 1996, the physicist Alan Sokal submitted a deliberately nonsensical article, "Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity," to the cultural studies journal *Social Text*, which published it. He then revealed the hoax, arguing that the journal had published it because it flattered the editors' ideological preconceptions. The episode is often cited in debates about standards in academic publishing (and Sokal's own point was about sloppy reasoning, not about the legitimacy of studying science socially).

## Perspectivism and objectivity

What is **objectivity**? The word is used in several senses:

1. **Ontological**: something is objective if it exists independently of what anyone thinks. The mass of the Earth is objective in this sense; the value of a currency is not.
2. **Epistemic**: a judgment is objective if it is made without bias, based on evidence, in a way others could check.
3. **Procedural**: a procedure is objective if its results don't depend on who carries it out (a standardized test, a double-blind trial).

Thomas Nagel (*The View from Nowhere*, 1986) described objectivity as the attempt to transcend one's particular perspective, to form a conception of the world that includes oneself as one element among many, "a view from nowhere." He argued that this is valuable but can never be complete, since subjective points of view are also part of reality.

The historians Lorraine Daston and Peter Galison (*Objectivity*, 2007) showed that the ideal of objectivity itself has a history. In scientific atlases of the 18th and 19th centuries, they trace a shift from "truth-to-nature" (drawing the idealized, typical specimen), to "mechanical objectivity" (letting photographs and machines record without human interference), to "trained judgment" (relying on experts' educated interpretation). Each ideal addressed a particular danger.

**Standpoint theory** and **feminist epistemology** argue that objectivity is not achieved by pretending to have no perspective. Sandra Harding ("Rethinking Standpoint Epistemology: What Is 'Strong Objectivity'?," 1993) argued for **strong objectivity**: examining the social values and interests that shape research, especially from the perspective of marginalized groups whose situations may reveal things invisible from dominant positions. Donna Haraway ("Situated Knowledges," 1988) criticized the "god trick" of claiming to see everything from nowhere, and argued for "situated knowledges" that are accountable for their perspective. See [Standpoint and feminist epistemology](12-social-epistemology.md#standpoint-and-feminist-epistemology).

These views can be read as extensions, not denials, of objectivity. Recall Nietzsche's version of perspectivism (see [Nietzsche](02-history-of-epistemology.md#nietzsche)): "the *more* eyes, different eyes, we can use to observe one thing, the more complete will our 'concept' of this thing, our 'objectivity,' be." Recall also the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each perspective captures part of the truth, and combining perspectives gets closer to the whole. **Perspectivism in this sense is not relativism.** It presupposes that there is one elephant.

## Post-truth and bullshit

In 2016, *Oxford Dictionaries* chose **"post-truth"** as its word of the year, defining it as "relating to or denoting circumstances in which objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief."

### Frankfurt on bullshit

The philosopher Harry Frankfurt ("On Bullshit," 1986; published as a short book in 2005) drew a precise and useful distinction between lying and **bullshit**.

- The **liar** cares about the truth, in a sense. To lie, you must know (or believe) what is true, and deliberately say the opposite. The liar is "responding to the truth," trying to lead you away from it.
- The **bullshitter** is **indifferent** to truth. What they say may be true or false; they don't care, because their aim is something else: to impress, to persuade, to fill airtime, to create an impression of themselves. "It is just this lack of connection to a concern with truth—this indifference to how things really are—that I regard as of the essence of bullshit."

Frankfurt concluded that "bullshit is a greater enemy of the truth than lies are," because the liar still plays the game of truth (and can be caught out in it), while the bullshitter abandons it altogether. When bullshit becomes widespread, people lose the habit of asking whether claims are true at all.

G. A. Cohen ("Deeper into Bullshit," 2002) identified a second kind, common in academia: **unclarifiable unclarity**, text that is obscure not because its ideas are deep but because it has no clear content. His test: if adding or removing a "not" makes no difference to how plausible a sentence seems, the sentence may be bullshit.

### Brandolini's law

The Italian programmer Alberto Brandolini proposed (2013) what is now called **Brandolini's law**, or the bullshit asymmetry principle: "The amount of energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger than to produce it." This asymmetry is structural: a false claim takes seconds to make and share; checking and refuting it takes research. It explains why misinformation spreads and why the [burden of proof](16-critical-thinking-toolkit.md#burden-of-proof) matters so much.

### Is there anything new in post-truth?

Lee McIntyre (*Post-Truth*, 2018) argues that post-truth is not simply lying, which is as old as politics, but the **subordination of truth to political allegiance**: the idea that one's side can choose its own facts, and that a factual claim should be accepted or rejected depending on whether it helps or hurts one's team. He traces its roots to cognitive biases, the decline of traditional media gatekeepers, the rise of social media, and a vulgarized form of postmodern skepticism about truth.

Others (for example, Hugo Mercier, *Not Born Yesterday*, 2020) argue that people are less gullible than the post-truth narrative suggests, and that much apparent acceptance of falsehoods is **expressive** (signaling group loyalty) rather than sincere belief. See [Identity-protective cognition](14-psychology-of-reasoning.md#identity-protective-cognition).

> **What to do.** (1) Separate **truth** (the property) from **certainty** (a state of mind) and **consensus** (a social fact). Uncertainty and disagreement don't show there is no truth. (2) Ask of any claim: **Is the speaker trying to get it right?** If not, what are they trying to do? (3) Don't let the difficulty of refuting nonsense (Brandolini's law) push you into cynicism. Rely on sources with good track records and on institutions whose job is checking (see [Epistemic institutions](12-social-epistemology.md#epistemic-institutions)).

## Check your understanding

**1.** State the T-schema and explain how it bears on the claim that "truth is relative."

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"p" is true if and only if p. For example, "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level" is true if and only if water boils at 100 °C at sea level. So the truth of the sentence depends on how things are with water, not on who believes it. If truth were relative to believers, the T-schema would fail, since the sentence could be "true for" someone while water did not boil at that temperature.
</details>

**2.** What is the main objection to the coherence theory of truth?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

There can be many internally coherent but mutually incompatible systems (a consistent novel, a consistent set of false beliefs), so coherence alone can't be what truth is. The theory seems to confuse a test of justification with the nature of truth.
</details>

**3.** How does deflationism explain the usefulness of the word "true," if truth has no substantial nature?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"True" is a logical device for generalization and endorsement without repetition: "Everything she said is true," "The theorem is true," "Always tell the truth." These can't easily be expressed without a truth predicate, since we can't list all the propositions involved.
</details>

**4.** Reconstruct the self-refutation argument against global truth relativism.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"All truth is relative" is either absolutely true or relatively true. If absolutely true, there is at least one absolute truth, so the claim is false. If only relatively true (true for relativists), it may be false for non-relativists, and the relativist has no non-relative ground to ask them to accept it. Either way the global thesis fails.
</details>

**5.** "Different cultures have different moral beliefs, so there is no objective morality." Evaluate.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The premise (descriptive relativism) is true, but the conclusion doesn't follow. Different cultures also had different beliefs about the shape of the Earth and the causes of disease, without showing there is no fact about those things. Disagreement is compatible with one view being right. Also, much apparent moral disagreement reflects different *factual* beliefs (for example, about the afterlife or about the effects of a practice) combined with shared values. Moral objectivity may still be false, but this argument doesn't show it.
</details>

**6.** What is the difference between lying and bullshit, according to Frankfurt?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The liar knows the truth and tries to lead others away from it; the liar is still oriented toward truth. The bullshitter is indifferent to whether what they say is true; they are pursuing some other goal (impressing, persuading, filling space). Frankfurt thinks bullshit is more corrosive because it abandons the practice of caring about truth.
</details>

**7.** Distinguish perspectivism from relativism.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Perspectivism holds that knowledge is always from some perspective and that combining more perspectives gives a more complete (more objective) understanding of a single reality. Relativism holds that truth itself varies with perspective, with no single reality that all perspectives are about. The blind men and the elephant illustrate perspectivism: there is one elephant.
</details>

**8.** Give an example of a socially constructed fact that is nonetheless objective in the sense that your believing otherwise doesn't change it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The fact that you owe your bank 5,000 euros. Money, debts, and banks exist only because of collective human practices (they are institutional facts). But given those practices, the debt is real whether or not you believe in it.
</details>

## Further reading

**Theories of truth**
- Simon Blackburn, *Truth: A Guide* (Oxford University Press, 2005). Engaging introduction for general readers.
- Michael Lynch, *True to Life: Why Truth Matters* (MIT Press, 2004).
- Michael Lynch (ed.), *The Nature of Truth: Classic and Contemporary Perspectives* (MIT Press, 2001; 2nd ed. 2021). Anthology.
- Paul Horwich, *Truth* (Oxford University Press, 2nd ed. 1998).
- Marian David, "The Correspondence Theory of Truth"; Daniel Stoljar and Nic Damnjanovic, "The Deflationary Theory of Truth," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.

**Relativism and constructionism**
- Paul Boghossian, *Fear of Knowledge: Against Relativism and Constructivism* (Oxford University Press, 2006). Short and sharp.
- Ian Hacking, *The Social Construction of What?* (Harvard University Press, 1999).
- Maria Baghramian and Annalisa Coliva, *Relativism* (Routledge, 2019).
- Bernard Williams, *Truth and Truthfulness: An Essay in Genealogy* (Princeton University Press, 2002).

**Paradox**
- R. M. Sainsbury, *Paradoxes* (Cambridge University Press, 3rd ed. 2009), chs. 6–7.

**Post-truth**
- Harry Frankfurt, *On Bullshit* (Princeton University Press, 2005) and *On Truth* (Knopf, 2006). Both very short.
- Lee McIntyre, *Post-Truth* (MIT Press, 2018).
- Hugo Mercier, *Not Born Yesterday: The Science of Who We Trust and What We Believe* (Princeton University Press, 2020).
