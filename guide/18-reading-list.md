## How to read philosophy

Philosophy is read differently from fiction or news. A few habits make an enormous difference:

1. **Read slowly, and more than once.** A first pass for the overall shape; a second pass for the argument.
2. **Find the thesis.** What is the author trying to establish, and against whom?
3. **Reconstruct the argument** in standard form (see [Reconstructing real arguments](03-logic-and-arguments.md#reconstructing-real-arguments)). Number the premises. Supply what's missing.
4. **Summarize each section in a sentence or two**, in your own words. If you can't, you haven't understood it yet.
5. **Look for the key distinctions and examples.** Philosophers' examples (the Gettier cases, the brain in a vat, the shipowner) are where arguments are tested.
6. **Evaluate only after understanding.** Apply the principle of charity. Ask what the strongest objection is, and how the author might reply.
7. **Use secondary sources**, especially the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, to see how others read the text, but read the primary text first.
8. **Write.** A short written response (one page: summary, one objection, one reply) teaches more than several hours of rereading.

The philosopher Jim Pryor's online "Guidelines on Reading Philosophy" and "Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper" are excellent, brief, and free.

## Level 1: Start here

Accessible books that require no background. If you read only five books, choose from this list.

**Epistemology**
- Jennifer Nagel, *Knowledge: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press, 2014). The best short overview of the field.
- Duncan Pritchard, *What Is This Thing Called Knowledge?* (Routledge, 4th ed. 2018). Clear, short chapters, ideal for self-study.
- Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912). Over a century old and still one of the finest introductions. Free online.
- Simon Blackburn, *Truth: A Guide* (Oxford University Press, 2005).

**Logic and argument**
- Anthony Weston, *A Rulebook for Arguments* (Hackett, 5th ed. 2017). Short and practical.
- Walter Sinnott-Armstrong, *Think Again: How to Reason and Argue* (Oxford University Press, 2018).
- Graham Priest, *Logic: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press, 2nd ed. 2017).

**Science and evidence**
- Samir Okasha, *Philosophy of Science: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press, 2nd ed. 2016).
- Carl Sagan, *The Demon-Haunted World* (Random House, 1995).
- Gerd Gigerenzer, *Calculated Risks* / *Reckoning with Risk* (2002).
- David Spiegelhalter, *The Art of Statistics* (Pelican, 2019).

**Mind and reasoning**
- Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset* (Portfolio, 2021).
- Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). Read with the caveats in [Chapter 14](14-psychology-of-reasoning.md#replication-and-the-psychology-of-psychology).
- Steven Pinker, *Rationality* (Viking, 2021).

**Knowledge in society**
- Cailin O'Connor and James Owen Weatherall, *The Misinformation Age* (Yale University Press, 2019).
- Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West, *Calling Bullshit* (Random House, 2020).
- Harry Frankfurt, *On Bullshit* (Princeton University Press, 2005). About 70 small pages.

## Free online resources

**Encyclopedias**
- *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (plato.stanford.edu). Authoritative, regularly updated, free. Start with the entries "Epistemology," "The Analysis of Knowledge," "Skepticism," "Foundationalist Theories of Epistemic Justification," "Social Epistemology," and "Bayesian Epistemology."

**Courses and videos**
- *Wireless Philosophy* (Wi-Phi): short animated videos by professional philosophers, including a series on critical thinking.
- *Crash Course Philosophy*: accessible video introductions.
- Coursera, "Think Again: How to Reason and Argue" (Duke University; Walter Sinnott-Armstrong and Ram Neta).
- Coursera, "Introduction to Philosophy" (University of Edinburgh), including a unit on epistemology by Duncan Pritchard.
- "Calling Bullshit: Data Reasoning in a Digital World" (University of Washington; Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West): lectures and case studies available online.
- The Digital Inquiry Group's *Civic Online Reasoning* curriculum (from the team formerly at the Stanford History Education Group): free lessons on lateral reading and source evaluation.

**Podcasts**
- *Philosophy Bites* (David Edmonds and Nigel Warburton): short interviews with leading philosophers.
- *A History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps* (Peter Adamson).
- *Very Bad Wizards* and *Hi-Phi Nation* for lively discussions of philosophy and psychology.

**Practice**
- *Good Judgment Open* (gjopen.com): a public forecasting platform where you can make probabilistic predictions and receive Brier scores.
- *Clearer Thinking* (clearerthinking.org): free interactive tools, including calibration training.
- *Kialo*: a platform for mapping pro and con arguments on debated questions.
- *Argdown*: a simple text syntax for argument maps.

## A twelve-week study plan

About 4–6 hours a week. Each week: read the guide chapter(s), one primary text or article, do the "Check your understanding" questions *before* opening the answers, and complete the practice task.

| Week | Guide chapters | Primary reading | Practice task |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | [1. What Is Epistemology?](01-what-is-epistemology.md) | Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, chs. 1–2 | Start a "belief journal": write down five beliefs you hold strongly and, for each, why you hold it and what would change your mind. |
| **2** | [2. History](02-history-of-epistemology.md) | Plato, *Meno* (80d–86c and 97a–98b); Descartes, *Meditations* I–II | Write a one-page summary of Descartes' method of doubt and one objection to it. |
| **3** | [3. Logic and Arguments](03-logic-and-arguments.md) | Weston, *A Rulebook for Arguments* (whole) | Put three newspaper opinion pieces into standard form, including hidden premises. Draw an argument map of one. |
| **4** | [4. Language and Concepts](04-language-concepts-and-definitions.md) | Orwell, "Politics and the English Language"; Chalmers, "Verbal Disputes" (sections 1–3) | Find a current public debate that is partly verbal. Apply the method of elimination. |
| **5** | [5. Knowledge](05-the-nature-of-knowledge.md) | Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?"; Zagzebski, "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems" | Build three original Gettier cases. Test each against the safety and sensitivity conditions. |
| **6** | [6. Justification](06-justification.md) and [7. Sources](07-sources-of-knowledge.md) | BonJour, "Can Empirical Knowledge Have a Foundation?" (1978); Coady, *Testimony*, ch. 4 | List ten things you know only by testimony. For each, rate how well you could justify trusting the source. |
| **7** | [8. Skepticism](08-skepticism.md) | Moore, "Proof of an External World"; selections from Wittgenstein, *On Certainty* | Write a dialogue between a skeptic and a contextualist. |
| **8** | [9. Induction, Probability, Bayes](09-induction-probability-bayes.md) | Hume, *Enquiry*, Section IV; Gigerenzer, *Calculated Risks*, chs. 1–5 | Do the calibration exercise. Solve three base-rate problems using natural frequencies. Start a prediction journal. |
| **9** | [10. Science and Evidence](10-science-and-evidence.md) | Popper, *Conjectures and Refutations*, ch. 1; Hill, "The Environment and Disease" | Take a health news story and trace it to the original study. Evaluate it with the study checklist in [Chapter 16](16-critical-thinking-toolkit.md#checklists). |
| **10** | [11. Truth](11-truth-and-relativism.md) and [12. Social Epistemology](12-social-epistemology.md) | Boghossian, *Fear of Knowledge*, chs. 1–2; Nguyen, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles" | Map your own information sources. Which are independent? | **11** | [13. Virtue and the Ethics of Belief](13-virtues-and-ethics-of-belief.md) and [14. Write up your findings, including the cruxes. |

**After twelve weeks**, review your belief journal from week 1. Which beliefs have you revised? Which are you now more (or less) confident in, and why?

## Keeping it up

Mastery comes from **use**, not from reading. Some ways to keep practicing:

- **Keep the prediction journal** going, and check your calibration every few months.
- **Join or start a reading group.** Argument with others is the natural environment of reasoning (see [The argumentative theory of reasoning](14-psychology-of-reasoning.md#the-argumentative-theory-of-reasoning)).
- **Teach someone.** Explaining the Gettier problem or Bayes' theorem to a friend will show you what you don't yet understand.
- **Return to the interactive explorer** that accompanies this guide (the `index.html` file at the root of this repository) for a map-based view of the concepts and self-check questions.
- **Revisit the checklists** in [Chapter 16](16-critical-thinking-toolkit.md#checklists) until they become habits.

> "The important thing is not to stop questioning."
> — Albert Einstein, quoted in *Life* magazine (1955)
