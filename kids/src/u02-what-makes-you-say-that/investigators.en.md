## Claims need reasons

A **claim** says that something is true. "The grapes are sour." "This game is boring." "The bridge is closed." A claim can be right or wrong, and it stands or falls on its **reasons**.

That is why the best short question is **"What makes you say that?"** It is not rude. It does not say "you're wrong". It just asks to see the reason, so that both people can look at it together.

## The fox's reasons

The fox's claim was: *the grapes are sour*. When Little Grape asked what made him say it, he had two reasons: the grapes were high, and he was tired.

Look at what those reasons are about. They are about the fox's position and the fox's energy. They say nothing about grapes. If the grapes had been sweet, they would be just as high and he would be just as tired. A reason that stays the same whether the claim is true or false does not help the claim.

Compare: "I tasted one" or "a bird spat one out". Those would be different if the grapes were sweet. That is what makes them **evidence**: they point toward one answer more than another.

## Sour grapes

The fox did not lie on purpose. He wanted the grapes and could not get them, and "they are sour" made that feel better. Psychologists call the habit of building reasons for what we already want to believe **motivated reasoning**. The reasons look real, but they were picked *after* the answer.

It works the other way too. Someone who fails a test may decide the test was "unfair" before looking at it. Someone who hopes a rumour is true may find every "reason" to share it.

So a second question joins the first. After "What makes you say that?" ask: **"Would I say this if I wanted the opposite?"** If the answer is yes, the reason is not carrying any weight.

## A fair test

Little Grape did something even better than asking. She helped the fox *check*. A ladder let him taste one grape, and the claim could finally be tested. Notice the order: ask for the reason, see that it is weak, then find a test that the claim could fail.

::: think
- List the fox's two reasons. Which sentence would still be true if the grapes were sweet?
- When have you decided something was "boring" or "unfair" before you really tried it?
- Is it always wrong to say "I don't want it anyway"? When might it be true?
:::

::: bigidea
A reason helps a claim only if it would be different when the claim is false. Ask: "What makes you say that?" and "Would I say it if I wanted the opposite?"
:::
