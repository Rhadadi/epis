For grown-ups · Unit 2

What makes you say that?

Explorers page (7–10) · Investigators page (11–14)

Goals

By the end of this unit, children can say:

  • Explorers (7–10): “I can ask What makes you say that?” “I can tell a reason about the thing from a reason about me.” “I know what an excuse is.”
  • Investigators (11–14): “I can explain why a reason must change if the claim is false to count as evidence.” “I can spot sour grapes: reasons chosen to fit what I want.” “I can ask for a reason without attacking the person.”

The idea in adult words

A claim is justified by reasons, and a good reason is one that is sensitive to the truth of the claim: it would be different if the claim were false. The fox’s reasons (the grapes are high, I am tired) hold whether the grapes are sweet or sour, so they carry no weight. This is the root of the distinction between evidence and mere rationalization. The fox also illustrates motivated reasoning, building a case for the conclusion we want (a “directional goal”), a topic of chapter 14; the question “What makes you say that?” and the idea of a test that the claim could fail connect to justification, chapter 6. In Persian the same idea is a proverb: «دستم به انگور نمی‌رسد، می‌گویم ترش است».

The misconception this unit targets

“A reason is a reason.” Children (and adults) accept any sentence that starts with “because”. The unit teaches a first filter: is the reason about the thing, or about me? Investigators add the sensitivity test: would the reason change if the claim were false?

Running the discussion

  1. Read or listen to the story. Stop after the fox says “sour” and ask: “What do you think made him say that?”
  2. Use the “Think about it” questions. Wait at least five seconds. Useful follow-ups: “What makes you say that?” (turn it on yourself!) and “Would you say it if you wanted the opposite?”
  3. Play the sorting game; the explanations matter more than the score.
  4. Close by agreeing on a family or class signal for the question, such as raising one finger.

Timing and materials

  • Explorers: two sessions of 20–25 minutes (story and talk; games and quick check).
  • Investigators: one 45-minute class period.
  • Optional: a bowl of grapes (or any fruit) and a blind taste test.

Easier and harder

  • Easier: ask the question about everyday claims (“this soup is yucky”) before using the story.
  • Harder: ask Investigators to find an advert or headline whose reasons would be the same even if its claim were false.

Answers

  • The fox’s claim: the grapes are sour. His reasons: they are high, and he is tired. Both are about the fox.
  • He could have tasted one, looked at the grapes, or asked someone who had tasted them.
  • “I didn’t want it anyway” is sometimes true, but when it appears right after failing to get something, ask whether it is an excuse.
